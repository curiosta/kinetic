CH 1

Pressure & Boyle's law

The ocean is heavy, and the air you carry gets squeezed.

At sea level you're already under one "atmosphere" of air: about 1.013 bar, which we'll call 1 bar. Water is roughly 850 times denser than air, so it piles on pressure fast. A column of seawater 10 m tall over one square metre holds 10 m³ × 1,025 kg/m³ = 10,250 kg. Its weight (× 9.81 m/s²) spread over that square metre is about 100,500 pascals. That's ≈ 1 bar.

So: every 10 m of seawater adds one more atmosphere. Your gauge shows depth, but your body responds to absolute pressure, which is air plus water.

Boyle's law: squeeze the gas, shrink the gas

For a fixed amount of gas at constant temperature, P₁ × V₁ = P₂ × V₂. Double the pressure and the volume halves. Your body is mostly liquid, and liquids barely compress. The gas spaces are what feel it: lungs, middle ears, sinuses, mask, BCD, the bubbles in your wetsuit.

Worked example · a 6-litre balloon going down
DepthAbsolute pressureBalloon volumeChange from previous row
0 m1 bar6.0 Ln/a
10 m2 bar3.0 L−50% (pressure doubled)
20 m3 bar2.0 L−33%
30 m4 bar1.5 L−25%

Look at the last column. Pressure doubles in the first 10 m, but going from 30 m to 40 m adds only 25% more. The shallows are where gas volumes change fastest. That's why buoyancy is twitchiest near the surface, why your ears need the most attention in the first few metres, and why the last 5 m of an ascent (1.5 → 1 bar, a 50% expansion) deserve the most care.

✋
Feel the numberAt 10 m, every square centimetre of your skin carries about 2 kg-force (2 bar ≈ 2.04 kgf/cm²). Your palm, about 100 cm², has roughly 200 kg-force pressing on it. You don't feel it because it pushes equally from every side through tissue that doesn't compress. Only air pockets notice.

The one rule that's pure physics: never hold your breath

Your regulator gives you air at whatever pressure surrounds you. Fill your lungs with 6 L at 10 m (2 bar), then swim up holding that breath. By the surface (1 bar) that same gas wants to take up 12 L. Lungs can't double in size. Small air sacs tear, and air can be forced into the chest cavity or bloodstream (pulmonary barotrauma, arterial gas embolism). Diving-medicine literature describes these injuries after breath-hold ascents of only a metre or two, so this isn't just a deep-dive risk. The fix is simple: breathe slowly and continuously, all the time, and on an emergency ascent keep exhaling ("ahhh").

You're at 5 m with 4 L of air in your BCD and you rise to the surface without venting. How much does it want to expand to?

5 m = 1.5 bar. 4 L × 1.5 ÷ 1 = 6 L, so 2 L more air, which means about 2 kg more lift (Chapter 2). That runaway lift is why you vent on every ascent.

CH 2

Buoyancy, weighting & trim

Archimedes is your co-pilot: you float by the litre.

Archimedes' principle: anything in water is pushed up by a force equal to the weight of the water it displaces. Displace more water than you weigh and you float. Displace less and you sink. Match exactly and you hover. That's "neutral buoyancy", the skill that separates calm divers from flailing ones.

Every litre you displace in seawater (density ≈ 1.025 kg/L) gives you about 1.025 kg of lift. In fresh water it's 1.000 kg. Your fine controls are all gas volumes: BCD air, wetsuit bubbles and your lungs.

Worked example · lake to sea

A diver plus gear displacing about 100 L gets 100 kg of lift in fresh water and 100 × 1.025 = 102.5 kg in the sea: about 2.5 kg extra. So if you trained in a pool or lake, expect to add roughly 2 to 3 kg of lead for the ocean. A quick rule of thumb is about 2.5% of your total weight with gear. Always confirm with a buoyancy check at the surface.

The shrinking wetsuit

Neoprene is a foam full of tiny gas cells, and Boyle's law applies to them too. Say a full suit gives you about 4 kg of lift at the surface (it varies a lot with thickness and size). If it behaved like pure gas it would give ~2 kg at 10 m and ~1 kg at 30 m. Real neoprene doesn't quite compress that fully, but the trend is real: you get heavier as you go down. You make up for it with small puffs into the BCD on descent, then vent that air as it expands on ascent.

You get lighter as you breathe

Worked example · the weight of air

A 12 L cylinder at 200 bar holds about 12 × 200 = 2,400 L of surface air. Air weighs ~1.2 g per litre, so that's about 2.9 kg of gas. Breathe it down to 50 bar and you've exhaled roughly 2.2 kg. You're now ~2 kg more buoyant than at the start. Your weights must still hold you at a 5 m safety stop with a near-empty tank. That's how a proper weight check is done.

🫁
Feel the numberYour lungs are the finest buoyancy control you own. A deep breath can add 2 L or more of volume, which is ~2 kg of lift. Experienced divers hover over coral by breathing a little deeper or a little shallower, without touching the BCD.

Trim: the physics of looking effortless

Water is about 850× denser than air, so drag dominates everything. Drag grows with your frontal area and with the square of your speed, which means the power you need grows with the cube of speed. Swim twice as fast and you need roughly 8× the power. A diver swimming at a 45° angle pushes a much bigger cross-section through the water than one lying flat. "Trim" (horizontal, streamlined, gear tucked in) cuts drag, and that cuts effort and air use (Chapter 3). Slow and flat wins.

Halfway through a dive you're slowly sinking even though you're kicking. What's the physics-first fix?

Stop kicking and add a short puff to your BCD, because your wetsuit has compressed with depth. Then fine-tune with your breathing. Kicking to stay up wastes air and stirs up sand.

CH 3

Why your tank shrinks at depth

Same lungs, same breaths, but each one costs more at depth.

Your regulator delivers air at the surrounding pressure, or you couldn't inhale. So a lungful at 30 m (4 bar) holds four times as many air molecules as a lungful on the beach, and it drains the cylinder four times as fast.

Divers measure breathing as SAC (surface air consumption): litres per minute at surface pressure. A relaxed recreational diver might be around 15–25 L/min. Cold, stress, currents and poor trim push it up, sometimes a lot. We'll use 20 L/min.

Worked example · how long does a 12 L tank last?

12 L cylinder filled to 200 bar = 2,400 surface litres. We keep 50 bar (600 L) as a reserve, so 1,800 L is usable. Consumption at depth = SAC × absolute pressure.

DepthPressureAir used per minuteTime on 1,800 L
0 m1 bar20 L90 min
10 m2 bar40 L45 min
20 m3 bar60 L30 min
30 m4 bar80 L22.5 min
40 m5 bar100 L18 min

These are simplified: they ignore descent and ascent time, and real gas at 200 bar packs slightly less than the ideal-gas figure. The shape is what matters: the same tank lasts a quarter as long at 30 m as at the surface.

Why keep a reserve? Do the ascent maths

From 30 m, rising at 9 m/min takes ~3 minutes at an average of about 2.5 bar: 20 × 2.5 × 3 ≈ 150 L. A 3-minute safety stop at 5 m (1.5 bar) costs 20 × 1.5 × 3 = 90 L. That's ~240 L, or 20 bar from a 12 L tank, for a calm diver. Now picture sharing air with an out-of-air buddy who's breathing hard: two people, maybe double the SAC. A 50-bar reserve stops looking conservative.

🎈
Feel the numberAir at 30 m is 4× denser, so it's literally heavier to move through your regulator and airways. That's part of why exertion at depth feels harder, and why slow, deep breaths make your gas last longer than quick shallow ones.
Your SAC rises from 20 to 30 L/min in a current. How long does 1,800 L now last at 20 m?

30 × 3 bar = 90 L/min, so 1,800 ÷ 90 = 20 minutes instead of 30. Effort costs gas, and depth multiplies the cost.

CH 4

Nitrogen, decompression & narcosis

Your body is a soda bottle, and you decide how fast to open it.

Air is about 79% nitrogen and 21% oxygen. Each gas behaves as if it were alone, with a partial pressure = fraction × absolute pressure (Dalton's law). Henry's law says the amount of gas that dissolves in a liquid is proportional to its partial pressure. Your blood and tissues are the liquid.

Loading up: tissues with half-times

At 30 m, you're breathing nitrogen at 3.16 bar while your tissues are still at ~0.79. Nitrogen flows in. It doesn't flow in all at once. Decompression models picture the body as a set of "compartments", from fast ones like blood to slow ones like joints and fat, each with a half-time. Common models span half-times from a few minutes to more than ten hours.

Worked example · a 20-minute tissue at 30 m

Gap to fill: 3.16 − 0.79 = 2.37 bar. After one half-time (20 min) the tissue is halfway: 0.79 + 0.5 × 2.37 ≈ 1.98 bar. After two half-times (40 min) it's three-quarters: ≈ 2.57 bar, more than three times its surface level. Bring that tissue straight to 1 bar and it's heavily supersaturated, like a shaken soda.

Coming up: why slow ascents and safety stops work

A modest supersaturation leaves your tissues harmlessly through the lungs. Too much, too fast, and the gas forms bubbles in tissue and blood. That's decompression sickness ("the bends"): joint pain, numbness, fatigue, and in serious cases paralysis. Recreational divers stay within no-decompression limits (NDLs), ascend slowly (agency maximums are 18 m/min, and many computers want ~9–10 m/min), and add a 3-minute safety stop at about 5 m.

Flying after diving

A plane cabin is pressurised to roughly 0.75–0.8 bar, lower than sea level, which makes any leftover nitrogen more likely to bubble. Widely used guidance (originally from DAN workshops, and repeated by Indian dive centres) is to wait at least 12 hours after a single no-stop dive and 18 hours after multiple dives or days of diving, and longer after decompression dives. Andaman divers flying out of Port Blair: plan your last dive day around your flight.

Nitrogen narcosis: the "martini effect"

At high partial pressure, nitrogen acts like a mild anaesthetic: slowed thinking, overconfidence, fixation, giggles or anxiety. Many divers start to notice it around 30 m, which is one reason the Advanced level stops there. It varies between people and from day to day. Two things make it manageable: it gets stronger the deeper you go, and it fades within moments when you go up a few metres. No hangover.

Oxygen: essential, then toxic

Breathing oxygen at a high partial pressure can trigger convulsions without warning, which underwater can mean drowning. Recreational training uses 1.4 bar as the working limit (1.6 as an absolute contingency). That sets a maximum operating depth (MOD) for each gas:

Worked example · maximum depths at ppO₂ 1.4 bar

Air (21%): 1.4 ÷ 0.21 = 6.67 bar, which is ~56 m, far below recreational limits. EAN32: 1.4 ÷ 0.32 = 4.375 bar, which is ~34 m. EAN36: 1.4 ÷ 0.36 = 3.89 bar, which is ~29 m.
Why bother with nitrox, then? Less nitrogen. At 30 m on EAN32 your nitrogen pressure is 0.68 × 4 = 2.72 bar, the same as breathing air at only ~24.4 m (the "equivalent air depth"). That means longer no-stop times and shorter surface intervals. DIVEIndia, for example, requires nitrox certification for its Barren Island trips.

If something feels wrong after a dive

Joint pain, tingling, unusual fatigue or breathlessness after diving needs oxygen and medical help, fast. The PADI destination guide lists a hyperbaric chamber at Port Blair for the Andamans. Know where your nearest chamber is before you dive, and consider dive insurance (for example through DAN).

You planned a 30 m dive on EAN36. Is that OK?

30 m = 4 bar, and 0.36 × 4 = 1.44 bar, just over the 1.4 working limit. Use EAN32 (1.28 bar at 30 m) or plan shallower (MOD ≈ 29 m).

CH 5

Ears, sinuses & equalising

Why ears hurt at 2 m, and why the deep end is easier.

Behind each eardrum is the middle ear, a small pocket of air connected to the back of your throat by the narrow Eustachian tube. As you descend, water pushes the eardrum inward. The air behind it stays at surface pressure until you open the tube and let higher-pressure air in. That's equalising.

Worked example · the force on your eardrum at 2 m

Pressure difference at 2 m: 0.2 bar = 20,000 Pa. An eardrum's working area is well under a square centimetre (say ~0.7 cm² = 0.00007 m²). Force = 20,000 × 0.00007 ≈ 1.4 newtons, about the weight of a 140 g object balanced on a membrane thinner than paper. At 2 m you're barely below the surface, and it already hurts.

Remember the Boyle table: the change from 0 to 2 m (1 → 1.2 bar) squeezes an air space by about 17%. From 30 to 32 m it's only 5%. The shallows are where equalising matters most.

👂
Feel the numberIf the pressure difference builds too far, soft tissue around the tube's opening can press it shut, and then no amount of blowing opens it. Diving-medicine texts commonly put this around 90 mmHg ≈ 0.12 bar, about 1.2 m of seawater. Wait until it hurts and you may already be past the point where equalising works.

How to equalise: early, often, gently

  • Start before you leave the surface, then equalise every half-metre to metre in the first few metres, and keep going all the way down.
  • Valsalva: pinch your nose and blow gently against it. Never hard.
  • Toynbee: pinch your nose and swallow. Frenzel: pinch your nose and push air up with your tongue like a piston. It takes a bit of practice but is very gentle.
  • Wiggle your jaw. Descend feet-first, which helps air rise into the tubes.
  • If it hurts, go up a metre and try again. Never push through pain, because a burst eardrum or inner-ear injury can end your diving.
  • Don't dive congested. Decongestants can wear off mid-dive and trap air on the way up (a "reverse block").

The same physics applies to your sinuses (which equalise on their own through small openings, so congestion blocks them) and your mask: breathe out a little through your nose on descent, or the mask "squeezes" your face.

Why is equalising easier between 20 and 30 m than between 0 and 10 m?

0 → 10 m doubles the pressure (1 → 2 bar, a 100% change). 20 → 30 m goes from 3 → 4 bar, only 33%. Smaller relative change means smaller volume change, so less air to add per metre.

CH 6

Light, colour, sound & heat

Water is a filter, a lens, a speaker and a heat sink.

Why reds vanish first

Water molecules absorb light, and they absorb long wavelengths (red) far more strongly than short ones (blue). Light fades exponentially with distance (the Beer–Lambert law): I = I₀ × e^(−a·d), where a is the absorption coefficient for that colour. Using laboratory values for pure water:

Worked example · the red snapper at 15 m

Sunlight reaching 15 m keeps e^(−0.624 × 15) ≈ 0.009% of its red, essentially none. So the fish has no red light to reflect and looks grey-brown. Then you switch on a torch 1 m away. The light travels 1 m out and 1 m back: e^(−0.624 × 2) ≈ 29% of the red survives. The fish suddenly turns scarlet. That's why underwater photographers use strobes and get close.

The mask is a magnifying glass

Light bends where it passes from water (refractive index ≈ 1.33) through your flat mask into air. Objects appear at about three-quarters of their real distance, so they look about a third bigger. That "2-metre" fish was probably 1.5 m, and the reef you're about to touch is further away than it looks.

Sound: louder, faster, directionless

Sound travels at about 1,500 m/s in seawater versus ~343 m/s in air, roughly 4.4× faster. Your brain locates sounds mostly from the tiny difference in when they reach each ear. Underwater that difference shrinks by the same factor, and sound also travels through your skull bones straight to both inner ears. Result: you hear a boat engine clearly but can't tell where it is. Treat engine noise as "look up, stay close to the line or reef, and surface carefully", ideally with a surface marker buoy.

Heat: why 28 °C water still chills you

Water conducts heat about 25× faster than air (≈0.6 vs ≈0.025 W/m·K) and holds vastly more heat per litre. Your body is at 37 °C, so even the warm 26–30 °C seas of the Andamans and Lakshadweep steadily pull heat out on a long dive or a third dive of the day. A wetsuit works by trapping a thin layer of water that your body warms. Cold also raises your air consumption (Chapter 3).

A diver at 25 m says the blood from a small cut looks green. Is something wrong?

No. Blood's red has been absorbed by 25 m of water, so what's left reflecting is mostly green. Shine a torch on it and it's red again. Pure Beer–Lambert.

Two Indian twists to know

Andaman & Nicobar rules split dive centres into two categories. "Category A" centres follow WRSTC/ISO agency standards (the PADI/SSI/CMAS limits above). "Category B" centres follow National Institute of Water Sports (NIWS) norms instead. For Category B, the A&N guidelines set a 12–60 age band and a 12 m limit for introductory dives. The A&N rules also allow only no-decompression diving, and require an orientation dive if you have fewer than 30 logged dives or haven't dived in 3 months. Ask your centre which category it is.

Equivalence is approximate. Agencies recognise each other's cards for continuing education, but prerequisites differ (for example, CMAS One Star allows 20 m where PADI/SSI Open Water stop at 18 m). The table aligns them by role, not by identical rules.

04 · Governing bodies

Who sets the rules

Recreational scuba has no single world regulator. Standards come from training agencies, coordinated through the WRSTC and written into ISO standards. Countries and states add their own operating rules on top. In India, training follows agency standards, while state and UT tourism departments register and regulate operators. Water temperatureAbout 26–30 °C across these sites in season (PADI guides; Dive Netrani).

You're here if…

You've never dived and want to feel what breathing underwater is like before committing to a course. Expect a briefing, shallow skills practice and one guided dive with a professional beside you, to a maximum of 12 m. Read Ch 1 and Ch 5 first. Equalising is the #1 thing that ends first dives early.

Physics-smart questions to ask

How long will I actually be underwater? (Some centres promise 40–50 min; some "dives" are 15 min.) One-to-one instructor? Max depth? In the Andamans: are you a Category A (agency-standard) or Category B (NIWS-norm) centre?

Getaway idea: Havelock in Dec–Mar (calmest, clearest). Do a try dive on day 1, and if you love it, roll straight into an Open Water course. Ask whether the centre will credit your try dive toward the course.

You're here if…

You want a lifetime licence to dive to 18 m (20 m for CMAS One Star) with a buddy, anywhere in the world. Budget 3–4 days: theory (or eLearning), a pool day and four open-water dives. Must swim 200 m and float for 10 minutes. Read Ch 2 and Ch 3. Buoyancy and gas planning are the core skills.

Physics-smart questions to ask

Group size (2–4 is ideal)? Is there a full pool day before the sea? Which agency (PADI / SSI / CMAS)? Every centre below issues internationally recognised cards.

Sites for new Open Water divers

AquariumHavelock · boat · ~2–15 m

Calm, shallow and fishy. A favourite for training dives.

Margherita's Mischief & K-RockNeil · ~6–19 m

Boulder reef and a volcanic rock on sand, suited to beginner–intermediate divers.

Grand Central StationNetrani · gradual slope to ~20 m

Rabbitfish, parrotfish, batfish and barracuda. Stay within your 18 m.

Umma Gumma reefGrande Island, Goa · to ~12 m

Rocky reef with a wall. Currents can run either way.

KadmatLakshadweep · 20–50 m visibility

Calm lagoon on one side, coral wall on the other. Rays, turtles and reef sharks (Lakshadweep Admin).

Bus StopNeil · ~13–20 m

Gently sloping reef for certified divers. Plan depth carefully near your 18 m limit.

You're here if…

You're certified, have some logged dives, and want deeper (to 30 m), currents, wrecks, night and navigation. Read Ch 4: at 30 m your no-stop time is about 20 minutes, your air lasts a quarter as long as at the surface, and narcosis starts to appear.

Physics-smart upgrade

Add an Enriched Air Nitrox course. It's short, extends no-stop times at 18–30 m, and some Indian trips (Barren Island) require it.

You're here if…

You want to look after other divers (Rescue), or work in diving (Divemaster, the first pro level, min age 18, 60 logged dives to certify). These centres run Instructor Development or Instructor Training programmes, so they have deep teaching benches.

Before you sign up

Rescue needs current first aid/CPR (PADI EFR or SSI React Right) within 24 months. Divemaster internships often take weeks to months on-site. Ask about dive count, staff ratio and what "internship" includes.

National Institute of Water Sports (NIWS)

Caranzalem, Goa
Govt · Ministry of Tourism

Government institute that lists a scuba diving course among its water-sports training. It also sets the national safety norms used by regulators. A route for people heading into dive-operations careers in India.

niws.nic.in

Events worth knowing

PADI Women's Dive Day is held every July worldwide (the 2026 edition was 18 July). July is monsoon on most Indian coasts, so Pondicherry, open all year, is the realistic Indian option. Temple Adventures' Temple Reef Foundation runs beach clean-ups and reef work, a good way to dive with purpose.
