BASE jumping and wingsuit flying: the physics of the risk

No Indian BASE school, permitted site or BASE-specific rule found as of Oct 2026.

Jumping from cliffs, bridges, buildings and antennas, often less than 152 m up, sometimes in a wingsuit. Here's why low height, slow air and nearby rock leave so little margin, with the published death and injury figures. This is far outside the beginner frame: it comes only after extensive skydiving experience and expert training.

CH 1

Low exits: seconds, not minutes

A skydiver leaves a plane thousands of metres up. A BASE jump starts from a cliff, bridge, building or antenna, often less than 152 m (500 ft) above the landing area.

BASE stands for the four kinds of fixed object people jump from: Building, Antenna, Span (a bridge, arch or dome) and Earth (a cliff). The 2013 study of wingsuit deaths notes that these jumps start from "much lower altitudes than skydives, often less than 152.4 m (500 feet) above the landing area".

When you step off, you start at zero speed. Gravity speeds you up, and air drag grows with the square of your speed until it balances your weight. That balance point is terminal velocity, about 193 km/h (120 mph) for a belly-down skydiver in USPA's freefall table. Getting there takes time and height, and a low jump doesn't have much of either.

Worked example · the speed-up phase (model: 90 kg, belly-down, terminal ≈ 193 km/h)

With drag: height = (vt²/g)·ln cosh(gt/vt) and speed = vt·tanh(gt/vt), with vt ≈ 53.6 m/s. Falling 100 m with no canopy takes about 4.8 s; the 267 m (876 ft) New River Gorge Bridge, about 8.5 s. Model values (illustrative).

What that means for risk

  • Every second is height: in the first three seconds you fall about 42 m, so a 100 m object has already used close to half its height.
  • Little time to fix anything: on a skydive, USPA's table gives about 5.7 s just to fall 1,000 ft (305 m) at full speed. Many BASE jumps don't have 305 m in total.
  • Low speed, little control: the 2013 study notes that BASE jumpers "have far less aerodynamic control" than skydivers, because there's no fast airflow at the start (Ch 2).

Safety This is outside the beginner frame

Nothing on this page is a starting point. BASE jumping and wingsuit flying come after extensive skydiving experience and expert training, and even then the published death rates below are among the highest of any sport (Ch 8).

A jumper waits 4 seconds before pulling from a 100 m object. Roughly how much height has gone?

About 72 m, leaving about 28 m for the canopy to open and fly, which this model says isn't enough (Ch 2).

Sources: Mei-Dan et al., Wilderness Environ Med 2013 · USPA SIM 3-1 (freefall table) · NPS: Bridge Day

CH 2

Opening slowly: the pilot chute at low speed

The canopy doesn't pull itself out. A small pilot chute does, and it only pulls as hard as the air rushing past lets it.

The 2013 study defines the pilot chute as "a small round parachute that pulls the main canopy out of its container". Its pull is a drag force, and drag grows with the square of airspeed. Halve the speed and the pull drops to a quarter.

Worked example · pilot-chute pull vs delay (drag ∝ speed², compared with full terminal speed)
Seconds before the pullSpeedPull, vs at terminal speed
1 s35 km/h3%
2 s68 km/h12%
3 s96 km/h25%
6 s154 km/h64%
10 s183 km/h90%

Pull ratio = (speed ÷ 193)². At 96 km/h that's (0.5)² = 0.25. Model values (illustrative).

Why opening takes height

  • Opening isn't instant: the canopy has to come out, unfold and fill with air while you keep falling. The lab shows how much height a few seconds of opening can use.
  • Pilot-chute problems kill: the 2008 analysis of 106 BASE deaths lists deployment failure and pilot-chute malfunction among the contributing factors. The 2013 wingsuit study lists 5 deaths from pilot-chute complications.
  • The wingsuit burble: USPA warns that a wingsuit leaves a burble (turbulent air) above and behind the flyer that can starve the pilot chute of air. The 2013 study describes a burble catching a pilot chute after it was thrown, "delaying or preventing the canopy from being pulled out".

Safety Slow air means a slow, uncertain opening

The physics that makes a low jump short also makes its opening weaker. Equipment and procedures for this are specialist training, not something to work out from a page.

The pull happens at 68 km/h instead of 136 km/h. How does the pilot chute's pull compare?

Half the speed gives a quarter of the pull: (68 ÷ 136)² = 0.25.

Sources: Westman et al., Br J Sports Med 2008 · Mei-Dan et al., Wilderness Environ Med 2013 · USPA SIM 5-8 (wingsuits)

CH 3

Off-heading openings, line twists and object strike

A canopy that opens facing the wrong way flies straight back at the cliff you just left.

A parachute is a wing: once open, it flies forward. On a skydive, a canopy that opens facing the wrong way just means a turn in open sky. Next to a cliff, there may be only a few metres of air behind you. The 2013 study calls this object strike and describes it as "the leading cause of serious injury and death in BASE jumping". In the 2008 analysis of cliff jumps, a "parachute opening towards the object jumped was the most frequent equipment factor".

Worked example · time to the wall after a 180° opening (example canopy speed 10 m/s, still air)
Gap to the wallTime to reach it
5 m0.5 s
10 m1 s
20 m2 s

Time = gap ÷ speed: 10 ÷ 10 = 1 s. The 10 m/s canopy speed is an example, not a measured figure. A wind blowing toward the cliff shortens these times (Ch 4).

What else goes wrong at opening

  • Line twists: Kinetic's interpretation (general parachuting, not from the BASE studies): the suspension lines can twist as the canopy opens, so the jumper can't steer until they're untwisted, while the canopy keeps flying on whatever heading it has.
  • Body position: the 2008 analysis lists freefall instability among contributing factors, and the 2013 study notes that an unstable or uneven position at deployment "can result in uneven canopy inflation, leading to cliff or ground strike".
  • Height left: every metre left under canopy (lab, Part 1) is time to sort out a heading problem. A low jump leaves very little.

Safety Near an object, a heading problem has no buffer

Most of the serious harm in these studies comes from hitting something. Distance from the object and time under an open canopy are the margins, and low jumps have little of both.

A canopy opens facing a cliff 15 m away and flies at 10 m/s. How long before it reaches the rock, if nothing changes?

15 ÷ 10 = 1.5 seconds.

Sources: Mei-Dan et al., Wilderness Environ Med 2013 · Westman et al., Br J Sports Med 2008

CH 4

Wind and rotors near cliffs

A canopy flies through the air, so whatever the air does near a cliff, the jumper goes with it.

The 2008 analysis of BASE deaths lists environmental factors including poor visibility, cold, water and strong or turbulent winds. Two pieces of physics explain why wind matters so much close to an object.

  • Your ground speed is your airspeed plus the wind. A wind blowing toward the cliff carries the canopy toward it, whichever way it faces.
  • Wind force grows with speed squared. A gust of twice the speed pushes four times as hard.
Worked example · a 4 m/s wind toward the cliff (example canopy airspeed 10 m/s)
Canopy facingSpeed over the groundTime to cover a 10 m gap
Away from the cliff10 − 4 = 6 m/s awaymoving away, slowly
Toward the cliff10 + 4 = 14 m/s toward≈ 0.7 s

10 ÷ 14 ≈ 0.7 s. Example speeds, not measured figures.

Rotors: rough air behind an edge

Kinetic's interpretation (general fluid physics, not from the BASE studies): when wind flows over a cliff edge or around a building, it can't follow a sharp corner. The flow separates and leaves swirling, turbulent air on the sheltered side, often called a rotor. Air there can blow in a different direction from the wind above, and it can change suddenly, which matters most under a canopy close to the wall.

Safety Wind near objects is hard to read

The wind at the exit point may not be the wind at the wall or at the landing area. The studies list strong and turbulent wind among the factors in deaths.

A gust rises from 5 m/s to 10 m/s. How much harder does it push on a canopy?

Four times as hard: force grows with speed squared, (10 ÷ 5)² = 4.

Sources: Westman et al., Br J Sports Med 2008 · Mei-Dan et al., Wilderness Environ Med 2013

CH 5

Wingsuits: lift, drag and glide ratio

A wingsuit turns a falling body into a crude wing. It trades downward speed for forward speed, but it still comes down fast.

A wingsuit adds fabric between the arms and body and between the legs. Air pressurises those layers into wing-shaped cells. USPA says the extra surface area gives "less vertical and more horizontal speed". A fluid-mechanics review (2023) reports a recorded vertical speed of about 40 km/h with forward speeds up to 100 km/h, a typical glide ratio of about 2.5, and a cited record glide ratio of 4. Hang gliders reach about 15.

In a steady glide in still air, the glide ratio equals the wing's lift-to-drag ratio: forward distance ÷ height lost = lift ÷ drag. A better wing (more lift for the same drag) glides flatter.

Worked example · what glide ratio means at 100 km/h forward (still air)
Glide ratioSink speedGlide angleHeight lost per km
1.567 km/h33.7°667 m
2.5 (typical, as reported)40 km/h21.8°400 m
4 (record, as cited)25 km/h14.0°250 m

Sink speed = forward speed ÷ glide ratio; angle = arctan(1 ÷ glide ratio). Even the record suit loses 250 m of height for every kilometre.

What it means for risk

  • Still too fast to land: the 2013 study notes that wingsuit flyers keep "such high airspeeds that it is not possible to land safely on hard ground". The parachute is still needed (Ch 6).
  • A slow start: a wingsuit needs airspeed to fly. The 2013 study notes that after a poor exit a jumper "is likely to fall next to the cliff for a few seconds" before gaining enough speed to control the flight.
  • Competition measures the same physics: FAI wingsuit performance events, flown from aircraft, score time (best lift), distance (best glide) and speed (least drag) through a window from 2,500 to 1,500 m.

Safety Wingsuits come after hundreds of skydives

USPA requires at least 200 skydives and a current licence before any wingsuit jump, even from a plane. The 2013 study says wingsuit BASE jumpers "should accumulate numerous (hundreds) wingsuit skydives" first.

A suit glides at 3 : 1 and flies 100 km/h forward. How fast is it sinking?

100 ÷ 3 ≈ 33 km/h downward.

Sources: USPA SIM 5-8 (wingsuits) · Nazemian Alaei & Valipour, J Applied Fluid Mechanics 2023 · Mei-Dan et al., Wilderness Environ Med 2013 · FAI Wingsuit Flying rules 2026 · USPA Basic Safety Requirements

CH 6

Proximity flying and the terrain margin

Flying a wingsuit close to a slope feels like flying low. The geometry says the margin is decided long before you get there.

Proximity flying means flying a wingsuit close to slopes, ridges and trees. The 2013 study says skilled jumpers "can fly within a few meters of rock formations" and that the flight path "is determined far above the feature". It links the rise in wingsuit deaths to this style of flying.

The margin is simple geometry. For every kilometre flown, the suit loses 1,000 ÷ glide ratio metres. A slope of angle θ drops 1,000 × tan θ metres. If the slope drops more slowly than you sink, the gap closes.

Worked example · glide ratio 2.5 (400 m lost per km) over slopes of different angles
SlopeGround drops per kmMargin change per kmA 50 m margin lasts
15°268 m−132 m≈ 380 m of flight
20°364 m−36 m≈ 1.4 km (≈ 50 s)
25°466 m+66 mgrows

Margin change = 1,000 × (tan θ − 1 ÷ glide ratio). Five degrees of slope turn a growing margin into a shrinking one. Still air and a steady glide; real flights aren't.

Pull height and the flare

  • Pull height: USPA tells beginner wingsuit skydivers, and anyone who deploys inconsistently, to deploy at no lower than 5,000 ft (about 1,500 m) above the ground. BASE wingsuit flights end far closer to the terrain, and no published standard covers them.
  • The flare: Kinetic's interpretation: pitching up trades speed for a moment of slower sink. In an ideal no-drag case, 30 m/s (108 km/h) of speed could buy at most v²/2g ≈ 46 m of height. Drag takes much of that, and slowing down weakens the pilot chute's pull (Ch 2).
  • What the deaths show: in the 2013 study, 19 of 39 wingsuit BASE deaths (49%) were cliff strikes and 18 (46%) were ground impacts. It attributes 17 of the 39 deaths to "wingsuit path miscalculation" and says ground impact is typically the result of a "low pull".

Safety The margin closes faster than it feels

A few degrees of slope or a little lost glide decide whether the gap grows or shrinks. By the time it looks close, the path has already been set.

A suit with glide ratio 2.5 flies over a 25° slope. Does the gap to the ground grow or shrink?

It grows, by about 66 m per km: the slope drops 466 m per km and the suit sinks 400 m.

Sources: Mei-Dan et al., Wilderness Environ Med 2013 · USPA SIM 5-8 (wingsuits)

CH 7

One canopy, no reserve, and the landing

A skydiver carries a second parachute. A BASE jumper doesn't, because there's rarely time to use one.

The 2013 study describes "the single canopy used in BASE jumping (no reserve chute)". Skydivers carry a reserve because they open high: USPA's table gives about 5.7 s to fall each 1,000 ft (305 m) at full speed, time to deal with a problem. A low jump never has that height, so if the one canopy fails, there's no second chance.

Worked example · a 150 m object, 3 s before the pull, 3 s to open (model)
PhaseHeight usedHeight left
Freefall, 3 s≈ 42 m≈ 108 m
Opening, 3 s (96 km/h down to 5 m/s)≈ 48 m≈ 60 m
Under canopy at 5 m/s60 m≈ 12 s to land

Opening height ≈ average speed × time = (26.7 + 5) ÷ 2 × 3 ≈ 48 m. A clean opening is assumed. About 12 seconds to steer, avoid the object and land. Model values (illustrative).

Landing hazards

  • Small, rough landing areas: at Kjerag in Norway, most of the 82 nonfatal accidents in 20,850 jumps were ankle sprains or fractures, minor concussions and bruised knees, and a helicopter was used in one-third of all accidents.
  • Serious injuries are mostly legs: in a 2012 survey of 68 jumpers, 61% of severe injuries were to the lower limbs, 20% to the back or spine, 18% to the chest and 13% to the head, and 52% of injured jumpers needed surgery.
  • Rescue: at a cliff site, help can mean a helicopter, as the Kjerag figures show.

Safety There is no backup parachute

Everything rides on one canopy opening cleanly, on heading, with enough height left. That's why this activity sits far outside the beginner frame.

From a 150 m object, the pull comes at 4 s instead of 3 s. Using the lab, what happens to the height left under canopy?

It drops from about 60 m to about 20 m: the extra second uses 30 m more of freefall, and the faster opening uses about 10 m more.

Sources: Mei-Dan et al., Wilderness Environ Med 2013 · Søreide et al., J Trauma 2007 · Mei-Dan et al., Clin J Sport Med 2012 · USPA SIM 3-1 (freefall table)

CH 8

The human side: experience and the numbers

The people in these studies were experienced. The numbers are still stark.

BASE jumping is hard to measure: the 2013 study calls it "a largely unorganized and unregulated activity", so researchers rely on jump logs at single sites, surveys and a fatality list kept by jumpers. Each figure below comes from one study, with its own method and years.

Worked example · what the studies found
StudyFinding (quoted or exact)
Kjerag, Norway, 1995–2005 (20,850 jumps)9 deaths: 1 in every 2,317 jumps. 82 nonfatal accidents: 1 in every 254. "A five- to eightfold increased risk of injury or death compared with that of skydiving."
106 BASE deaths, 1981–2006Annual risk in 2002 "estimated at about one fatality per 60 participants".
Survey of 68 jumpers (2012)Median 286 jumps over 5.8 years; 0.2% of jumps (2 per 1,000) caused a severe injury; 72% had seen a death or serious injury.
Wingsuit BASE deaths, 1981–201139 of 180 BASE deaths (22%) involved wingsuits. Their share rose from 16% (2002–2007) to 49% (2008–2011).

1 in 2,317 is about 0.04% per jump. For someone making 100 jumps a year at that rate, the chance of a fatal jump that year is 1 − (1 − 1/2,317)¹⁰⁰ ≈ 4%. That's Kinetic's arithmetic on one site's rate, an estimate, not a study finding.

Experience, as the sources describe it

  • Skydiving comes first: USPA requires at least 200 skydives and a current licence before any wingsuit jump. Archana Sardana, profiled by The Hindu as India's first civilian BASE jumper, said you need "at least 200 skydives to even qualify for BASE-jumping training" (her claim, not an official rule).
  • Experience doesn't remove the risk: the 2012 survey's jumpers had a median of 286 BASE jumps, and most had seen someone die or be badly hurt.
  • New suits, new risks: the 2013 study suggests some jumpers "act as test pilots of new wingsuit concepts".

Safety Among the deadliest sports studied

The 2013 study calls BASE "one of the most dangerous sporting activities in practice". This page explains the physics behind that. It isn't an invitation or a route in.

Between 2008 and 2011, what share of recorded BASE deaths involved wingsuits?

49% (29 of 59), up from 16% in 2002–2007.

Sources: Søreide et al., J Trauma 2007 · Westman et al., Br J Sports Med 2008 · Mei-Dan et al., Clin J Sport Med 2012 · Mei-Dan et al., Wilderness Environ Med 2013 · USPA Basic Safety Requirements · The Hindu (2019)

04 · Governing bodies

Who sets the rules

BASE jumping has no world governing body or licence. Wingsuit flying from aircraft is a recognised air sport with world rules. India's air-sports policy names BASE, but we found no BASE-specific rules, school or site.
