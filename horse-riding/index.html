CH 1

Your centre of mass over a moving base

Standing on the ground, your base keeps still. On a horse, it walks, trots and turns underneath you.

You stay balanced while your centre of mass sits above your base of support. In the saddle that base is your seat bones, thighs and stirrups, and it moves with every step. The German national federation's handbook (reprinted by the US Dressage Federation) puts the rider's aim like this: follow the horse's movement smoothly, keeping your centre of gravity in harmony with the horse's. For the basic seat it asks for the upper body upright above the seat bones and an imaginary vertical line from shoulder to heel.

When the horse speeds up, slows down or turns, your base accelerates. To stay balanced without gripping, you have to lean so that gravity and the push from the saddle add up along your body. Speeding up at a rate a needs a lean forward of tan φ = a ÷ g; turning at speed v on a circle of radius r needs a lean into the turn of tan φ = v² ÷ (r g), the same physics as a cyclist in a bend.

5 m/s on a 10 m radius: 5² ÷ (10 × 9.81) ≈ 0.255, and tan⁻¹ 0.255 ≈ 14.3°. Halving the radius doubles the lean's tangent; doubling the speed quadruples it. The horse leans too, which is why a rider who stays upright while the horse tips into the turn ends up off-centre.

CH 2

Gaits & footfall patterns

Listen to the hoofbeats: four, two, three, four.

A 2007 study in the Journal of Experimental Biology measured the footfalls of ridden horses with sensors on all four legs. Its footfall table (LH = left hind, LF = left fore, and so on):

  • Walk: four beats. LH, LF, RH, RF at roughly even intervals, with two to four feet on the ground and no moment in the air.
  • Trot: two beats. Diagonal pairs land together, LH with RF, then RH with LF; between them there can be a moment with no feet down.
  • Canter: three beats. On the right lead: LH, then RH and LF together (or nearly), then RF, then a moment in the air. The last foreleg down before that moment (here RF) is the leading leg.
  • Gallop: four beats. The diagonal pair splits: LH, RH, LF, RF on the right lead, then a moment in the air.

In that study (eight Icelandic horses ridden in an arena), walks were measured from 0.6 to 3.2 m/s, trots from 1.8 to 6.7 m/s, canters from 4.0 to 8.9 m/s and gallops from 4.7 to 8.8 m/s. The canter and gallop speeds overlapped: what defines a gait is its footfall pattern, not its speed. Speed is stride length times stride frequency, v = L × f.

Why a horse changes gait: energy

If speed is just stride length × frequency, why switch from trot to canter at all? A classic experiment by Donald Hoyt and Richard Taylor (Nature, 1981) trained small horses to walk, trot and gallop on a treadmill across a wide range of speeds, including speeds they wouldn't normally choose in that gait, and measured their oxygen use. Within each gait, the energy to cover a metre had a minimum at one particular speed and rose on either side. The speeds the horses picked naturally sat close to those minimums, and the switch to the next gait happened where it became the cheaper way to go. A horse pushed to trot very fast is spending more energy per metre than if it cantered.

Worked example · what that means for you in the saddle

The study's exact speeds were for its own small horses on a treadmill, so we don't quote them as targets. Bigger horses, riders' weight and footing all shift the numbers; the shape of the curve, a cheapest speed within each gait, is the lesson.

You hear "da-da-dum … da-da-dum" with a pause after each group. Which gait is it?

Three beats and a moment in the air: the canter.

CH 3

Shock absorption, posture & following the motion

The saddle rises and falls under you. Your joints decide whether you go with it or bounce.

Each time a pair of legs pushes off, the horse's body is lifted and then falls back. In trot each diagonal pair does this, so the back rises and falls twice per stride. Model that motion as a smooth up-and-down wave of amplitude A and frequency f: the saddle's biggest downward acceleration is a = A (2π f)². If that is more than g, the saddle drops away faster than gravity can pull a stiff rider after it, so the rider lifts off and lands again: the bounce.

±5 cm2.815.5 m/s²1.58 g

0.05 × (2π × 2.4)² ≈ 11.4 m/s² ÷ 9.81 ≈ 1.16 g. Doubling the frequency quadruples the acceleration.

Riders deal with it in three ways. First, springs: the handbook's light seat asks for the hip, knee and ankle to work like springs, with a supple ankle letting the heel sink below the toe, and a back that swings with the horse's back, so the rider's centre of mass moves less than the saddle does. Second, rising trot: the rider stands up on one beat and sits on the next, meeting only every other bounce. Third, fewer contact points to brace on: the BHS Stage 1 riding test includes walk and trot without stirrups and trot and canter in a light seat, both of which train balance rather than grip. A collapsed hip or a hollow back, faults the handbook names, makes the rider stiff where they need to give.

Why does a rider who grips hard with the knees bounce more, not less?

Gripping locks the joints that act as springs, so the rider's body has to follow the saddle as one rigid lump: when the saddle drops faster than g, the whole rider lifts off.

CH 4

The physics of jumping: take-off, arc & landing

Once the last hind foot leaves the ground, the arc is fixed.

In the air, the only big outside force on horse and rider is gravity, so the horse's centre of mass follows a parabola set entirely at take-off. Legs can tuck and the neck can stretch, but nothing done in the air changes that arc. Leaving the ground at speed v and angle θ, the centre of mass rises (v sin θ)² ÷ 2g, stays airborne for 2 v sin θ ÷ g (landing at take-off height) and travels v² sin 2θ ÷ g. For the top of the arc to be over the fence, the horse has to take off half that distance before it.

6 m/s at 30°: v sin θ = 3.0 m/s, rise = 3.0² ÷ 19.62 ≈ 0.46 m, time = 6.0 ÷ 9.81 ≈ 0.61 s, flight = 36 × sin 60° ÷ 9.81 ≈ 3.18 m. A steeper take-off buys height but shortens the flight, so the horse must take off closer to the fence. 6 m/s is 360 m/min, inside the 325–375 m/min the EFI sets for its show-jumping grades.

Landing: where the big forces are

The arc is set at take-off, but the forces come at landing, and they go through the forelegs. Meershoek and colleagues (Equine Veterinary Journal, 2001) put force plates under the landing side of a 1 m vertical fence and filmed six experienced jumping horses (599 kg on average), each ridden by its usual rider (74 kg on average). The first foreleg to land is the trailing leg, then the leading leg. Each reached a peak vertical force of about 1.5 times the horse's body weight, and the trailing leg's flexor tendons were loaded harder.

599 kg × 9.81 × 1.5 ≈ 8,800 N, the weight of about 900 kg resting on one leg for a fraction of a second. The study found the peak coffin- and fetlock-joint moments were 82% and 45% higher than published values for trot. That's one reason coaches limit the number of jumps in a session and care about the footing on the landing side.

The rider is a projectile too. The handbook's forward (jumping) seat (upper body in front of the vertical, elastic hip and knee joints, deep heel and knee, quiet hands) keeps the rider's centre of mass over the horse's as the horse's body rotates up and then down over the fence. If your centre of mass gets behind or ahead of the horse's, your two parabolas no longer match, and the difference has to be made up through your seat, legs and hands, usually with a jolt. Fence heights climb steadily: the BHS Stage 2 riding test asks for a course of fences up to 75 cm, the EFI's Preliminary show-jumping grade is 0.90–1.05 m, and its Grand Prix 1.35–1.50 m.

A horse takes off at 6 m/s and 30° and its centre of mass starts 1.25 m up. How high does the centre of mass get?

1.25 + 0.46 ≈ 1.71 m. If the tucked legs hang 0.6 m below it, the horse clears a 1.0 m rail by about 11 cm, and hits a 1.2 m one.

CH 5

The rider–horse system: aids, communication & horse senses

You can't out-muscle half a tonne. Riding is signalling, not steering by force.

Momentum is mass times velocity, p = m v. A 500 kg horse carrying a 70 kg rider (illustrative masses) has about 850 kg·m/s of momentum at 1.5 m/s and about 3,400 kg·m/s at 6 m/s. Stopping from that canter in 3 s takes an average force of 3,420 ÷ 3 ≈ 1,140 N, roughly the weight of a 116 kg mass, and it is the horse's legs that supply it. The rider's job is to ask.

Riders ask with aids: signals through the seat, the legs and the hands on the reins. The handbook calls a correct seat the basic prerequisite for applying all of them, because a rider who is off balance sends signals by accident. Equitation science explains why light aids work. The International Society for Equitation Science's training principles say pressure from the bit, leg, spur or whip must be removed at the onset of the correct response, and that light signals should come before a pressure-release sequence so the horse learns to answer the light signal. The release, not the pull, is what teaches.

Your horse stops when you squeeze the reins. When should the squeeze end?

As soon as the horse starts to respond. Releasing the pressure at that moment is what tells the horse it was the right answer.

CH 6

Falls & safety: helmets and body protectors

In the saddle, your head is higher than most doorways. Falling from there is a real impact.

Sitting on a horse about 1.6 m tall at the withers, a rider's head is roughly 2.4 m above the ground (illustrative). Dropping from that height, the head reaches v = √(2 g h) ≈ 6.9 m/s, about 25 km/h, straight down, before adding any of the horse's forward speed: in a 6 m/s canter the total is √(6.9² + 6²) ≈ 9.1 m/s.

Worked example · stopping a head moving at 6.9 m/s (average deceleration, illustrative)
Stopping distanceAverage decelerationIn g
0.5 cm (hard ground, no helmet)4,761 m/s²485 g
1 cm2,381 m/s²243 g
2 cm1,190 m/s²121 g
3 cm (a crushable helmet liner)794 m/s²81 g

a = v² ÷ (2d): 6.9² ÷ (2 × 0.03) ≈ 794 m/s². Six times the stopping distance means one-sixth of the average force. Real impacts are messier (peaks, rotation, angles), which is why helmets are tested to standards rather than designed on a napkin.

Helmets. The EFI's Technical Guidelines make a properly fastened helmet with a three-point retention harness compulsory for anyone jumping a horse, and protective headgear that meets international testing standards mandatory while riding on the show grounds. British Eventing lists the helmet standards it accepts (such as PAS 015 and VG1), recommends replacing a helmet every 3–5 years, and says any helmet involved in a fall where the rider's head hits the ground should be replaced at once, because internal damage isn't always visible.

Compare our 6.9 m/s head drop above: energy goes with speed squared, so (6.9 ÷ 5.9)² ≈ 1.37, about 37% more energy than the standard flat-anvil test, before any forward speed. Source table: Virginia Tech Helmet Lab, "Equestrian Standards Helmet Impact Criteria" (as of Dec 2022).

Body protectors are designed to absorb the energy of a fall, a kick or being stepped on, protecting the chest, ribs and abdomen. British Eventing makes them mandatory for cross-country and requires the BETA 2018 Level 3 or EN 13158:2018 Level 3 standard, the highest of three levels; a good fit covers the chest, ribs and spine and finishes above the saddle when you sit. Stay off unfamiliar horses, ride with a coach until you're independent, and listen when the coach says a horse, a gait or a fence is too much for today.

Your head hits the ground in a fall but your helmet looks fine. What should you do with it?

Replace it. British Eventing advises replacing any helmet after a fall where the head hits the ground, because the damage inside may not show.

04 · Governing bodies

Who sets the rules

In India the EFI runs national competitions and is the country's member of the FEI, the world governing body. Riding schools set their own teaching syllabus; several follow the British Horse Society's system. We checked each on an official source.

Global

World governing body

FEI · Fédération Équestre Internationale

Governs international Jumping, Dressage and Para Dressage, Eventing, Driving and Para Driving, Endurance and Vaulting; its members are national federations such as the EFI. It describes its mission as celebrating the bond between horse and human, with athlete welfare and ethical partnership with the horse.

inside.fei.org
Rider-education framework

FEI Solidarity · Rider's Education Programme

A curriculum from beginner to early competition that national federations can apply to adopt, with two national certificates: a Rider's Diploma and a Rider's Licence. Its 2025 brochure planned the full launch for January 2026.

inside.fei.org
Riding qualifications

BHS · The British Horse Society

A UK charity whose qualifications are known as Stages: Stage 1 (basic horse care and riding) through Stages 2–4 to BHSI and Fellowship, assessed at BHS Approved Centres.

bhs.org.uk
Safety standards

British Eventing · helmet & body-protector standards

Requires a body protector certified to BETA 2018 Level 3 or EN 13158:2018 Level 3 for cross-country, lists the helmet standards it accepts, and explains how to check fit and condition.

britisheventing.com

India

Constituted in 1967; runs national Dressage, Show Jumping, Eventing, Tent Pegging and Endurance, issues EFI rider IDs, and is India's member of the FEI.
