Kinetic · Adventure science · Skydiving

Exit. Arch. Open. Let drag and lift bring you home.

Skydiving is a balance of forces: gravity against drag in free fall, then lift and drag under a canopy that flies like a wing. Take the challenge, learn the science with a Freefall Lab, then find dropzones in India and abroad, from a first tandem to a licence.

CH 1

Free fall & terminal velocity

You speed up for about ten seconds. Then the air holds you at a steady speed.

Leaving the aircraft, gravity accelerates you at 9.81 m/s². As you speed up, air drag ½ ρ CdA v² grows until it equals your weight. Then you stop accelerating: that's terminal velocity, vt = √(2 m g ÷ (ρ CdA)). CdA is your drag area: spread out belly-down it's big, head-down it's small.

Worked example · 90 kg with gear, ρ 1.1 kg/m³ (from our Freefall Lab, illustrative)
PositionCdATerminal velocity95% reached after
Belly-to-earth0.56 m²≈ 193 km/h (≈ 120 mph)≈ 10 s, 341 m
Head-down0.31 m²≈ 257 km/h (≈ 160 mph)≈ 13 s, 606 m

vt = √(2 × 90 × 9.81 ÷ (1.1 × 0.56)) ≈ 53.6 m/s. Real terminal velocity changes with air density, so jumpers fall a little faster high up.

Thinner air, faster fall

Because vt ∝ 1 ÷ √ρ, a drop in density raises terminal speed. At about 10,000 ft the standard atmosphere is only ~69% of sea-level pressure (Ch 5); density is lower by a similar fraction, so the same belly position runs a little faster than our sea-level-ish lab value until you get lower.

Order of magnitude · same 90 kg belly jumper if density were 0.9× the lab value
Relative density ρ / ρlabTerminal velocityCompared with lab 193 km/h
1.00 (lab)≈ 193 km/hbaseline
0.90≈ 203 km/h≈ +5%
0.75 (roughly high-exit air)≈ 223 km/h≈ +15%

√(1/0.90) ≈ 1.054; √(1/0.75) ≈ 1.155. USPA's freefall-time table is still the practical planning tool; density is one reason real times vary.

How long is the freefall?

USPA's Skydiver's Information Manual gives freefall times at constant speed: at 120 mph, 10,000 ft takes about 57 s and 5,000 ft about 28 s; at 160 mph, 10,000 ft takes about 43 s. It notes that many factors affect terminal velocity. Our lab includes the speed-up after exit, so it gives a little longer: about 32 s from 10,000 ft to an opening at 5,000 ft.

CH 2

Body position & fall rate

Your body is the control surface. Arch, and the air does the rest.

A stable belly-to-earth arch (hips forward, arms and legs out) puts your centre of mass below your centre of drag, like a shuttlecock, so you settle face-down. Moving arms and legs changes how the air pushes on you: you can turn, move forward and change fall rate. Because vt ∝ √m, heavier jumpers fall faster in the same position, so groups match fall rates with jumpsuits and body position.

Worked example · same belly position (CdA 0.56 m², illustrative)
Mass with gearBelly terminal velocityHead-down terminal velocity
70 kg≈ 170 km/h≈ 227 km/h
90 kg≈ 193 km/h≈ 257 km/h
110 kg≈ 213 km/h≈ 285 km/h

193 × √(110 ÷ 90) ≈ 213 km/h. To match a 70 kg friend, the 110 kg jumper needs about 1.57 times the drag area, or the light jumper less.

Tracking: falling forward on purpose

After a formation breaks off, jumpers track: arms swept back, legs extended, body slightly de-arched so the air pushes you forward as well as up. You trade some vertical drag for horizontal speed, opening horizontal separation before deployment. The physics is the same drag equation with a tilted body: the relative wind is no longer straight up your chest. USPA's A-licence practical exam asks for a track of at least 100 ft after breakoff — distance that comes from that horizontal component of drag.

A jumper's mass doubles. What happens to terminal velocity in the same position?

It rises by √2, about 41%.

CH 3

Deployment & opening forces

From 193 km/h to a gentle glide in a few seconds.

At opening altitude you throw a small pilot chute, which pulls out the bag holding the main canopy; the lines stretch, then the canopy inflates. A fabric slider on the lines slows the inflation so the opening is a few seconds long rather than a snap. Spreading the deceleration over time lowers the force: F = m (Δv ÷ Δt + g).

Worked example · 90 kg, from 53.6 m/s to 5 m/s (illustrative)
Opening timeAverage decelerationAverage harness load
1 s (too fast)≈ 5.0 g≈ 6.0 g (≈ 5.3 kN)
3 s≈ 1.65 g≈ 2.65 g (≈ 2.3 kN)
5 s≈ 1.0 g≈ 2.0 g (≈ 1.8 kN)

(53.6 − 5) ÷ 3 ≈ 16.2 m/s² ≈ 1.65 g; plus 1 g to hold you up against gravity. Real openings peak above the average.

Where the kinetic energy goes

At 53.6 m/s a 90 kg jumper carries about ½ m v² ≈ 129 kJ — roughly the energy of dropping that mass 146 m. The opening does not destroy that energy instantly; the slider and the stretching canopy dump it into heating and moving air over a few seconds. Halving the opening time roughly doubles the average force (same Δv, half Δt), which is why a snivelling, staged inflation is kinder than a hard snatch.

Two canopies and a backup

Sport rigs carry a main and a reserve canopy. An automatic activation device (AAD) fires the reserve if you're still falling fast at a set low altitude. Skyhigh India, for example, describes its tandem system as having a main, a reserve and an AAD.

If the opening took 2 s instead of 3 s, what's the average deceleration?

(53.6 − 5) ÷ 2 ≈ 24.3 m/s² ≈ 2.5 g (3.5 g harness load).

CH 4

Canopy flight: glide & flare

A modern canopy is a wing. You land it like a plane: into the wind, then flare.

A ram-air canopy flies forward and down: its glide ratio is forward speed ÷ sink rate. Steering toggles pull down the trailing edge on one side to turn. Just before landing you flare: pulling both toggles down swaps forward speed for a moment of extra lift, slowing both your descent and your forward speed so you touch down gently. Landing into the wind cuts your groundspeed.

Worked example · canopy at 12 m/s forward, 5 m/s down (illustrative student canopy)
ItemNumberHow
Glide ratio (still air)2.4 : 112 ÷ 5
Airspeed≈ 43 km/h12 × 3.6
Groundspeed landing into 15 km/h≈ 28 km/h43 − 15
Groundspeed landing downwind≈ 58 km/h43 + 15
Wing loading, 90 kg on 21 m² (≈ 226 ft²)≈ 4.3 kg/m²heavier loading = faster canopy

Kinetic energy rises with speed squared: landing downwind at 58 km/h carries (58 ÷ 28)² ≈ 4.3 times the energy of landing into wind.

What the flare is doing

In steady glide, lift ≈ weight and you sink at a few metres per second. A flare raises the wing's angle of attack: lift briefly exceeds weight, sink rate falls toward zero, and forward speed bleeds off as induced drag rises. Start too high and you float then sink hard; start too low and you still have forward speed at touchdown. Heavier wing loading (same canopy, more mass — or a smaller canopy) raises trim speed the same way a PPG wing does (paramotoring Ch 2): landings become less forgiving, which is why student canopies are large and lightly loaded.

USPA's B licence needs a completed canopy proficiency card (including accuracy landings within 10 m / 33 ft on 10 jumps), and its C licence asks for 25 landings within 2 m of a target: canopy skill is trained, not assumed.

A canopy flies 10 m/s forward and sinks 4 m/s. What glide ratio?

10 ÷ 4 = 2.5 : 1.

CH 5

Altitude, air & oxygen

At 10,000 ft every breath holds less oxygen.

Air pressure falls with height. Oxygen is still about 21% of the air, but each breath carries less of it, because the partial pressure of oxygen, which drives it into your blood, falls with total pressure. Thinner air also means less drag, so you fall slightly faster high up. And it's colder: in the standard atmosphere, temperature falls about 6.5 °C per kilometre.

Rules on extra oxygen for high jumps vary between countries and dropzones; ask yours. Tandem jumps at Skyhigh India are from 10,000 ft, the company says.

At 13,000 ft, what share of sea-level pressure is there?

About 61%.

CH 6

Wind drift, spotting & the rules

Jump from the right place in the sky, or the wind lands you somewhere else.

Under canopy you drift with the wind for several minutes, so the aircraft drops jumpers upwind of the landing area. Choosing that exit point is called spotting. Winds aloft are often stronger and in a different direction from those on the ground, which is why dropzones check them before every load.

Worked example · opening at 5,000 ft (1,524 m), descending at 5 m/s (from our Freefall Lab)
WindTime under canopyDrift with the wind
10 km/h≈ 5.1 min≈ 0.85 km
20 km/h≈ 5.1 min≈ 1.69 km

1,524 ÷ 5 ≈ 305 s ≈ 5.1 min; 20 km/h × 5.1 ÷ 60 h ≈ 1.69 km. Steering towards the landing area cancels some of this; spotting does the rest.

Opening at 4,000 ft (1,219 m) at 5 m/s in a 24 km/h wind: about how far do you drift?

1,219 ÷ 5 ≈ 244 s ≈ 0.068 h; × 24 ≈ 1.6 km.

CH 7

What the accident data show

About 1 death in every 200,000 to 430,000 jumps in recent US years.

The United States Parachute Association (USPA) counts every fatal skydiving accident in the US and divides by the number of jumps. That gives a rate per 100,000 jumps. A rate lets you compare years with different numbers of jumps.

Worked example · USPA fatality rates, US, 2021 to 2025
YearDeathsJumpsPer 100,000 jumpsAbout 1 death in
2021103.57 million0.28357,000 jumps
2022203.9 million0.51195,000 jumps
2023103.65 million0.27365,000 jumps
202493.88 million0.23431,000 jumps
2025163.47 million0.46217,000 jumps

Rate = deaths ÷ jumps × 100,000. For 2025: 16 ÷ 3,470,000 × 100,000 = 0.46. One death in 3,470,000 ÷ 16 ≈ 217,000 jumps. USPA gives the 1961 rate as 11 per 100,000 and says jumpers in 1961 were 22 times more likely to die on a jump than jumpers today.

What went wrong in 2025

  • Low turns: USPA says 4 of the 16 people who died in 2025 made an intentional low turn under a working main canopy. A turn speeds the canopy up and points it at the ground.
  • Speed into the ground: a canopy carries you like a fall from a height. The height is v² ÷ 2g. At 5 m/s that is 5² ÷ (2 × 9.81) ≈ 1.3 m. At 10 m/s it is about 5.1 m. At 15 m/s it is about 11.5 m. These speeds are examples to show the physics, not measured figures.
  • Emergency procedures: USPA says 4 people died without starting their emergency procedures, and 2 more after doing the wrong action. It quotes a study where about 10% of people acted well in an emergency, 10% became hysterical and 80% froze.
  • Students: no student died in 2025. USPA says this is only the second time since 1961.

Safety Practise the emergency, not only the jump

USPA asks every jumper to practise emergency procedures often and thoroughly, with a coach or instructor in the hanging harness at the drop zone. It also says a safe landing is usually still possible after a turn started too low, if the jumper stops the turn high enough.

CH 8

Opening altitude: counting the seconds

At 193 km/h, 1,000 ft goes by in under 6 seconds.

In freefall on your belly you fall at about 53.6 m/s (Ch 1). The ground comes up at that speed until you open. So every opening height is also a time budget: time = height ÷ speed. The USPA Basic Safety Requirements set the lowest heights at which the main canopy must be open.

Worked example · time left at 53.6 m/s belly freefall
Height above groundIn metresWhat USPA says happens hereSeconds to the ground
5,000 ft1,524 mTandem jumps: canopy open by this height28.4 s
3,000 ft914 mStudents and A-licence holders: canopy open by this height17.1 s
2,500 ft762 mB, C and D licence holders: canopy open by this height14.2 s
2,000 ft610 mC and D licence holders with a waiver: no lower than this11.4 s
1,000 ft305 mBelow this, USPA says to go straight to the reserve without a cutaway5.7 s

1 ft = 0.3048 m. 914 ÷ 53.6 ≈ 17.1 s. These times ignore the slowing down while the canopy opens, which takes a few more seconds (Ch 3). Head-down at about 71.4 m/s the times are a quarter shorter.

Why the heights are where they are

  • Time to fix a problem: a canopy can open badly. You need time to look, decide, cut away and pull the reserve. A higher opening gives more of that time.
  • Decision heights: USPA says students and A-licence holders decide and act on an emergency no lower than 2,500 ft, and B and C licence holders no lower than 2,000 ft.
  • Head-down: at 71.4 m/s the 2,500 ft budget shrinks from 14.2 s to 762 ÷ 71.4 ≈ 10.7 s.

Safety Open on time, every time

USPA calls these heights minimum opening altitudes. Students must wear an altimeter they can see. USPA says skydivers must be ready to carry out emergency procedures on every jump, and to start them no lower than their decision altitude.

A student opens at 3,000 ft. How many seconds of freefall were left?

914 ÷ 53.6 ≈ 17 s.

Why does a head-down jumper get less time from the same height?

They fall faster, about 71 m/s instead of about 54 m/s, so the same height takes less time.

Sources: USPA Skydiver's Information Manual 2-1: Basic Safety Requirements (opening altitudes) · USPA: 2025 fatality summary (emergency decision altitudes)

CH 9

The automatic backup: AAD

A small computer watches your speed and height and opens the reserve if you do not.

An automatic activation device (AAD) measures air pressure. Air pressure falls as you go up, so from pressure it works out your height above the ground and how fast you are coming down. If you are still falling fast at a low height, it cuts the loop that holds the reserve container closed, so the reserve can come out. The CYPRES 2 Expert model fires when you fall faster than 35 m/s (78 mph) at about 225 m (750 ft) above the ground.

Worked example · time left when a CYPRES 2 Expert fires at about 225 m
SituationSpeedTime to the ground from 225 m
Belly freefall53.6 m/s225 ÷ 53.6 ≈ 4.2 s
Head-down freefall71.4 m/s225 ÷ 71.4 ≈ 3.2 s
Under an open canopyabout 5 m/s down225 ÷ 5 = 45 s (the AAD does not fire)

CYPRES says 35 m/s is about 70% of freefall speed. With this page's belly speed it is 35 ÷ 53.6 ≈ 65%. A normal open canopy comes down far slower than that. Below about 40 m (130 ft) the unit stops working, because an opening there is no longer useful. The guide says the aircraft must always climb above about 450 m (1,500 ft).

What an AAD can and cannot do

  • It is a backup: the CYPRES guide says the unit is strictly a backup. It is not a substitute for proper training and for doing your emergency procedures on time. It can fail at any time for any reason.
  • Steep dives under canopy: the guide warns that a dive faster than 35 m/s under a main canopy below 225 m can fire the unit, which can leave two canopies out. It says: never do that.
  • Data from 2025: USPA says 3 of the 2025 deaths could have come out differently with a working AAD. It also says an AAD made for normal jumps can fail to fire in a wingsuit flat spin, because the fall is too slow.

Safety Keep it on, and keep it serviced

USPA rules require students to jump with a working AAD that meets its maker's service schedule. The AAD is a backup. Open at your own height and follow your own emergency procedure.
