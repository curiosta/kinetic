CH 1

Lift, drag & the wing

A bag of nylon becomes a wing the moment air fills it.

A paraglider wing is two skins of ripstop fabric joined by ribs into dozens of cells. Openings at the leading edge let air in, and that ram-air pressure inflates the cells into an aerofoil: a curved shape, thicker at the front, that turns the oncoming air slightly downward. By Newton's third law, pushing air down pushes the wing up. The same effect also shows up as lower pressure over the top surface than under the bottom (Bernoulli's principle). They are two descriptions of one flow.

The lift equation

Every wing, from a paraglider to a jet, obeys L = ½ × ρ × v² × S × CL: air density ρ, airspeed v, wing area S, and a lift coefficient CL set mostly by the angle of attack (the angle between the wing and the oncoming air). The pilot changes CL with the brakes and the speed bar.

Worked example · what the reference wing needs at trim

Pilot + gear + wing ≈ 100 kg, so weight ≈ 100 × 9.81 = 981 N. At trim, 37 km/h = 10.3 m/s at sea level (ρ = 1.225 kg/m³):

StepCalculationResult
Dynamic pressure q½ × 1.225 × 10.3²≈ 65 Pa
Lift needed≈ weight (lines are close to vertical)981 N
Lift coefficient needed981 ÷ (65 × 21 m²)CL ≈ 0.72

A CL around 0.7 is an ordinary, comfortable value for a wing at a moderate angle of attack. Pull brake and CL rises while speed falls; push the speed bar and the reverse happens. Lift stays roughly equal to weight either way.

✋
Feel the number65 Pa is tiny: about 6.6 kg-force spread over a square metre, the weight of a large bag of rice laid on a dining table. Spread over 21 m² it holds up a whole person, which is why a paraglider is so big, light and slow.

Speed squared: small changes, big effects

Because v is squared, 20% more airspeed means 1.2² = 1.44× the lift at the same angle of attack, and 20% less means only 0.64×. Drag follows the same law. This is why gusts feel so strong, and why a wing that loses airspeed close to the ground loses its lift quickly.

Drag: the cost of making lift

Drag is the force pulling backwards along the flight path. Part is parasitic (lines, pilot, harness, fabric), part is induced (the unavoidable cost of making lift with a finite wing, including the swirling wingtip vortices). In steady glide the pilot's weight is balanced by lift (up) and drag (back), and the glider descends at exactly the angle where they balance. For the reference wing, drag = weight ÷ glide ratio = 981 ÷ 8.5 ≈ 115 N.

A pilot flies at 30 km/h instead of 37 km/h at the same weight. Roughly what lift coefficient is needed now?

Lift must still equal weight, so CL scales with 1/v²: 0.72 × (37/30)² ≈ 1.1. That's a much higher angle of attack, closer to the stall (Chapter 5). Slow flight is not "safer" by default.

CH 2

Glide ratio, sink & wind

Height is your fuel tank, and glide ratio is your mileage.

The glide ratio (L/D) is how many metres you travel forward for each metre of height lost in still air. It equals lift ÷ drag. Beginner wings are typically quoted somewhere around 8 to 9; high-performance competition wings go well above 10. The sink rate is how fast you descend: airspeed ÷ glide ratio.

Worked example · a 1,000 m flight (roughly Billing take-off to Bir landing)
ConditionGround speedGlide over groundDistance in ~14 min
Still air37 km/h8.5 : 18.5 km
15 km/h headwind22 km/h≈ 5.1 : 1≈ 5.1 km
15 km/h tailwind52 km/h≈ 11.9 : 1≈ 11.9 km

Sink rate = 10.3 m/s ÷ 8.5 ≈ 1.2 m/s, so 1,000 m lasts ≈ 830 s ≈ 14 min whatever the wind. Wind only changes where you land. (The Billing–Bir height difference of about 1,000 m comes from secondary sources; see sources.md.)

The polar curve and the speed bar

Every wing has a "polar": a graph of sink rate against airspeed. Minimum sink comes at a slow speed (best for staying up in weak lift); best glide comes at a slightly faster speed (best for going far in still air). Into a headwind or through sinking air, pilots speed up with the speed bar to get through the bad air faster. They give up a little glide through the air to gain glide over the ground.

🧭
Feel the numberA 30 km/h headwind against a 37 km/h wing leaves you crawling forward at 7 km/h, slower than a brisk walk. Wind stronger than your trim speed means you go backwards over the ground. That is why every school sets conservative wind limits for students.
You're 300 m above a landing field 2 km upwind. Wind is 15 km/h. Can you make it with the reference wing?

Ground glide into a 15 km/h headwind ≈ 5.1 : 1, so 300 m buys about 300 × 5.1 ≈ 1.5 km. Not enough, so pick a landing option downwind or nearer. This is the reasoning behind "always have a landable field within glide".

CH 3

Thermals & ridge lift

You never climb by yourself. You find air that climbs faster than you sink.

If your wing sinks at 1.2 m/s and you're inside air rising at 3 m/s, your vario reads +1.8 m/s. All soaring comes down to this subtraction. The two main lift sources are thermals (heat) and ridge lift (wind on a slope).

Thermals: buoyancy you can ride

Sunlight heats the ground unevenly: dry fields, rock and villages warm faster than forest or water. Air touching a warm patch gets warmer and less dense than the air around it, so it rises (Archimedes again, as with a hot-air balloon). Bubbles or columns break away and climb, often marked by circling birds or, higher up, by a cumulus cloud.

Rising air expands and cools at the dry adiabatic lapse rate: about 9.8 °C per 1,000 m. Its dew point falls more slowly, roughly 2 °C per 1,000 m. When temperature meets dew point, water vapour condenses and a cloud forms. That gives pilots a rule of thumb:

Worked example · predicting cloud base

Cloud base above ground ≈ 125 m × (temperature − dew point). On a day of 30 °C with dew point 14 °C: 125 × 16 = ≈ 2,000 m above the ground. With dew point 22 °C (humid, post-monsoon), only 125 × 8 = 1,000 m. A humid day means low, often weak thermals.

Ridge (dynamic) lift

When wind blows onto a slope, the air has to go up. The upward component near the face is roughly wind speed × sin(slope angle).

Wind onto the faceSlopeUpward air near the faceNet climb for a 1.2 m/s-sink wing
10 km/h (2.8 m/s)30°≈ 1.4 m/s≈ +0.2 m/s (barely soarable)
18 km/h (5 m/s)30°≈ 2.5 m/s≈ +1.3 m/s
18 km/h (5 m/s)15°≈ 1.3 m/s≈ +0.1 m/s

Simplified: real lift is strongest just in front of and slightly above the ridge line, and fades with height. Kamshet's evening sea-breeze soaring on the Western Ghats ridges is a classic example (Nirvana Adventures describes "lift bands" as the breeze creeps up the Ghats).

Behind the ridge is rotor

On the downwind (lee) side the air spills over and tumbles into turbulent, sinking eddies called rotor. Being caught there low is one of the classic accident scenarios. Rule: if you can't stay upwind of the ridge, go out in front and land, never behind.

Your vario reads +0.5 m/s in a thermal and your wing sinks at 1.2 m/s at that speed. How fast is the air itself rising?

Air speed upward = your climb + your sink = 0.5 + 1.2 = 1.7 m/s.

CH 4

Wing loading & altitude

Same wing, different pilot, different mountain: different speeds.

Wing loading is all-up weight divided by wing area. Every certified wing size comes with a weight range in its manual, and flying inside it matters. From the lift equation, if weight goes up, speed must rise as √(weight) to keep the same angle of attack.

Worked example · bottom vs top of a weight range

Suppose a wing is certified for 85–105 kg. √(105/85) ≈ 1.11, so the heavy pilot flies about 11% faster, and sinks 11% faster too. To a first approximation the glide ratio doesn't change: a heavier glider goes down the same slope, just quicker. Heavier loading feels more solid in turbulence; lighter loading climbs better in weak lift.

Thin air at mountain sites

Air density falls with altitude. In the standard atmosphere it is about 1.225 kg/m³ at sea level and about 0.97 kg/m³ at 2,400 m. To make the same lift, true airspeed rises by √(1.225/0.97) ≈ 1.13.

Altitude (ISA)Air densityTrue airspeed at "trim"Effect

Hot days make it worse: warm air is less dense, so "density altitude" can be higher than the real altitude. You need a longer, stronger run to launch, and you land faster.

🏃
Feel the numberKinetic energy grows with speed squared. Landing at 41.7 instead of 37 km/h carries (41.7/37)² ≈ 27% more energy into your legs if you don't flare well.
A 70 kg pilot borrows a wing rated 85–105 kg. With 15 kg of gear, are they in range?

All-up weight = 70 + 15 = 85 kg: right at the bottom of the range. The wing will be slow and more easily deformed by turbulence. Instructors size wings by all-up weight, not body weight.

CH 5

Stalls, collapses & turns

A wing has two ways to stop working: too much angle, or too little.

Too much angle of attack: the stall

Lift coefficient rises with angle of attack, but only up to a critical angle. Beyond it the airflow can no longer follow the curved top surface, separates, and lift collapses. On a paraglider this usually comes from too much brake for too long. The wing slows, falls back behind the pilot, and recovery needs exactly the right, trained response. A stall happens at a critical angle, not at a fixed speed you can read.

Too little angle of attack: collapses

A paraglider holds its shape only by internal air pressure. If turbulence suddenly reduces the angle of attack (for example a strong gust from above, or flying fast into sinking air), part of the leading edge can fold under: an asymmetric or frontal collapse. Modern wings are tested to reopen by themselves. Under EN 926-2, beginner wings are class A (most passive); B, C and D demand progressively more skill.

Turning costs height: load factor

In a banked turn the lift must hold you up and pull you round the circle, so the wing has to make more lift than your weight. Load factor n = 1 ÷ cos(bank).

BankLoad factorStall speed ×Sink rate × (same CL)
30°1.15 g1.07≈ 1.24
45°1.41 g1.19≈ 1.68

Stall speed scales with √n, and sink at the same lift coefficient scales with n1.5. A spiral dive, used deliberately to lose height fast, can reach several g and heavy sink rates. That's why it's taught under supervision and why pilots must know how to exit it.

The safety layers

SIV (Simulation d'Incident en Vol) courses teach collapses, stalls and recoveries over water with an instructor on the radio and a rescue boat below. Pilots also carry an emergency (reserve) parachute; APPI's rules require a certified reserve on high flights from APPI 2 onward. Himachal Pradesh's 2025 amendment to its Aero Sports Rules requires tandem pilots to complete a safety (SIV) course; already-registered tandem pilots were given until 31 August 2026 (news reports; see sources.md).

Why does a pilot who pulls a lot of brake to "slow down for safety" in turbulence risk making things worse?

More brake = higher angle of attack, closer to the stall, while the gusts keep changing the angle. Instructors teach active flying: small, quick brake inputs to keep the wing overhead and pressurised, not a big constant pull.

CH 6

Weather & micro-meteorology

The sky is a fluid, and you're flying a leaf in it.

Wind gradient near the ground

Friction slows the lowest layers of air, so wind is usually weaker near the ground than at take-off height. On final approach you descend into slower-moving air and your headwind component drops. Keep a little extra speed near the ground and don't hang on deep brake until the flare.

Venturi, rotor and gusts

Wind squeezed through a gap or over a pass speeds up (continuity: same flow through a smaller area). Obstacles (trees, buildings, ridges) leave turbulent rotor downwind. A forecast "average" wind of 12 km/h with gusts to 30 km/h is a very different day from a steady 12.

Storms: cumulonimbus

A cumulus that keeps growing vertically can become a thunderstorm. Its updraft can be strong enough to carry a paraglider into the cloud ("cloud suck"), and its downdraft hits the ground as a gust front that spreads outward. When towers start building, pilots land early.

India's flying seasons are monsoon-shaped

Kamshet (Western Ghats)Oct–May (Temple Pilots); Nov–mid-June (Nirvana Adventures). East winds in winter, west sea-breeze in summer. Monsoon suspends flying for about four months.
Bir Billing (Dhauladhar)Billing Paul Adventure runs courses all year except 15 Jul–15 Sep. International competitions have been held here in late Oct–Nov (PWCA, 2023 and 2024).
Panchgani (Sahyadri)The 2026 Paragliding World Cup page describes February as the best month: mild temperatures, strong thermals and sea-breeze convergence lines.

Know your limits, then halve them

Most accidents in free flight trace back to a decision on the ground: flying in conditions beyond the pilot's skill. Talk to your instructor and local pilots, check the forecast, and remember that not flying is always an option. Tandem passengers: in Himachal Pradesh, operators and tandem pilots must be registered with the state tourism department. Ask to see the registration.

A forecast says ground temperature 26 °C, dew point 22 °C, light winds. What kind of thermal day do you expect?

Spread 4 °C → cloud base ≈ 125 × 4 = 500 m. Low base, humid air, weak or short thermals: a training or short local-flying day, not a cross-country day.

How it works in India

There is no single national licence ladder yet. The Aero Club of India (ACI) is India's National Air Sports Control (NAC-India), recognised by the Ministry of Civil Aviation under the National Air Sports Guidelines 2023, and it issues FAI sporting licences. A Lok Sabha reply (27 Jul 2026) confirms the 2022 policy was superseded by those 2023 guidelines, and that the sports ministry has not recognised any national sports federation for paragliding.

Schools use different systems. Temple Pilots and Nirvana Adventures (Kamshet) run APPI courses. PG Gurukul (Bir) says it follows BHPA training with a BHPA-certified instructor and issues ACI licences carrying an IPPI rating. Other Bir schools issue local "P1–P4" certificates, some via the Billing Paragliding Association. Before you book, ask: which system, which IPPI stage, and who signs it?

04 · Governing bodies

Who sets the rules

Globally, the FAI's hang gliding and paragliding commission (CIVL) writes the proficiency standards and sanctions championships. National federations and school systems train to them. In India, air sports sit under the civil aviation ministry's guidelines, with state tourism departments regulating operators where it matters most (Himachal Pradesh). You're here if…

You've done your basic course and want to fly independently, then learn to stay up: ridge soaring and thermalling. APPI 3 needs 30 supervised high flights and a thermalling or soaring certification; IPPI stage 4 needs 25 flights from 3 sites and 15 hours. Read Ch 3 and Ch 4.

Physics-smart questions to ask

How many flying days does the school expect in this season? Will I fly more than one site (IPPI 4 needs 3)? Is there a thermalling clinic? Who coaches me by radio on my first thermal days?

*Altitudes from secondary sources; see sources.md.

You're here if…

You fly independently and want safety training (SIV), cross-country (XC) skills, or to watch or enter competitions. SafePro Para stage 5 needs at least 100 flights or 50 hours on 5 sites; APPI 5 needs 200 flights, 100 hours on 10 sites plus advanced SIV and XC certifications. Read Ch 5 and Ch 6.

Physics-smart questions to ask

SIV: over which water body, with what rescue boat, and who is on the radio? XC: what retrieve plan and live tracking? Competitions: what qualification (IPPI stage, ranking) does the event require?
