CH 1

How rivers flow

A river has to move the same amount of water through every section. Squeeze it, and it speeds up.

The volume of water passing a point each second is the discharge, Q = A × v, where A is the cross-section area (roughly width × depth) and v the average speed. Unless water is added or taken away, Q stays the same along a stretch (continuity), so where the river gets narrower or shallower, it must flow faster. Add a steeper gradient and rocks, and you get a rapid.

Worked example · the same 120 m³/s through different sections
SectionWidth × depthAreaSpeed = Q ÷ A
Wide pool60 m × 4 m240 m²0.5 m/s
Normal run40 m × 2 m80 m²1.5 m/s
Narrow chute20 m × 2 m40 m²3.0 m/s

40 × 2 × 1.5 = 120 m³/s. Through a 20 m × 2 m chute: 120 ÷ 40 = 3 m/s. Real rivers are faster in the middle and near the surface, and slower near the banks and bed.

River features you can read from the bank

Once the river is flowing fast enough, the surface draws a map of what's underneath. A few landmarks:

  • Standing waves: where fast water piles into slower water, the surface rises into a wave that stays in place. A train of them is a wave train. They can be fun to punch through; very steep ones can flip a raft.
  • Pillows: water hitting a rock bulges up on the upstream face. The pillow tells you the rock is there; water usually divides around it.
  • Laterals: diagonal waves and seams that shove you sideways toward a bank, rock or hole. Ferry against them rather than fighting them head-on.
  • Tongues: the smooth V of fast water pointing downstream through a rapid: often the cleanest line.

Hydraulically, a rapid is where the flow flips between subcritical (slow, deep, gravity dominates) and supercritical (fast, shallow, inertia dominates). The jump back to subcritical is what builds waves and holes (Ch 5).

Grades: how hard is this water?

Rapids are rated on the International Scale of River Difficulty, from Class (Grade) I (moving water, small waves, few obstructions) through II–III (clear channels to more powerful, irregular waves needing manoeuvring) and IV–V (powerful, long or violent rapids demanding precise control) to VI (extreme, rarely run). American Whitewater, which publishes the scale, stresses it is not exact: a river's difficulty changes with water level, and cold, high or remote water needs an extra margin. In Rishikesh, the popular Shivpuri stretch has rapids such as Roller Coaster and Golf Course (Grade III+); the Kaudiyala run adds The Wall (Grade IV), according to Red Chilli Adventure. Kolad's Kundalika runs are Grade 2–3.

Dams, rain and snowmelt

Discharge changes the river completely. Kolad's rafting depends on water released each morning from the Bhira (Tata Power) dam upstream; monsoon rain raises it further. Himalayan rivers swell with snowmelt and monsoon rain, which is one reason Uttarakhand's rafting season on the Ganga, Alaknanda, Bhagirathi and Kali runs from 1 September to 30 June under the state's rules. In Himachal Pradesh, the Water Sports and Allied Activities Rules, 2021 define the season as the whole year except 15 July to 15 September (plus any days the Technical Committee or District Magistrate closes), and list the Beas among the state's rivers.

A 30 m-wide, 1.5 m-deep river flows at 1 m/s. What's its discharge?

30 × 1.5 × 1 = 45 m³/s, 45 tonnes of water every second.

CH 2

The force of moving water

Water that's twice as fast pushes four times as hard.

Water is about 800 times denser than air. The drag force it exerts on something held still in the current is F = ½ ρ Cd A v²: water density ρ (1,000 kg/m³) times a drag coefficient Cd times the area facing the flow A, times speed squared.

Worked example · push on a swimmer pinned against a rock (CdA ≈ 0.5 m², illustrative)
CurrentForceLike holding up
1.0 m/s≈ 250 N≈ 25 kg
1.5 m/s≈ 560 N≈ 57 kg
2.0 m/s≈ 1,000 N≈ 100 kg
3.0 m/s≈ 2,250 N≈ 230 kg

At 2 m/s: ½ × 1000 × 0.5 × 2² = 1,000 N. The CdA of 0.5 m² is a teaching value for a person side-on to the flow; real values depend on body position.

When a boat wraps: drag turns into a wall of water

The table above is for a person in the current. A raft or kayak pinned broadside against a rock presents a much bigger area, and once the upstream tube (or hull) dips, the river can start to fill it. Then the force is no longer just drag: it's the weight of the water piled against the boat, roughly F ≈ ρ g h A for a depth h of standing water on area A.

Worked example · a raft tube presenting 2 m × 0.6 m, with 0.4 m of water piled against it
WhatNumberCompared with drag at 2 m/s on a swimmer
Area A1.2 m²≈ 2.4× a side-on swimmer
Hydrostatic push ρ g h A≈ 4,700 N≈ 4.7× the 1,000 N drag figure above
Like holding up≈ 480 kgA whole crew's weight, pushing into the rock

1,000 × 9.81 × 0.4 × 1.2 ≈ 4,710 N. Real wraps are messier (partial fill, curved tubes, water spilling over), but the order of magnitude is why guides shout high side the instant a raft hits a rock: lift the upstream tube before the river climbs into it (Ch 6).

What this means for you

  • Don't stand up in fast water deeper than your knees. If a foot jams between rocks, forces like these can fold you over and hold you under (foot entrapment).
  • Defensive swimming position: on your back, feet up at the surface and pointing downstream to fend off rocks, arms out for balance. Swim actively towards safety when the water allows.
  • Boats pinned against rocks feel the same physics on a much larger area: a raft wrapped around a rock can hold tonnes of water.

CH 3

Buoyancy & life jackets

Most people only just float. A life jacket is what keeps your mouth above the foam.

Archimedes: the upward force equals the weight of fluid displaced. A human body is close to the density of fresh water, so with full lungs most of us barely float, and only part of the head stays out. A life jacket adds a few kilograms' worth of lift, high on the chest, so your face stays up. Whitewater makes this harder: aerated water full of bubbles is less dense, so the same jacket and body get less lift.

Worked example · lift from a life jacket with 7 litres of foam
WaterDensityLift from 7 L of foam (minus the foam's own small weight)
Clear river water≈ 1,000 kg/m³≈ 69 N (≈ 7 kg)
Foamy water, 30% air≈ 700 kg/m³≈ 48 N (≈ 4.9 kg)

7 L × 1 kg/L × 9.81 ≈ 69 N; with 30% air, 69 × 0.7 ≈ 48 N. The 7 L figure and the 30% air fraction are illustrative; life jackets are rated by buoyancy in newtons, and operators choose jackets suited to whitewater.

Wearing it right

  • Snug, every strap fastened: a loose jacket rides up over your face. Guides check by lifting the shoulder straps.
  • Helmet too: rocks are often just under the surface. Kolad's operators say every participant wears a helmet and life jacket throughout; Uttarakhand operators provide both.
  • Cold water: Himalayan rivers are fed by snow and glaciers. Sudden immersion makes you gasp involuntarily (cold shock), so keep your mouth closed when you go in. Some Rishikesh operators supply wetsuits and splash jackets in winter.
If the water is 40% air, how much lift does the same 7 L of foam give?

69 × 0.6 ≈ 41 N (about 4.2 kg). Foam really does let you sink lower.

CH 4

Ferry glides & eddies

To cross a river without being swept downstream, point upstream and let the current do half the work.

Your boat moves through the water at speed b; the water itself moves downstream at v. Your path over the riverbed is the vector sum. To go straight across, aim upstream at angle θ with sin θ = v ÷ b; you then cross at √(b² − v²). If the current is as fast as you can paddle, you can't hold a line across at all.

Worked example · crossing a 40 m river flowing at 1.5 m/s
BoatPoint straight acrossFerry glide
Kayak, 2 m/s20 s, carried 30 m downstreamAim ≈ 49° upstream; cross in ≈ 30 s, no drift
Raft crew, 1.2 m/s33 s, carried 50 m downstreamCan't hold a line: current is faster than the boat

Kayak: sin θ = 1.5 ÷ 2 = 0.75, θ ≈ 48.6°; crossing speed √(4 − 2.25) ≈ 1.32 m/s; 40 ÷ 1.32 ≈ 30 s. Pointing straight across: 40 ÷ 2 = 20 s, drift = 1.5 × 20 = 30 m.

Eddies

Behind a rock or on the inside of a bend, the main current leaves a sheltered pocket where water is slow or even circulates back upstream: an eddy. Paddlers "catch" eddies to stop, rest, scout the rapid below and wait for others. The eddy line, where fast and slow water meet, has strong shear: cross it with speed and the right edge or lean, or the current can flip a kayak.

✋
Feel the numberWalking across a moving airport walkway at an angle feels just like a ferry glide: aim a little "upstream" and you arrive straight across.

sin θ = 1 ÷ 2 = 0.5, so θ = 30° upstream; you cross at √(4 − 1) ≈ 1.73 m/s.

CH 5

Holes, strainers & swimming

The most dangerous parts of a river often look the least dramatic.

Water falling over a drop speeds up (v = √(2 g h)), plunges to the bottom and resurfaces downstream. Part of it flows back upstream at the surface, forming a hole or hydraulic that can hold a boat or swimmer. Smooth, uniform drops such as weirs and low-head dams make the most retentive holes, with no gaps to escape through.

Worked example · speed of water at the base of a drop (no friction)
DropSpeedDrag compared with 1 m/s water (∝ v²)
0.5 m3.1 m/s≈ 10 ×
1 m4.4 m/s≈ 20 ×
2 m6.3 m/s≈ 39 ×

2 m: √(2 × 9.81 × 2) ≈ 6.26 m/s; 6.26² ≈ 39. Friction and turbulence make real speeds lower, but the point stands: steep drops make powerful water.

Why some holes keep you

At the base of a drop, water plunges down, hits the bed and returns to the surface. Part of that surface water flows back upstream into the drop: a recirculating hydraulic or hole. A short, aerated hole that you can punch through is a play feature. A long, smooth, uniform hole — especially below a low-head dam or weir — can recirculate a boat or swimmer for a long time ("keeper"). The escape routes are the deep current underneath (swim down and out) or the softer edges of the hole, not the backwash at the surface.

✋
Feel the numberFrom the 2 m drop table above, water arrives at about 6 m/s. That is still ~36 times the dynamic pressure of a 1 m/s current — enough to hold a swimmer against the boil line until they find the deep exit or the edge.

The hazards guides watch for

  • Strainers: fallen trees and debris let water through but not people. The current pins you against them. Swim hard away; if you can't, go head-first and try to climb over.
  • Undercut rocks and sieves: water flows under or between rocks where a body can be trapped.
  • Holes: if caught, tuck into a ball or swim down and out with the deep current, or sideways to the edge of the hole.

Safety Follow the briefing

Every licensed trip starts with a safety briefing: how to sit, the paddle commands, what to do if you fall out, and how to be pulled back in. Uttarakhand's rules require guides to be examined every year on security and rescue. Listen, and ask questions before you launch.

Water drops 1.5 m over a ledge. Roughly how fast is it at the bottom?

√(2 × 9.81 × 1.5) ≈ 5.4 m/s.

CH 6

Boats, balance & the roll

A raft stays up because it's wide. A kayak stays up because you do.

A boat is stable when tilting it moves its centre of buoyancy (the centre of the displaced water) outward faster than the centre of mass moves, creating a torque that rights it. Naval architects summarise that with the metacentric height GM: roughly how far the righting lever grows per radian of heel. For a simple pontoon shape, the buoyant part of GM is BM = I ÷ V, where I is the waterplane's second moment of area and V the displaced volume — so beam (width) enters as its cube. A wide raft has huge GM; a narrow kayak has little, so the paddler balances it with their hips, like riding a bicycle.

Worked example · same displacement, different beam (illustrative pontoon model)
CraftBeam × waterline lengthI ≈ (L B³) ÷ 12BM = I ÷ V at V = 0.3 m³
Playboat kayak0.6 m × 2.5 m0.045 m⁴≈ 0.15 m
Creek kayak0.65 m × 2.7 m0.062 m⁴≈ 0.21 m
Small raft (two tubes)1.8 m × 3.5 m1.70 m⁴≈ 5.7 m

I for a rectangle is L B³ / 12; V = 0.3 m³ is about 300 kg of displacement (boat + paddler + kit, illustrative). Tripling the beam multiplies BM by about 27. A raft's enormous BM is why guests can stand and high-side; a kayak's tiny BM is why a hip snap (and a roll) matter. Real hulls add freeboard, tube shape and the paddler's weight shift — this table is only the width effect.

Worked example · why high-siding works on a pinned raft
SituationWhat the water doesWhat the crew does
Raft hits a rock side-onCurrent piles onto the upstream tube and drags it downEveryone moves to the downstream (rock) tube
Six people × 70 kg move about 1.5 m—Shift ≈ 6 × 687 N × 1.5 m ≈ 6.2 kN·m of righting torque

Torque = weight × distance moved: 6 × 70 × 9.81 × 1.5 ≈ 6,180 N·m. Lifting the upstream tube lets water flow under the raft instead of into it. Only do it on your guide's command.

The kayak roll

Upside down, a kayaker can right the boat without getting out. A hip snap rotates the boat while the paddle gives brief support at the surface; the body stays low and the head comes up last, keeping the centre of mass near the water so very little lift is needed. Rolls are learned on flat water first: Rishikesh kayak schools such as Sea Hawk Adventures teach wet exits, eddy turns and the roll on a four-day basic course before Class II rapids.

Power on the river

Falling water carries enormous power: P = ρ g Q Δh. A 100 m³/s river dropping 2 m through a rapid releases about 1000 × 9.81 × 100 × 2 ≈ 2 MW, while a paddler can sustain perhaps a few hundred watts. You don't fight the river; you use it.

Four rafters of 60 kg each shift 1.2 m across the raft. What torque does that create?

4 × 60 × 9.81 × 1.2 ≈ 2,830 N·m.

CH 7

Cold water: the first minutes of a swim

Cold water takes your breath first and your strength next. Dress for the swim, not for the boat.

Heat flows from warm to cold. It flows faster when the temperature gap is bigger and when the thing touching you conducts heat well. Water conducts heat about 23 times better than air (about 0.56 against 0.024 W/m·K near 0 °C). So a swim in a cold river pulls heat out of you far faster than the same air. The US CDC's NIOSH says hypothermia can happen in any water below 21 °C (70 °F).

Worked example · how the temperature gap changes heat loss (skin at about 33 °C, illustrative)
WaterGap to skinHeat loss compared with 20 °C water
20 °C13 °C1.0 ×
10 °C (50 °F)23 °Cabout 1.8 ×
5 °C, snow melt28 °Cabout 2.2 ×

Heat flow by conduction grows in step with the temperature gap: 23 ÷ 13 ≈ 1.8. The skin temperature is a teaching value. Moving water carries heat away faster still, because it keeps replacing the water you warmed.

Worked example · the first hour in cold water (Canadian Safe Boating Council, 1-10-1)
TimeWhat happensWhat helps
About 1 minuteCold shock: a gasp, then very fast breathingA life jacket keeps your head up while you gasp
About 10 minutesUseful movement: muscles and nerves cool and get weakUse this time to get to shore or to a boat
About 1 hourHypothermia can make you lose consciousnessGet out of the water, then warm up

The CSBC says breathing can be 600 to 1,000% faster than normal in cold shock. The National Center for Cold Water Safety warns that 1-10-1 can make the danger seem smaller: you cannot always control your breathing, you weaken the whole time, and many people drown before hypothermia.

What American Whitewater's Safety Code says

  • When the water is below 10 °C (50 °F), a wetsuit or drysuit is essential. The next best choice is wool or synthetic clothing, not cotton.
  • Cold-water immersion slows your reactions a lot, and it robs you of clear thinking.
  • Dress warmer than you would for the same trip on land, more so on rivers fed by snow and glaciers.
  • Uncontrollable shaking, loss of coordination or trouble speaking after a long time in the cold can mean hypothermia.
  • Do not wear waders in rough water. They can fill with water.

Safety A cold swim starts before you fall in

Put on the life jacket and the suit on the bank. The Minnesota DNR advises a foam life jacket, not an inflatable one, for cold water, because an inflatable can fail to inflate well in the cold. After a swim, warm the person up and get medical help if they show signs of hypothermia.

CH 8

Throw ropes and the swing to shore

A rope held from the bank turns the river's push into a swing. Stand in the right place and the current does the rescue.

When a swimmer holds a rope fixed on the bank, the river pushes them downstream. The rope stops them going further from the thrower, so they move on a circle around the thrower, like a pendulum. Split the river's push into two parts. The part along the rope pulls on the thrower. The part across the rope swings the swimmer toward the bank.

Worked example · how the push splits as the swimmer swings in (drag 560 N, from Ch 2 at 1.5 m/s)
Angle between rope and currentPush that swings you to the bankPull on the thrower
90° (rope straight across)about 560 Nabout 0 N
60°about 485 Nabout 280 N
30°about 280 Nabout 485 N
0° (swimmer straight downstream)0 Nabout 560 N

Swing = 560 × sin(angle). Pull = 560 × cos(angle). This simple model leaves out the jerk when the rope comes tight and the force from the swimmer's own speed, so the real pull on the thrower can be larger. As the swimmer gets close to straight downstream, the swing stops and the full push goes into the rope.

This is why American Whitewater tells the thrower to stand at the downstream end of an eddy and throw upstream to the swimmer. The current then swings the swimmer down and into the slow water of the eddy (Ch 4), before the swing dies out.

Throw and catch (American Whitewater Safety Code)

  • Hold the free end of the rope in your other hand and the bag in your throwing hand. Throw underarm, overarm or sideways, and aim over the swimmer so the rope falls across them.
  • In the water: grab the rope, not the bag. Turn onto your back and put the rope over the shoulder away from the thrower.
  • Do not tie knots or loops in the rope, and take care not to get tangled while you throw or swim. Loose ropes can trap people.
  • An arm, a paddle or a branch can also help a swimmer reach the shore.
  • Going into the water on a tied rope is high-risk. The code says to learn it only on a formal swiftwater rescue course.

Groups and gear

  • The code advises three or more people and at least two craft, so not everyone is in one raft.
  • Wear a snug whitewater life jacket with a minimum Performance Level of 70 (US Type III or Type V) and carry a throw rope where you can reach it.

Safety The thrower must not be pulled in

The full push of the river can land on the thrower when the swimmer hangs straight downstream. Stand where you have firm footing and brace before the rope comes tight. A rope tied to a rescuer is only safe with a quick-release rescue life jacket and formal swiftwater training.

CH 9

When the river rises

The same river can be easy in the morning and dangerous by afternoon. A rise in flow raises speed, force and power together.

Recall Q = A × v from Ch 1. When more water comes down (bigger Q), the river gets deeper and faster at the same time. Drag on a swimmer grows with speed squared (Ch 2). The power of the river grows with the flow too. For each metre of river, the power lost by falling water is about ρ × g × Q × slope.

Worked example · one 40 m wide run at two levels (illustrative numbers, CdA 0.5 m² as in Ch 2)
LevelDepthSpeedFlow Q = A × vDrag on a swimmer
Normal2.0 m1.5 m/s120 m³/sabout 560 N
After rain2.5 m2.0 m/s200 m³/sabout 1,000 N

40 × 2.5 × 2.0 = 200 m³/s. Drag: ½ × 1,000 × 0.5 × 2.0² = 1,000 N. A rise of only 0.5 m in depth nearly doubles the push on a swimmer here. The numbers are a teaching model. Real rivers change shape as they rise.

Worked example · river power per metre of length, slope 2 m per km (illustrative)
FlowPower = ρ g Q × slope
100 m³/sabout 2,000 W per metre
200 m³/sabout 3,900 W per metre

1,000 × 9.81 × 200 × 0.002 ≈ 3,900 W. Double the flow and the river releases double the power on the same stretch.

What American Whitewater's Safety Code says about high water

  • As flow goes up, the river's speed and power increase tremendously. Most rapids get harder and there are fewer eddies to stop in. Rescue gets harder.
  • Floating wood and strainers make even easy rapids dangerous.
  • A small rise where the river is wide and shallow is multiplied where it narrows, so the put-in can fool you.
  • Use reliable gauge data. In remote rivers the meaning of a gauge height can change from year to year.
  • Sun on snow, hard rain and dam releases upstream can raise the river after you start.
  • On snow-fed rivers the flow can be low in the morning and high in the afternoon, or the other way round, depending on how far you are from the snow.
  • Continuous rivers at high flow are extremely dangerous, because long stretches become one rapid.

Safety Check the level, then check it again

Check the gauge and the weather in the whole catchment before you start. Watch the banks during the trip. If the water climbs over markers or into bushes, get off at the next safe place.

04 · Governing bodies

Who sets the rules

Paddling has world federations for canoe/kayak and for rafting, national members in India, and state regulators for commercial rafting. We checked each on an official website.

Global

World federation

International Canoe Federation (Paddle Worldwide)

The global governing body for paddlesports, now branded Paddle Worldwide: canoe sprint and canoe slalom (both Olympic), plus disciplines such as dragon boat. Its federation directory lists India's member.

canoeicf.com
World federation

United Rafting (IRF + WRF)

The international governing body for rafting, formed by unifying the International Rafting Federation and the World Rafting Federation. Runs the Guide Training & Education (GTE) awards, first developed by the IRF in 2001.

worldrafting.com

India

National federation

Indian Kayaking & Canoeing Association (IKCA)

India's member of the International Canoe Federation, per the ICF's federation directory. (IKCA's own website was not available when we checked.)

canoeicf.com · IKCA
National federation

Rafting Federation of India

Listed by the International Rafting Federation as India's provisional member since 2013. The IRF lists no website for it. (An earlier "Indian Rafting Foundation" appears on the same IRF page as a past member, 2004–2008; we could not verify a current World Rafting India page, so it is not listed as active.)

internationalrafting.com
State regulator

Uttarakhand Tourism Development Board (UTDB)

Issues commercial rafting and kayaking permits under the Uttarakhand River Rafting/Kayaking Rules, 2014 (amended 2015, 2017 and 2018): seasons, permits, guide examinations and penalties for operating without a permit.

uttarakhandtourism.gov.in

First year

General guidance, not a source.

  1. Months 1–3: learn the basics with a school or club on this page.
  2. Months 3–6: join a club affiliated to the state association.
  3. Months 6–9: enter a club, district or open event.
  4. Months 9–12: ask your club about registering with the national federation and state trials.
