CH 1

How rivers flow

A river has to move the same amount of water through every section. Squeeze it, and it speeds up.

The volume of water passing a point each second is the discharge, Q = A × v, where A is the cross-section area (roughly width × depth) and v the average speed. Unless water is added or taken away, Q stays the same along a stretch (continuity), so where the river gets narrower or shallower, it must flow faster. Add a steeper gradient and rocks, and you get a rapid.

Worked example · the same 120 m³/s through different sections
SectionWidth × depthAreaSpeed = Q ÷ A
Wide pool60 m × 4 m240 m²0.5 m/s
Normal run40 m × 2 m80 m²1.5 m/s
Narrow chute20 m × 2 m40 m²3.0 m/s

40 × 2 × 1.5 = 120 m³/s. Through a 20 m × 2 m chute: 120 ÷ 40 = 3 m/s. Real rivers are faster in the middle and near the surface, and slower near the banks and bed.

Grades: how hard is this water?

Rapids are rated on the International Scale of River Difficulty, from Class (Grade) I (moving water, small waves, few obstructions) through II–III (clear channels to more powerful, irregular waves needing manoeuvring) and IV–V (powerful, long or violent rapids demanding precise control) to VI (extreme, rarely run). American Whitewater, which publishes the scale, stresses it is not exact: a river's difficulty changes with water level, and cold, high or remote water needs an extra margin. In Rishikesh, the popular Shivpuri stretch has rapids such as Roller Coaster and Golf Course (Grade III+); the Kaudiyala run adds The Wall (Grade IV), according to Red Chilli Adventure. Kolad's Kundalika runs are Grade 2–3.

Dams, rain and snowmelt

Discharge changes the river completely. Kolad's rafting depends on water released each morning from the Bhira (Tata Power) dam upstream; monsoon rain raises it further. Himalayan rivers swell with snowmelt and monsoon rain, which is one reason Uttarakhand's rafting season on the Ganga, Alaknanda, Bhagirathi and Kali runs from 1 September to 30 June under the state's rules.

A 30 m-wide, 1.5 m-deep river flows at 1 m/s. What's its discharge?

30 × 1.5 × 1 = 45 m³/s, 45 tonnes of water every second.

CH 2

The force of moving water

Water that's twice as fast pushes four times as hard.

Water is about 800 times denser than air. The drag force it exerts on something held still in the current is F = ½ ρ Cd A v²: water density ρ (1,000 kg/m³) times a drag coefficient Cd times the area facing the flow A, times speed squared.

Worked example · push on a swimmer pinned against a rock (CdA ≈ 0.5 m², illustrative)
CurrentForceLike holding up
1.0 m/s≈ 250 N≈ 25 kg
1.5 m/s≈ 560 N≈ 57 kg
2.0 m/s≈ 1,000 N≈ 100 kg
3.0 m/s≈ 2,250 N≈ 230 kg

At 2 m/s: ½ × 1000 × 0.5 × 2² = 1,000 N. The CdA of 0.5 m² is a teaching value for a person side-on to the flow; real values depend on body position.

What this means for you

  • Don't stand up in fast water deeper than your knees. If a foot jams between rocks, forces like these can fold you over and hold you under (foot entrapment).
  • Defensive swimming position: on your back, feet up at the surface and pointing downstream to fend off rocks, arms out for balance. Swim actively towards safety when the water allows.
  • Boats pinned against rocks feel the same physics on a much larger area: a raft wrapped around a rock can hold tonnes of water.
✋
Feel the numberWading across a knee-deep stream that's barely walking pace already tugs at your legs. A Grade III rapid moves several times faster, and the force grows with the square.
The current rises from 1 m/s to 2.5 m/s. How many times stronger is the push?

2.5² = 6.25 times: from about 250 N to about 1,560 N in our model.

CH 3

Buoyancy & life jackets

Most people only just float. A life jacket is what keeps your mouth above the foam.

Archimedes: the upward force equals the weight of fluid displaced. A human body is close to the density of fresh water, so with full lungs most of us barely float, and only part of the head stays out. A life jacket adds a few kilograms' worth of lift, high on the chest, so your face stays up. Whitewater makes this harder: aerated water full of bubbles is less dense, so the same jacket and body get less lift.

Worked example · lift from a life jacket with 7 litres of foam
WaterDensityLift from 7 L of foam (minus the foam's own small weight)
Clear river water≈ 1,000 kg/m³≈ 69 N (≈ 7 kg)
Foamy water, 30% air≈ 700 kg/m³≈ 48 N (≈ 4.9 kg)

7 L × 1 kg/L × 9.81 ≈ 69 N; with 30% air, 69 × 0.7 ≈ 48 N. The 7 L figure and the 30% air fraction are illustrative; life jackets are rated by buoyancy in newtons, and operators choose jackets suited to whitewater.

Wearing it right

  • Snug, every strap fastened: a loose jacket rides up over your face. Guides check by lifting the shoulder straps.
  • Helmet too: rocks are often just under the surface. Kolad's operators say every participant wears a helmet and life jacket throughout; Uttarakhand operators provide both.
  • Cold water: Himalayan rivers are fed by snow and glaciers. Sudden immersion makes you gasp involuntarily (cold shock), so keep your mouth closed when you go in. Some Rishikesh operators supply wetsuits and splash jackets in winter.
If the water is 40% air, how much lift does the same 7 L of foam give?

69 × 0.6 ≈ 41 N (about 4.2 kg). Foam really does let you sink lower.

CH 4

Ferry glides & eddies

To cross a river without being swept downstream, point upstream and let the current do half the work.

Your boat moves through the water at speed b; the water itself moves downstream at v. Your path over the riverbed is the vector sum. To go straight across, aim upstream at angle θ with sin θ = v ÷ b; you then cross at √(b² − v²). If the current is as fast as you can paddle, you can't hold a line across at all.

Worked example · crossing a 40 m river flowing at 1.5 m/s
BoatPoint straight acrossFerry glide
Kayak, 2 m/s20 s, carried 30 m downstreamAim ≈ 49° upstream; cross in ≈ 30 s, no drift
Raft crew, 1.2 m/s33 s, carried 50 m downstreamCan't hold a line: current is faster than the boat

Kayak: sin θ = 1.5 ÷ 2 = 0.75, θ ≈ 48.6°; crossing speed √(4 − 2.25) ≈ 1.32 m/s; 40 ÷ 1.32 ≈ 30 s. Pointing straight across: 40 ÷ 2 = 20 s, drift = 1.5 × 20 = 30 m.

Eddies

Behind a rock or on the inside of a bend, the main current leaves a sheltered pocket where water is slow or even circulates back upstream: an eddy. Paddlers "catch" eddies to stop, rest, scout the rapid below and wait for others. The eddy line, where fast and slow water meet, has strong shear: cross it with speed and the right edge or lean, or the current can flip a kayak.

✋
Feel the numberWalking across a moving airport walkway at an angle feels just like a ferry glide: aim a little "upstream" and you arrive straight across.
The current is 1 m/s and you paddle at 2 m/s. What ferry angle keeps you going straight across?

sin θ = 1 ÷ 2 = 0.5, so θ = 30° upstream; you cross at √(4 − 1) ≈ 1.73 m/s.

CH 5

Holes, strainers & swimming

The most dangerous parts of a river often look the least dramatic.

Water falling over a drop speeds up (v = √(2 g h)), plunges to the bottom and resurfaces downstream. Part of it flows back upstream at the surface, forming a hole or hydraulic that can hold a boat or swimmer. Smooth, uniform drops such as weirs and low-head dams make the most retentive holes, with no gaps to escape through.

Worked example · speed of water at the base of a drop (no friction)
DropSpeedDrag compared with 1 m/s water (∝ v²)
0.5 m3.1 m/s≈ 10 ×
1 m4.4 m/s≈ 20 ×
2 m6.3 m/s≈ 39 ×

2 m: √(2 × 9.81 × 2) ≈ 6.26 m/s; 6.26² ≈ 39. Friction and turbulence make real speeds lower, but the point stands: steep drops make powerful water.

The hazards guides watch for

  • Strainers: fallen trees and debris let water through but not people. The current pins you against them. Swim hard away; if you can't, go head-first and try to climb over.
  • Undercut rocks and sieves: water flows under or between rocks where a body can be trapped.
  • Holes: if caught, tuck into a ball or swim down and out with the deep current, or sideways to the edge of the hole.

Follow the briefing

Every licensed trip starts with a safety briefing: how to sit, the paddle commands, what to do if you fall out, and how to be pulled back in. Uttarakhand's rules require guides to be examined every year on security and rescue. Listen, and ask questions before you launch.

Water drops 1.5 m over a ledge. Roughly how fast is it at the bottom?

√(2 × 9.81 × 1.5) ≈ 5.4 m/s.

CH 6

Boats, balance & the roll

A raft stays up because it's wide. A kayak stays up because you do.

A boat is stable when tilting it moves its centre of buoyancy (the centre of the displaced water) outward faster than the centre of mass moves, creating a torque that rights it. A wide raft has huge stability; a narrow kayak has little, so the paddler balances it with their hips, like riding a bicycle.

Worked example · why high-siding works on a pinned raft
SituationWhat the water doesWhat the crew does
Raft hits a rock side-onCurrent piles onto the upstream tube and drags it downEveryone moves to the downstream (rock) tube
Six people × 70 kg move about 1.5 m—Shift ≈ 6 × 687 N × 1.5 m ≈ 6.2 kN·m of righting torque

Torque = weight × distance moved: 6 × 70 × 9.81 × 1.5 ≈ 6,180 N·m. Lifting the upstream tube lets water flow under the raft instead of into it. Only do it on your guide's command.

The kayak roll

Upside down, a kayaker can right the boat without getting out. A hip snap rotates the boat while the paddle gives brief support at the surface; the body stays low and the head comes up last, keeping the centre of mass near the water so very little lift is needed. Rolls are learned on flat water first: Rishikesh kayak schools such as Sea Hawk Adventures teach wet exits, eddy turns and the roll on a four-day basic course before Class II rapids.

Power on the river

Falling water carries enormous power: P = ρ g Q Δh. A 100 m³/s river dropping 2 m through a rapid releases about 1000 × 9.81 × 100 × 2 ≈ 2 MW, while a paddler can sustain perhaps a few hundred watts. You don't fight the river; you use it.

Four rafters of 60 kg each shift 1.2 m across the raft. What torque does that create?

4 × 60 × 9.81 × 1.2 ≈ 2,830 N·m.

03 · The paddling ladder

First raft trip → big water → learn to kayak → raft guide → trip leader & safety kayaker → competition

Customers don't need a licence to raft: licensed operators and certified guides take responsibility. Guides and operators are regulated (in Uttarakhand, by the state's River Rafting/Kayaking Rules), and United Rafting's Guide Training & Education (GTE) system sets an international standard for whitewater professionals. 04 · Governing bodies

Who sets the rules

Paddling has world federations for canoe/kayak and for rafting, national members in India, and state regulators for commercial rafting. We checked each on an official website.

Global

World federation

International Canoe Federation (Paddle Worldwide)

The global governing body for paddlesports, now branded Paddle Worldwide: canoe sprint and canoe slalom (both Olympic), plus disciplines such as dragon boat. Its federation directory lists India's member.

canoeicf.com
World federation

United Rafting (IRF + WRF)

The international governing body for rafting, formed by unifying the International Rafting Federation and the World Rafting Federation. Runs the Guide Training & Education (GTE) awards, first developed by the IRF in 2001.

worldrafting.com

India

National federation

Indian Kayaking & Canoeing Association (IKCA)

India's member of the International Canoe Federation, per the ICF's federation directory. (IKCA's own website was not available when we checked.)

canoeicf.com · IKCA
National federation

Rafting Federation of India

Listed by the International Rafting Federation as India's provisional member since 2013. The IRF lists no website for it.

internationalrafting.com
State regulator

Uttarakhand Tourism Development Board (UTDB)

Issues commercial rafting and kayaking permits under the Uttarakhand River Rafting/Kayaking Rules, 2014 (amended 2015, 2017 and 2018): seasons, permits, guide examinations and penalties for operating without a permit.

uttarakhandtourism.gov.in
