CH 1

Wind pressure grows with speed squared

Double the wind and the kite pulls about four times as hard.

Moving air pushes on a kite with a pressure of q = ½ ρ v², where ρ is air density and v is wind speed. The force on the kite is that pressure times its area times a coefficient that depends on the kite's shape and how it's flown. Because v is squared, small changes in wind make big changes in pull: a gust 50% stronger pulls 1.5² = 2.25 times as hard.

Worked example · a 9 m² kite at sea level, 15 °C (ρ ≈ 1.23 kg/m³)
WindWind pressure ½ρv²½ρv² × 9 m²vs 10 km/h
10 km/h4.7 Pa≈ 43 N (≈ 4 kg)1 ×

30 km/h42.6 Pa≈ 383 N (≈ 39 kg)9 ×
40 km/h75.7 Pa≈ 681 N (≈ 69 kg)16 ×

20 km/h = 5.56 m/s; ½ × 1.227 × 5.56² ≈ 18.9 Pa; × 9 m² ≈ 170 N. This is a force scale, not a measured line pull (Ch 3).

What it means on the snow

  • Gusts: pull rises with the square of the gust, so gusty days are much harder than the average wind suggests.
  • Kite size: pull scales with area, which is why riders choose smaller kites for stronger wind.
  • Learn in light, steady wind: schools pick conditions where beginners can control the kite.
The wind rises from 15 to 30 km/h. How much more does the kite pull?

(30 ÷ 15)² = 4 times.

Physics: dynamic pressure q = ½ρv².

CH 2

Altitude thins the air, cold thickens it

At 2,000 m the air is about a fifth thinner than at the coast, so the same wind pulls less.

Air density is ρ = p ÷ (R × T): it falls as pressure drops with altitude and rises as the air gets colder. NASA's Earth Atmosphere Model gives pressure in the lower atmosphere as p = 101.29 × [(T + 273.1) ÷ 288.08]^5.256 kPa, with standard temperature T = 15.04 − 0.00649 × h °C at altitude h metres, and density = p ÷ (0.2869 × (T + 273.1)).

Cold helps: at 2,000 m but −10 °C instead of the standard 2.1 °C, density is 79.6 ÷ (0.2869 × 263.1) ≈ 1.05 kg/m³, about 86% of sea level at 15 °C.

What it means on the snow

  • Mountain spots: the same kite in the same wind pulls less high up, so riders may need a bigger kite or more wind.
  • Cold days: colder, denser air pulls a little harder for the same wind.
  • Real example: Haugastøl, a snowkite base on Norway's Hardangervidda plateau, sits at 1,000 m above sea level.
Same wind and kite: about how much less pull at 3,000 m (standard atmosphere) than at sea level?

0.91 ÷ 1.23 ≈ 0.74, so about a quarter less.

CH 3

The wind window & apparent wind

A kite moving across the wind feels far more wind than you do.

A kite feels the apparent wind: the true wind combined with the air rushing past because the kite itself is moving. If the kite flies across the wind at speed u, the apparent wind is about √(v² + u²). In the middle of the wind window (the power zone, downwind of the rider) a moving kite gets a lot of apparent wind and pulls hard; at the edge of the window it slows and parks, and the pull drops.

Worked example · true wind 20 km/h
Kite's own speed across the windApparent windWind pressure vs a parked kite
0 (parked)20 km/h1 ×
20 km/h≈ 28 km/h2 ×
40 km/h≈ 45 km/h5 ×

√(20² + 40²) ≈ 44.7 km/h; (44.7 ÷ 20)² = 5. That's why diving a kite through the power zone can pull you off your feet.

Using the window

  • Edge of the window: the place to park the kite when you stop, launch or land.
  • Power zone: where to avoid sending the kite by mistake.
  • Steering: moving the kite up and down through the window controls how much it pulls.
True wind 30 km/h and the kite flies across it at 30 km/h. Roughly how much more wind pressure does it feel than when parked?

√(30² + 30²) ≈ 42.4 km/h; (42.4 ÷ 30)² = 2 times.

Physics: vector addition of velocities, dynamic pressure.

CH 4

Depower, quick release & the 5th line

Every kite rig has ways to cut the pull fast, and the rules make you carry them.

A kite's pull depends on its angle of attack, the angle at which air meets it. Pushing the bar away tilts the kite so air meets it at a smaller angle, which cuts the force: that's depower. A quick release lets go of the bar from the harness, and a safety leash lets the kite fall onto one line so it "flags out", flapping like a flag with almost no pull.

What official sources require or describe
SourceWhat it says
IKA Snowkite Class RulesA detachable kite leash with a safety release, able to fully flag out the kite; a quick release that disconnects the bar from the harness with one hand movement; front lines with a depowering device
IKA Snowkite Class RulesA brightly coloured helmet to EN 1385 or EN 1077 (or equivalent) worn at all times when racing
Swiss Kitesurf (school)Bars with a double quick release that can be triggered with a 3.5 kg tug; most winter kites have a 5th line so the kite can be landed and kept on the ground without force

A 3.5 kg tug is about 34 N (3.5 × 9.81), far less than the pull of the kite in Ch 1.

Practise before you need it

  • Know your release: schools drill triggering it until it's automatic.
  • Helmet: Swiss Kitesurf gives every student a harness and helmet; Haugastøl asks students to bring a helmet.
  • Space downwind: keep clear of people, fences and slopes where the kite could drag you.

Safety Know your quick release before you launch

A tug of about 3.5 kg fires the quick release, far less than the kite’s pull. Schools drill it until it’s automatic. Keep space downwind, clear of people, fences and slopes where the kite could drag you.

What does a kite do when it "flags out"?

It hangs from one line and flaps like a flag, giving almost no pull.

Sources: IKA Snowkite Class Rules (PDF) · Swiss Kitesurf snowkite school · Haugastøl kite course

CH 5

Slopes & avalanches

A kite can pull you up a slope that's steep enough to slide.

Switzerland's avalanche institute SLF says a slab avalanche needs a bonded snow slab over a weak layer, an extra load to trigger it, and a slope of at least 30°. Its long-term figures for fatal avalanches triggered by skiers: 97% happened on slopes steeper than 30°, 82% steeper than 35° and 43% steeper than 40°.

Worked example · what 30°, 35° and 40° look like as a gradient
Slope angleRise per 100 m across (tan θ)SLF: share of fatal skier-triggered avalanches on steeper slopes
30°≈ 58 m97%
35°≈ 70 m82%
40°≈ 84 m43%

tan 30° ≈ 0.577, so a 30° slope climbs about 58 m for every 100 m you travel across the map. Slopes look less steep than they are, so riders measure them.

What it means for snowkiters

  • Flat and gentle terrain: frozen lakes and open plateaus are where most people learn.
  • Read the bulletin: in the mountains, check the official avalanche bulletin before riding near slopes.
  • Weather changes: Haugastøl warns that weather and visibility on Hardangervidda can change fast.
A slope rises 50 m for every 100 m across. Is it steeper than 30°?

tan θ = 0.5, so θ ≈ 26.6°: no, just under 30°. SLF's figures still put 3% of fatal skier-triggered avalanches below 30°.

Sources: SLF: avalanche types · SLF: avalanche bulletin interpretation guide (PDF) · Haugastøl

CH 6

Wind chill

The wind that powers your kite also pulls heat from your skin.

Moving air carries heat away from skin faster than still air, so exposed skin cools as if the air were colder. The US National Weather Service calculates wind chill as 35.74 + 0.6215T − 35.75V0.16 + 0.4275TV0.16, with T in °F and V in mph. The metric form is 13.12 + 0.6215T − 11.37V0.16 + 0.3965TV0.16 with T in °C and V in km/h.

Staying warm

  • Cover skin: wind chill applies to exposed skin, so face, hands and neck matter most.
  • Warm clothes: Haugastøl asks students to bring warm winter clothes along with a helmet and skis or a snowboard.
  • Plan the walk back: if the wind drops, you'll be walking in the cold.

04 · Governing bodies

Who sets the rules

Snowkiting is part of sailing's world: a kite class under World Sailing, run by the kiteboarding association, with an ice and snow sailing association holding world championships. We checked each on its own official website.

Global

Class association

International Kiteboarding Association (IKA)

Administers the IKA Snowkite Class and its class rules, adopted as a World Sailing international class in 2020.

kiteclasses.org
World federation

World Sailing

The class's international authority, which co-operates with the IKA on the snowkite class rules and approves their changes.

sailing.org
Ice and snow sailing

World Ice and Snow Sailing Association (WISSA)

Founded in Germany in 1987. Holds a World Championship each year for ice and snow sailing, including a kite class on skis or snowboards.
