CH 1

Tyre grip, slip angle & the friction circle

Four patches of rubber, each about the size of a hand or a postcard, do all the work.

Everything a kart or car does (accelerate, brake, turn) is a force from the track on the tyres. A simple model says a tyre can give at most F = μ N, where N is the load pressing it down and μ is the grip coefficient. Racing tyres on dry tarmac can give μ above 1, so they can hold more than the car's weight in sideways force (we use μ = 1.2 as an illustrative value throughout this page).

That grip is a budget shared between braking or accelerating and cornering. Draw the forward and sideways forces as arrows: their combination has to stay inside a circle of radius μ N, the friction circle. Brake at 80% of the limit and only about 60% is left for turning, because √(1 − 0.8²) = 0.6.

Worked example · sharing one tyre's grip (friction circle)
Grip used for brakingGrip left for cornering
0%100%
50%87%
80%60%
100%0%

√(1 − 0.5²) ≈ 0.87. Grip left falls slowly at first, then very quickly: that's why the last bit of braking into a corner has to be released so smoothly.

How does a tyre make a sideways force at all? When you steer, the wheel points slightly inside the direction it is actually travelling. The difference is the slip angle, usually a few degrees. The rubber in the contact patch twists, and that twist pushes the kart sideways. More slip angle gives more force up to a peak; beyond it the tyre slides and the force drops away. That drop is why the limit arrives so suddenly.

You're using 50% of the grip for braking. How much is left for turning?

√(1 − 0.5²) ≈ 87%: far more than the 50% you might guess.

CH 2

Cornering force: m v² ÷ r

A corner is a constant sideways push towards its centre, and only the tyres can give it.

Following a curve of radius r at speed v needs a centripetal force F = m v² ÷ r. On a flat track the tyres provide it, up to μ m g. Setting the two equal gives the limit speed: v = √(μ g r). The mass cancels, so in this simple model a heavy car and a light kart on the same tyres have the same limit (real tyres lose a little grip per kilo at high loads, so weight still costs a bit). The sideways acceleration at the limit is v² ÷ r = μ g: 1.2 g for μ = 1.2.

Worked example · limit speed through different corners (no downforce, from our Grip & Corner Lab)
Corner radiusDry, μ = 1.2Less grip, μ = 0.8
10 m39 km/h32 km/h
30 m68 km/h55 km/h
60 m96 km/h78 km/h
120 m135 km/h110 km/h

For 30 m: √(1.2 × 9.81 × 30) ≈ 18.8 m/s × 3.6 ≈ 68 km/h. Four times the radius only doubles the speed, because v grows with √r.

With μ = 1.2, what's the limit speed through a 20 m radius corner?

√(1.2 × 9.81 × 20) ≈ 15.3 m/s, about 55 km/h.

CH 3

Weight transfer

Brake, and the nose dips; turn, and the outside tyres take the load.

The tyres push on the ground, but the mass of the car sits higher up, at its centre of mass. When you brake at deceleration a, that height creates a turning effect that moves load from the rear tyres to the front ones: ΔF = m a h ÷ L, where h is the height of the centre of mass and L the wheelbase. Accelerating moves load to the rear, and cornering moves it to the outside tyres (with the track width in place of L).

Worked example · a 1,000 kg car, centre of mass 0.5 m high, wheelbase 2.5 m (illustrative)
Braking atLoad moved to the frontLike moving…
0.5 g≈ 980 N100 kg
1.0 g≈ 1,960 N200 kg
1.2 g≈ 2,350 N240 kg

1,000 × 9.81 × 0.5 ÷ 2.5 ≈ 1,960 N at 1 g. A lower centre of mass, or a longer wheelbase, means less transfer.

Weight transfer is why technique matters. Loaded front tyres grip better, so the car turns in eagerly just as you come off the brakes; lift off the throttle suddenly mid-corner and the rear goes light and can slide. Karts are an extreme case: they have no differential (the rear axle is solid), so the chassis is designed to flex and lift the inside rear wheel a little in corners, letting the kart turn. And because grip is shared (Ch 1), drivers do most of their braking in a straight line, then trade brake for steering as they turn in.

Same car, braking at 0.5 g. How much load moves forwards?

1,000 × 0.5 × 9.81 × 0.5 ÷ 2.5 ≈ 980 N, about 100 kg.

CH 4

The racing line & the apex

The fastest way round a corner is the one with the biggest radius you can fit on the track.

Because the limit speed is √(μ g r) (Ch 2), drivers use the whole width of the track to make the arc as large as possible: outside on the way in, the apex (the point nearest the inside) in the middle, and outside again on the way out. For a 90° bend with inside radius a on a track of width w, the biggest arc that fits has a radius of about a + 3.4 w.

Worked example · a 90° bend, 8 m-wide track, 5 m inside radius, μ = 1.2 (illustrative)
LineRadiusLimit speed
Hugging the inside5 m28 km/h
Down the middle9 m37 km/h
Outside–inside–outside≈ 32 m70 km/h

5 + 3.41 × 8 ≈ 32.3 m; √(1.2 × 9.81 × 32.3) ≈ 19.5 m/s ≈ 70 km/h. The wide arc is slightly longer, but you cover it much faster.

The perfect geometric arc isn't always the fastest lap, though. A corner that leads onto a long straight is often taken with a late apex: you turn in a little later and do most of the turning early, so the car is straightened up sooner and you can accelerate earlier. The extra exit speed is carried all the way down the straight, which is worth more than a slightly slower entry.

Why does hugging the inside of a bend feel short but lap slowly?

Its radius is the smallest, so the grip limit √(μ g r) is the lowest: 28 km/h instead of 70 km/h in our example.

CH 5

Braking distance & g-forces on the driver

Twice the speed needs four times the room to stop.

At the grip limit the tyres can slow you at about μ g, so stopping from speed v takes a distance d = v² ÷ (2 μ g) and a time t = v ÷ (μ g). Distance grows with the square of speed. On top of that comes the distance you travel before you even press the pedal, which is why track marshals wave flags well before a hazard.

Worked example · braking distance at the limit, no downforce, no reaction time (from our Lab)
FromDry, μ = 1.2Less grip, μ = 0.8Time at μ = 1.2
60 km/h11.8 m17.7 m1.42 s
100 km/h32.8 m49.2 m2.36 s
150 km/h73.7 m110.6 m3.54 s
200 km/h131.1 m196.6 m4.72 s

100 km/h = 27.8 m/s; 27.8² ÷ (2 × 1.2 × 9.81) ≈ 32.8 m. Lose a third of your grip and the distance grows by half.

Every change of speed or direction is felt by the driver too. At 1.2 g sideways, a head and helmet of roughly 6 kg (a rough figure) need about 7 kg-force from the neck, on every corner of every lap; braking pushes the body forwards into the belts and seat. That's why racers train their necks and core, why the seat is fitted tightly, and why karting and racing schools insist on a proper helmet, gloves and closed shoes. Heat matters too: a closed helmet and race suit in an Indian summer make drinking water part of the plan.

How far do you need to stop from 150 km/h at μ = 1.2?

41.7² ÷ (2 × 1.2 × 9.81) ≈ 73.7 m, more than twice the distance from 100 km/h.

CH 6

Downforce & tyre temperature

Wings are upside-down aircraft wings: they push the car into the track.

A wing that makes lift on an aircraft makes downforce when it's turned upside down: F = ½ ρ CLA v², where ρ is the air density and CLA sums up the size and shape of the wings and floor. Downforce adds to the load on the tyres without adding mass, so the friction limit μ N rises while the force needed to turn, m v² ÷ r, doesn't. It grows with v², so it matters most in fast corners. Karts make very little; winged single-seaters make a lot.

Worked example · an illustrative winged car: 700 kg with driver, CLA = 3 m²
SpeedDownforceAs a share of its weight
100 km/h≈ 1.4 kN0.20 ×
150 km/h≈ 3.1 kN0.46 ×
200 km/h≈ 5.6 kN0.81 ×
250 km/h≈ 8.7 kN1.26 ×

½ × 1.2 × 3 × 55.6² ≈ 5,560 N at 200 km/h. In a 100 m corner with μ = 1.2 the limit rises from 124 km/h without wings to about 149 km/h with them (1.74 g), as our Lab shows.

Grip also depends on temperature. Rubber is viscoelastic: as it rolls, it flexes and recovers a little out of step, turning some energy into heat (hysteresis), and that same property helps it key into the track's surface. Too cold and the rubber is hard and slides early; inside its working range it grips best; overheated, it goes greasy and wears fast. That's why drivers warm up on the first lap, why a session starts gently, and why grip changes as a track rubbers in or cools down.

The illustrative car makes 1.4 kN at 100 km/h. What does it make at 200 km/h?

Twice the speed, four times the force: about 5.6 kN.

03 · Licences & levels

Arrive & drive → karting academy & first licence → national championships → cars → FIA international grades

Recreational karting needs no licence. 04 · Governing bodies

Who sets the rules

Motorsport has a clear chain of authority: the FIA governs world motor sport and sets the international licence grades, its karting arm runs international karting, and each country has one national authority that issues licences and sanctions events. We checked each on an official source.

Global

World governing body

FIA · Fédération Internationale de l'Automobile

Governs world motor sport and publishes the International Sporting Code. Its Appendix L sets the international licence grades, from Grade G for young karters to Grade A and the FIA Super Licence.

fia.com · Appendix L 2026
Karting

FIA Karting (CIK-FIA)

The FIA's international karting body. Licences are issued by national authorities; three International Karting grades, G, F and E, depend on age, and taking part in an unauthorised event can cost you your licence.

fiakarting.com

India

National authority

FMSCI · Federation of Motor Sports Clubs of India

India's member of the FIA, listed by the FIA for both sport and mobility. It issues competition licences, sanctions events and warns licence holders not to take part in unauthorised events, which have no insurance and no track safety.

fia.com · members
Licences

FMSCI licence portal

Where drivers apply: it lists eSports, One Event, Club Sport / National / International, team entrant and international individual entrant licences.

lic.fmsci.co.in
