Hang on, shift, glide. You steer a rigid wing with your own body.

A hang glider is a rigid wing you hang beneath and steer by moving your body. It glides further and flies faster than a paraglider, and it lands faster too. CH 1

The rigid wing: lift, glide ratio & speed

A hang glider is a wing on an aluminium frame, and it is always going downhill through the air.

A hang glider has no engine. It trades height for distance, sliding down through the air the way a ball rolls down a gentle slope. How far it goes for each metre of height is its glide ratio: distance ÷ height = lift ÷ drag. The wing makes lift L = ½ ρ v² S CL, with ρ the air density, v the airspeed, S the wing area and CL how hard the wing is working.

Unlike a paraglider, which is a soft fabric wing held in shape by the air, a hang glider's sail is stretched over a rigid frame. That frame keeps its shape at higher speeds, so it glides further and flies faster. Wikipedia's figures: about 17:1 for a topless flex wing and about 20:1 for a rigid wing, against a best of about 10:1 for a paraglider (Wikipedia, Hang gliding). USHPA, the US hang gliding and paragliding body, describes gliders of 45–90 lb that normally fly at 20–30 mph and can exceed 80 mph (USHPA).

Worked example · how far you glide from 300 m in still air
WingGlide ratioDistance
Paraglider (best, from Wikipedia)10:13.0 km
Hang glider (illustrative)15:14.5 km
Topless flex wing (Wikipedia)17:15.1 km
Rigid wing (Wikipedia)20:16.0 km

Distance = height × glide ratio. Real glide is lower in sinking air or a headwind and higher in rising air.

Speed and lift. With pilot and glider at 110 kg (illustrative) and a 14 m² wing, the wing needs CL ≈ 1.04 at 40 km/h but only ≈ 0.46 at 60 km/h: lift rises with v², so faster flight needs a much shallower angle of attack. Slow down too far and the wing can't make enough lift at any angle: that's the stall.

You're at 300 m with a 15:1 glide in still air. How far can you reach?

300 × 15 = 4.5 km, and less with a headwind or sinking air.

CH 2

Weight-shift control & pitch stability

There are no control surfaces: you steer by moving your own body under the wing.

The pilot lies prone in a harness hung from one point on the frame and holds a triangular control bar. Moving your body sideways shifts the centre of mass and rolls the glider; pushing the bar out moves your weight back, raising the nose and slowing down; pulling in moves your weight forward and speeds up (Wikipedia). The turning effect is a moment: M = F × d. An 80 kg pilot shifting 10 cm makes a moment of about 78 N·m (illustrative).

Pitch stability. The wing's sweep and washout (a twist that lowers the angle of attack towards the tips) make it return to its trim speed after a disturbance, flying "hands off" in calm air. Weight shift only works while the wing is positively loaded: when turbulence unloads it, reflex lines or washout rods keep a minimum of washout so the nose comes back up (Wikipedia, Stability).

Compared with a paraglider. A paraglider pilot sits in a harness far below a soft wing and steers with brake lines; the wing can collapse in turbulence and reinflate. A hang glider's frame can't collapse, but if it is pitched past recovery it can tumble, turning end over end, which typically breaks the airframe. That's why hang glider pilots carry a reserve parachute that brings down pilot and glider together.

Each design has its own way of getting into trouble: neither is the safe one.

You push the control bar out. What happens?

Your weight moves back, the nose rises and the glider slows down; too far and it stalls.

CH 3

Launch, landing & the wind

You run to flying speed on your own legs, so the wind decides how far you have to run.

The wing only flies on airspeed, its speed through the air. Your ground speed is airspeed minus headwind. That's why CIVL's SafePro Delta proficiency standard teaches taking off and landing into the wind, and warns that wind force rises with the square of the wind speed (CIVL SafePro Delta 2025).

Worked example · landing a 110 kg pilot and glider (illustrative), airspeed 30 km/h
WindGround speedEnergy to lose, ½ m v²
15 km/h headwind15 km/h≈ 0.95 kJ
No wind30 km/h≈ 3.8 kJ
10 km/h tailwind40 km/h≈ 6.8 kJ

A downwind landing carries about seven times the energy of a landing into a 15 km/h breeze: the reason pilots plan every approach into the wind.

Landing is a flare: just above the ground the pilot pushes the bar out hard, raising the nose so drag soaks up the speed before the feet touch. A hang glider needs a flat landing area of roughly 15–60 m (Wikipedia), far more than a paraglider, because it lands faster. Getting the flare timing wrong, or landing downwind, are classic causes of the "whack": nose-first into the ground.

Safety Hang gliding is unforgiving, and India has very little of it

USHPA publishes yearly fatality reports for US hang gliding and paragliding (USHPA), but there's no reliable count of how many people fly, so no honest fatality rate can be quoted. What the physics does say: a stall or a misjudged landing near the ground leaves no height to recover. In India we found one school claiming to teach hang gliding, and no national hang-gliding events. Train only with a qualified instructor, and if you learn abroad, learn under that country's rating system.

You land with a 10 km/h tailwind instead of a 15 km/h headwind, at 30 km/h airspeed. How much more energy do you carry?

Ground speed 40 vs 15 km/h: (40 ÷ 15)² ≈ 7 times the energy.

CH 4

Turns, stalls & turbulence

Bank the wing and it has to hold up more than your weight, so it stalls at a higher speed.

In a level turn at bank angle φ the wing must produce lift n = 1 ÷ cos φ times the weight: the load factor. Because lift goes with v², the stall speed rises by √n. A turn that's comfortable at altitude can stall the wing near the ground, where there's no height to recover.

Worked example · load factor and stall speed in a level turn (wings-level stall 30 km/h, illustrative)
BankLoad factorStall speed
30°1.15 g≈ 32 km/h
45°1.41 g≈ 36 km/h
60°2.0 g≈ 42 km/h

At 60° of bank the wing carries twice your weight and stalls 41% faster. CIVL's SafePro Delta tells early-stage pilots to avoid turns, downwind flying and slow speeds close to the ground.

Turbulence. Gusts change the airflow over the wing in an instant: a sudden updraft raises the angle of attack (towards a stall), a downdraft unloads the wing. Strong unloading is what can start a tumble (Ch 2). CIVL's standard warns students against flying in gusty, strong or turbulent conditions until they have trained for them (CIVL SafePro Delta).

In a 60° banked turn, by how much does the stall speed rise?

Load factor 2, so stall speed × √2 ≈ 1.41: about 41% higher.

CH 5

Towing & the weak link

Where there are no hills, a winch or an aircraft pulls the glider up, and a deliberately weak link keeps the pull safe.

Hang gliders can be towed aloft by a winch on flat ground or behind a light aircraft ("aerotow"). Tow tension pulls on the glider, and if the glider swings off the line of the tow (a lockout) the tension usually rises and makes things worse. So every tow system includes a weak link: a piece of line designed to snap first (NZHGPA TM05 Aerotow Manual).

Worked example · weak link strength (NZHGPA TM05)
QuantityValue
Glider weak link≈ 1 g: about 90–100 kg breaking load
As a force≈ 0.88–0.98 kN
Tug-end link10% stronger, max 100 kg or 85% of glider + pilot weight

The link limits how hard the line can pull: the glider sees at most about its own weight in tow force. When it breaks, the glider suddenly loses that pull and the pilot must lower the nose and fly away, which is why tows start with enough height and in safe places.

TM05 also requires a reliable release at both ends, a hook knife for the glider pilot and eye protection against a snapping bridle. In India, SkyVentures says it uses a hydraulic payout winch for its courses (SkyVentures); ask any school how its tow system, weak links and release are checked.

Why is the weak link meant to break at about 1 g?

So the tow line can never pull the glider much harder than its own weight, for example in a lockout, where tension rises and the glider is pulled off course.

CH 6

Thermals: riding rising air

A glider always sinks through the air. To climb, you find air that rises faster than you sink.

The sun warms the ground, and the ground warms the air above it. Warm air is less dense, so a bubble of it rises. As it rises, the pressure around it falls, so it expands and cools. The FAA Glider Flying Handbook says rising dry air cools by about 3 °C per 1,000 ft (the dry adiabatic lapse rate). The bubble keeps rising while it stays warmer than the air around it. That rising column is a thermal.

Worked example · the FAA's stable and unstable air example (20 °C at the ground)
CaseRising bubble at 3,000 ftAir around it at 3,000 ftResult
A11 °C9 °Cbubble is warmer: it keeps rising, a good thermal
B11 °C13 °Cbubble is colder: it sinks back, stable air

20 °C − 3 × 3 °C = 11 °C. The handbook says case B becomes unstable if the ground warms by more than 2 °C, or the air at 3,000 ft cools by more than 2 °C.

Your climb rate = the speed the air rises − your sink rate. In a turn the wing has to carry more than your weight (Ch 4), so it sinks faster than in straight flight.

Worked example · climb rate in a thermal with a sink rate of 1.0 m/s in the turn (illustrative)
Air rises atYour sinkYou climb at
0.5 m/s1.0 m/s−0.5 m/s (you still go down)
1.0 m/s1.0 m/s0 m/s (you hold height)
2.0 m/s1.0 m/s+1.0 m/s
3.0 m/s1.0 m/s+2.0 m/s

The 1.0 m/s sink in a turn is a teaching value. The Glide Lab uses about 0.74 m/s for straight flight at 15:1 and 40 km/h.

Signs of thermals (FAA Glider Flying Handbook)

  • Small cumulus clouds, other gliders circling, and circling birds.
  • In dry places, dust devils often mark thermals starting from the ground.
  • Dark ground such as tilled soil or large car parks can make strong thermals.

Safety Thunderstorms: lift you cannot fly out of

The handbook says updrafts under a storm cloud can be over 1,000 ft per minute (about 5 m/s) and can pull a glider into the cloud. Downbursts can sink at 2,000 ft per minute (about 10 m/s) or more, and 3,000 ft per minute in extreme cases. A normal approach from 800 ft takes about 3 minutes. In 2,500 ft per minute sink, the glider meets the ground in 19 seconds. Stay away from towering cumulus and storms.

CH 7

Ridge lift and the lee side

Wind that hits a hill has to go up. On the far side, it comes down just as hard.

When wind meets a slope, the air is pushed up along it. The upward part of the wind is about wind speed × sin(slope angle). Only the part of the wind that blows straight at the ridge counts: wind × cos(angle off the ridge). The FAA handbook says an ideal ridge slopes about 1 in 4. Shallower slopes do not lift the air enough to beat a glider's sink.

Worked example · upward air on the face of a slope, wind 25 km/h (6.9 m/s) straight at the ridge (simple model)
SlopeAngleUpward airAgainst 0.74 m/s sink
1 in 4 (FAA ideal)about 14°about 1.7 m/sclimb
1 in 10about 6°about 0.7 m/sabout level, no climb

6.9 × sin 14° ≈ 1.7 m/s. This simple model is for learning only. The real flow over a hill is more complex.

Worked example · how much of a 25 km/h wind blows straight at the ridge
Wind angle off straight-onUseful part (cos)Useful wind
0°1.0025 km/h
30°0.87about 22 km/h
45°0.71about 18 km/h
60°0.50about 13 km/h

The FAA handbook says wind within 45° of straight-on still gives adequate lift. It says 15 to 20 knots (about 28 to 37 km/h) straight at the ridge is best.

Ridge rules (FAA Glider Flying Handbook)

  • With 10 knots of wind or less, avoid flying low over any ridge, because you can meet sink.
  • Make every turn away from the ridge. A turn toward it adds ground speed downwind and can end in the slope.
  • Approach a ridge at an angle (about 45°), so you can turn away fast if you hit sink.
  • Near the ridge, fly faster than minimum sink speed for better control.
  • Hold a crab angle so the wind does not drift you over the top into the lee side.

Safety The lee side pushes you down

The air that went up the front comes down behind the ridge. The handbook says this downdraft can reach 2,000 ft per minute (about 10 m/s) or more near a steep ridge in strong wind, with turbulence and rotors. Do not fly on the crest or below the top on the downwind side. Plan to cross ridges high, with a height limit you set before the flight.

CH 8

The polar: slow to stay up, fast to go far

One speed keeps you up longest. A faster one takes you furthest. Into wind, faster still.

A glider's polar is a graph of its sink rate at each airspeed. Two speeds on it matter most. Minimum sink speed loses the least height per second, so it keeps you up longest. Best glide speed (best L/D) loses the least height per metre forward, so it takes you furthest. The glide ratio at any speed is airspeed ÷ sink rate.

Worked example · the FAA's example polar (a sailplane, not a hang glider)
PointAirspeedSink rateGlide ratio in still air
Minimum sink40 knots1.9 knotsabout 21:1
Best glide (best L/D)50 knots2.1 knotsabout 24:1

50 ÷ 2.1 ≈ 24. 40 ÷ 1.9 ≈ 21. A hang glider's polar is a curve of the same kind, at lower speeds and with a smaller glide ratio (Ch 1).

Into a headwind, your speed over the ground is airspeed − headwind. The FAA handbook says to find the best speed in a headwind, shift the start of the graph by the wind speed. That always gives a faster speed than in still air.

Worked example · the same two speeds into a 20 knot headwind
AirspeedGround speedSink rateGlide over the ground
40 knots20 knots1.9 knotsabout 10.5:1
50 knots30 knots2.1 knotsabout 14.3:1

30 ÷ 2.1 ≈ 14.3. Flying slowly into wind wastes height. In sinking air the same idea applies: fly faster to get through it sooner.

Weight and the polar (FAA Glider Flying Handbook)

  • More weight raises every speed on the polar. In the FAA example, going from 800 to 1,200 lb raises the best glide speed from 60 to 73 knots.
  • The heavier glider sinks faster, but it glides the same distance at the higher speed. The glide ratio does not change.

Safety Pick the speed for the job

Low over the landing field, into wind, or in sink, a slow speed can leave you short. Know your wing's minimum sink and best glide speeds, and add speed when the wind is strong.

CH 9

High and cold: thin air

The air at 3,000 m has the same share of oxygen as at sea level, but about a third less pressure to push it into your lungs.

Air is about 21% oxygen at every height you can fly. What falls with height is the pressure. The FAA's Pilot's Handbook says oxygen's partial pressure falls in step with air pressure. Lower pressure pushes less oxygen into your blood. Lack of oxygen is hypoxia. The air also gets colder as you climb.

Worked example · NASA standard atmosphere (15 °C at sea level)
HeightAir pressure (share of sea level)Oxygen partial pressureStandard temperature
0 m100%about 21 kPa15 °C
1,500 mabout 84%about 18 kPaabout 5 °C
3,000 m (about 10,000 ft)about 69%about 15 kPaabout −4 °C
4,500 m (about 14,800 ft)about 57%about 12 kPaabout −14 °C

NASA's model: T = 15.04 − 0.00649 × h (°C, h in m), and p = 101.29 × [(T + 273.1) ÷ 288.08]^5.256 kPa. Oxygen partial pressure ≈ 0.21 × p. Real days are warmer or colder than the standard.

What the FAA says about hypoxia

  • Above 10,000 ft (about 3,000 m), the symptoms of hypoxia get worse and the time of useful consciousness drops fast.
  • The first signs can be a happy, carefree feeling (euphoria), then slower reactions, poor judgement, headache, blue lips and fingernails, and tingling. You can feel that everything is normal when it is not.
  • Time of useful consciousness is the time you have to make sound decisions without extra oxygen: 30 minutes or more at 20,000 ft, but only 5 to 10 minutes at 22,000 ft and 3 to 5 minutes at 25,000 ft.
  • One ounce of alcohol can act like an extra 2,000 ft of height on your body.

US flight rules give a useful benchmark. Under 14 CFR 91.211, the crew of a US-registered aircraft must use oxygen above 12,500 ft for any time over 30 minutes, and at all times above 14,000 ft. These rules are for aircraft. They show where pilots in general start to need oxygen. Follow the rules where you fly.

Safety Cold and thin air together

At about 4,500 m the standard air is about −14 °C, before the wind of flight. Dress for the cold at the top of the climb, not at launch. The FAA says the treatment for hypoxia is to fly lower or use extra oxygen. If you notice any symptom, go down.

🏔️
Feel the numberAt 3,000 m every breath brings in about two-thirds of the oxygen it does at sea level.

Why is euphoria a dangerous first sign of hypoxia?

It makes you feel fine, so you do not notice your judgement is getting worse.

Who sets the rules

Hang gliding is run worldwide through the FAI's CIVL commission and, country by country, by national bodies that issue ratings. India's FAI member, the Aero Club of India, covers hang gliding alongside paragliding; we found no separate Indian rating scheme.

How we label accreditation. Why it matters: an accredited school is checked against a standard for instructors, equipment and safety; a school’s own claim isn’t checked. School’s claim — x means only the school's own site says so. Accreditation: unverified means the school names no certifying body. We found no Indian accreditation list for hang-gliding schools, so ask any school which country's rating system its instructors hold and how its tow system is checked.
