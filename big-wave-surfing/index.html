CH 1

How big is big: measuring a wave

The biggest wave ever recorded as surfed stood 26.21 m from trough to crest: taller than an eight-storey building.

Big-wave records measure the face from the trough in front of the wave to its crest. Guinness World Records lists the largest wave surfed (unlimited, male) as 26.21 m (86 ft), ridden by Sebastian Steudtner at Praia do Norte, Nazaré, Portugal, on 29 October 2020, authenticated by the World Surf League. Guinness notes that the accuracy of a wave measurement depends on things like the weather and the camera angles, and that frames were extracted and corrected geometrically for the camera's position and tilt.

Energy grows with height squared (Ch 2): (15 ÷ 3)² = 25; (26.21 ÷ 3)² ≈ 76.

Why measuring is hard

  • Moving reference: the trough and crest move and change shape every second.
  • Photos, not tape measures: heights come from images, corrected for where the camera was.
  • Surfers as a scale: analysts use the rider's known size in the frame.
A 12 m wave versus a 3 m wave: how much more energy?

(12 ÷ 3)² = 16 times.

CH 2

Energy grows with height squared

A 15 m face carries about 25 times the energy of a 3 m one.

In linear wave theory the energy stored in each square metre of sea is E = ⅛ ρ g H² (ρ ≈ 1,025 kg/m³ for seawater). Double the height and the energy quadruples. Breaking big waves are steeper and more chaotic than this theory assumes, so treat it as a guide to scale, not an exact figure.

Worked example · energy per square metre of sea
HeightE = ⅛ρgH²vs 3 m
3 m≈ 11 kJ1 ×
6 m≈ 45 kJ4 ×
15 m≈ 283 kJ25 ×
20 m≈ 503 kJ≈ 44 ×

15 m: 1,025 × 9.81 × 225 ÷ 8 ≈ 282,800 J per m². That's the energy in every square metre of sea surface the wave covers.

What that energy does

  • Impact: a falling lip and the whitewater behind it hit with far more force than "a bit bigger" suggests.
  • Distance: the energy pushes surfers and boards a long way underwater and towards the rocks.
  • Equipment: boards, leashes and rescue sleds all have to survive it.

CH 3

Long-period swell from distant storms

Giant surf starts as a storm far out at sea, days before it reaches the coast.

Big-wave days need powerful, long-period swell. In deep water, linear theory gives wavelength L = gT² ÷ 2π and crest speed c = gT ÷ 2π; the energy travels at the group speed, half of c. Longer periods mean longer, faster waves that reach deeper into the sea and feel the seabed sooner.

Worked example · deep-water swell by period
PeriodWavelengthCrest speedEnergy travels at1,000 km takes
12 s≈ 225 m18.7 m/s33.7 km/h≈ 30 h
16 s≈ 400 m25.0 m/s45.0 km/h≈ 22 h
20 s≈ 625 m31.2 m/s56.2 km/h≈ 18 h

T = 16 s: L = 9.81 × 256 ÷ 6.283 ≈ 400 m; c ≈ 25.0 m/s; group speed 12.5 m/s = 45 km/h; 1,000 km ÷ 45 km/h ≈ 22 h.

Reading a big-wave forecast

  • Period matters: long-period swell carries more power for the same height and travels faster.
  • Direction matters: a spot only "turns on" when swell arrives from the angle its seabed focuses (Ch 4).
  • Calls are made days ahead: the WSL issues a Yellow Alert up to 72 hours before a possible Nazaré event day, then a Green Alert when it's on within 24 hours.
An 18-second swell: how long are the waves in deep water?

L ≈ 1.56 × 18² ≈ 506 m.

Physics: linear wave theory. Event calls: WSL: TUDOR Nazaré Big Wave Challenge

CH 4

Seabed shape: why some spots grow giants

Waves bend towards shallow water. Shape the seabed right and it focuses a swell onto one stretch of coast.

Once a wave feels the bottom, its speed depends on depth: slower in shallow water, faster in deep. Where part of a crest runs over deeper water it gets ahead, and the crest swings round towards the shallower side: refraction, the same bending that light does in a lens. At Nazaré, Portugal's Instituto Hidrográfico describes a submarine canyon whose head, a few hundred metres off the beach, reaches about 150 m deep while the seabed beside it is no more than 20 m, and attributes the wave amplification near the coast to refraction caused by the canyon combined with the nearshore dynamics near its head.

Worked example · wave speed over different depths (full linear dispersion relation)
Depth16 s swell18 s swell
20 m (shelf beside the canyon)≈ 13.3 m/s≈ 13.4 m/s
50 m≈ 19.2 m/s≈ 19.8 m/s
150 m (canyon head)≈ 24.6 m/s≈ 27.0 m/s

Solved from ω² = g k tanh(k d), with ω = 2π ÷ T. For an 18 s swell the same crest runs about twice as fast over the canyon as over the shelf beside it, which bends it sharply.

What refraction does

  • Focusing: energy that was spread along a long crest gets squeezed onto a shorter stretch, so the waves there grow.
  • Direction-sensitive: only swell from certain angles is focused, which is why forecasters watch direction as closely as size.
  • Shoaling: as the wave reaches shallow water it slows and grows taller before breaking (Ch 5).
In shallow water, wave speed is about √(g × depth). How fast is a wave over 10 m of water?

√(9.81 × 10) ≈ 9.9 m/s (about 36 km/h).

Sources: Instituto Hidrográfico: Hidromar 113 (Portuguese, PDF). Physics: linear wave dispersion relation.

CH 5

Tow-in: matching the wave's speed

A breaking giant moves at motorway speed. To catch one, many big-wave surfers get towed in behind a jet ski.

A wave breaks roughly when its height reaches about 0.78 times the water depth (a beach rule of thumb), and its speed there is about c ≈ √(g d). The bigger the wave, the deeper it breaks and the faster it moves. In tow-in surfing a jet ski driver pulls the surfer up to speed and lets go on the wave. The WSL's TUDOR Nazaré Big Wave Challenge is a one-day tow-in event, run only when the swell is 25 feet or more; the WSL says every team member must be able to tow in and rescue their partner.

Worked example · speed of a breaking wave (rule of thumb H ≈ 0.78 d)
Wave heightBreaks in aboutWave speed thereDropping the full face, no friction
3 m3.8 m≈ 22 km/h≈ 28 km/h
10 m12.8 m≈ 40 km/h≈ 50 km/h
15 m19.2 m≈ 49 km/h≈ 62 km/h
20 m25.6 m≈ 57 km/h≈ 71 km/h

15 m: d ≈ 15 ÷ 0.78 ≈ 19.2 m; √(9.81 × 19.2) ≈ 13.7 m/s ≈ 49 km/h. Drop: √(2 × 9.81 × 15) ≈ 17.2 m/s ≈ 62 km/h, an upper bound because water drag and the moving face cut it.

How the team works

  • Driver and surfer: in the WSL tow-in format, each team pairs riders with a jet ski driver.
  • Speed and timing: the driver matches the swell, the surfer lets go at the right moment, and the driver circles back to pick them up.
  • Rescue is part of the job: Jet Resgate's tow-in course in Nazaré covers waves and oceanography, towing and piloting technique, and rescue and basic life support.
A wave breaks in 16 m of water. Roughly how fast is it moving?

√(9.81 × 16) ≈ 12.5 m/s ≈ 45 km/h.

Sources: WSL: TUDOR Nazaré Big Wave Challenge format · Jet Resgate courses

CH 6

Wipeouts, foam & rescue

Whitewater is part air, and air doesn't hold you up.

Archimedes: the upward push on you equals the weight of the water you displace. Breaking waves churn air into the water, so the mixture is less dense, and anything floating in it, you, a board or a flotation vest, gets less lift. lift ∝ density of the water–air mix.

Worked example · lift from a float that gives 10 kg of lift in solid seawater
Air in the water (by volume)Mix densityLift from the same float
0%1,025 kg/m³10 kg
20%≈ 820 kg/m³≈ 8 kg
40%≈ 615 kg/m³≈ 6 kg

Mix density ≈ (1 − air fraction) × 1,025. Lift scales the same way: 10 × 0.6 = 6 kg at 40% air. The air fractions are examples to show the effect, not measurements.

Rescue standards

  • ISA/ILS water safety: the ISA and the International Life Saving Federation run a two-day surf rescue course with a run-swim-run fitness test (100 m run, at least 100 m swim through the surf, 100 m run, within five minutes), an unconscious board rescue, first aid and CPR. The accreditation lasts two years.
  • Jet ski rescue: Jet Resgate's Nazaré course trains rescue with jet skis and basic life support, with up to 10 people per course.
  • Team first: the WSL says safety is paramount and every big-wave team member must be able to rescue their partner.

Safety Big waves are a team sport

No one should surf big waves without a trained water-safety team, jet ski support and a plan for getting out. Learn the basics of surf safety first on Kinetic's surfing page.

Water with 30% air: how much lift does a 10 kg float give?

About 10 × 0.7 = 7 kg.

Sources: ISA courses: ISA/ILS water safety · Jet Resgate courses · WSL. Physics: Archimedes' principle.

04 · Governing bodies

Who sets the rules

Surfing's world federation, the professional tour that runs big-wave events and the world lifesaving federation that co-runs surf rescue training. We checked each on its own official website.

Global

World federation

International Surfing Association (ISA)

Recognised by the International Olympic Committee as surfing's world governing authority. Runs coaching and instructing courses and, with the ILS, a surf rescue and water-safety accreditation.

isasurf.org
Professional tour

World Surf League (WSL)

Runs the TUDOR Nazaré Big Wave Challenge, a one-day tow-in event, and verified the 26.21 m Guinness record as part of the Red Bull Big Wave Awards.
