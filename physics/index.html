CH 1

Forces & weight

A kilogram is how much; a newton is how hard.

A force is a push or a pull, counted in newtons (N); one newton is the weight of about 102 g, because 1 ÷ 9.81 = 0.102 kg. Your weight is gravity's pull on your mass, W = m × g, with g = 9.81 m/s², and a "g" in a sport page just means a force counted in multiples of your own weight.

Worked example · a 70 kg person
StepWorkingResult
1. Weight70 kg × 9.81 m/s²≈ 686.7 N, call it 687 N
2. That force in g687 N ÷ 687 N1 g, by definition
Worked example · a banked turn (100 kg pilot and wing, from the paragliding page)

Weight = 100 × 9.81 = 981 N. In a steady bank only the upward part of the lift, lift × cos(bank), holds you up, so lift = weight ÷ cos(bank) and the load factor is 1 ÷ cos(bank).

How each sport bends it

Same idea, different specifics. Each line is a figure from that sport's own page; follow the chip for the whole chapter.

  • Paragliding · Ch 5A 60° bank is 2 g, so the wing must carry twice your weight.
  • Bungee & zipline · Ch 3A 70 kg jumper on a 20 m cord of 100 N/m feels a peak of about 3.6 × body weight.
  • Rock climbing · Ch 3A fall factor of 0.35 loads the rope with about 4.2 kN for a 70 kg climber.
  • Ice skating · Ch 2At 4 m/s on a 5 m radius the blade takes about 1.05 × body weight.
🦵
What your body does about itYour skeleton and muscles carry that weight all day, and sport multiplies it. Strong legs, hips and trunk are what hold a 2 g turn or a landing together, which is why the starter routine on Get fit is mostly leg and core work (WHO and ICMR-NIN guidance are cited there).
A 60 kg person carries a 10 kg pack. What is the total weight in newtons?

Mass = 70 kg, so weight = 70 × 9.81 = ≈ 687 N. Gear counts: it is the all-up mass that gravity pulls on.

At 45° of bank, what load factor does a wing carry, and what does 100 kg weigh at that moment?

1 ÷ cos 45° = 1 ÷ 0.707 = 1.41 g, so the wing carries about 1.41 × 981 = 1,387 N, which feels like 141 kg.

Sources: NIST: standard acceleration of gravity · Newton's second law

CH 2

Energy & work

Climbing is lifting yourself, and lifting is paid in joules.

Work is force × distance and energy is the ability to do work, both counted in joules (J), with 1 kcal = 4,184 J. Lifting a mass m through a height h takes W = m × g × h, and dropping through the same height gives it back as speed, ½ m v² = m g h.

Worked example · a 70 kg climber gains 1,000 m (from the mountaineering page)
StepWorkingResult
1. Lifting work70 kg × 9.81 m/s² × 1,000 m686,700 J ≈ 687 kJ
2. Food energy at 20% efficiency (illustrative)687 kJ ÷ 0.20≈ 3,435 kJ
3. In kilocalories3,435,000 J ÷ 4,184 J/kcal≈ 821 kcal

Carrying a 15 kg pack makes it 85 kg: 85 × 9.81 × 1,000 = 833,850 J ≈ 834 kJ, then ÷ 0.20 = 4,169 kJ, then ÷ 4,184 = ≈ 996 kcal. The 20% is an illustrative figure used on the mountaineering page; the exact value varies from person to person.

How each sport bends it

Same idea, different specifics. Each line is a figure from that sport's own page; follow the chip for the whole chapter.

  • Mountaineering · Ch 4A 70 kg climber gaining 1,000 m does about 687 kJ of lifting work, roughly 821 kcal of food.
  • Archery · Ch 1A 40 lb bow stores about 43.5 J; about 32.6 J reaches a 20 g arrow, leaving at about 57 m/s.
  • Big-wave surfing · Ch 2Wave energy grows with height squared: a 15 m face carries about 25 × the energy of a 3 m one.
  • Rock climbing · Ch 6Lifting a 70 kg climber 20 m is about 13.7 kJ of mechanical work, about 3.3 kcal.
  • Skydiving · Ch 3At 53.6 m/s a 90 kg jumper carries about 129 kJ of kinetic energy.
🍚
What your body does about itYour body is the engine, and muscles turn only a fraction of the food energy they burn into lifting (the 20% above). Long days need real fuel, fluids and sleep; the numbers and sources are in Food & sleep on Get fit.
A 60 kg hiker with a 10 kg pack climbs 500 m. How much lifting work is that?

70 × 9.81 × 500 = 343,350 J ≈ 343 kJ. At the same illustrative 20%, that is 343 ÷ 0.20 ÷ 4.184 ≈ 410 kcal of food.

A 20 g (0.020 kg) arrow leaves the bow at 57.1 m/s. What is its kinetic energy?

½ × 0.020 × 57.1² = 0.010 × 3,260 ≈ 32.6 J, which matches the archery page's figure.

Sources: NIST SP 811: guide for the use of the SI · work

CH 3

Pressure & buoyancy

Water piles up on you; thin air thins out each breath.

Pressure is force per area, counted in pascals (1 Pa = 1 N/m²; 1 bar ≈ 100,000 Pa). In water it grows with depth, in air it falls with height, a gas squeezes in step (Boyle's law: P × V = constant), and anything submerged is pushed up by the weight of the water it displaces.

Worked example · 10 m of seawater, and a 6 L balloon going down
StepWorkingResult
1. Mass of water above 1 m²10 m³ × 1,025 kg/m³10,250 kg
2. Its weight, spread over 1 m²10,250 kg × 9.81 m/s²≈ 100,550 Pa ≈ 1 bar

The scuba page rounds this to 100,500 Pa (about 1 bar); fresh water is about 10.2 m per bar, because 100,000 ÷ (1,000 × 9.81) ≈ 10.2.

👂
What your body does about itAir spaces in your body (ears, sinuses, lungs) feel pressure first, so equalise early and often; the scuba ears chapter shows why. At altitude the body copes mainly by breathing more, and the mountaineering page (citing CDC) says the first phase takes about 3 to 5 days and that being fit does not change your risk.
A 4 L pocket of air at the surface is taken to 20 m. What volume is it there?

At 20 m the pressure is 1 + 2 = 3 bar, so V = 4 × 1 ÷ 3 ≈ 1.33 L.

What is the absolute pressure at 30 m in seawater, and what has happened to the gas in your lungs?

1 bar of air + 3 bar of water = 4 bar, so a breath takes a quarter of the surface volume (6 L → 1.5 L).

Sources: fluids, density and pressure · Archimedes' principle and buoyancy · molecular model of an ideal gas · NASA Glenn: Boyle's law · NASA Glenn: Earth atmosphere model · NIST: standard atmosphere

CH 4

Drag & friction

Go twice as fast and the air or water pushes back four times as hard.

Drag is the fluid's push against your motion, D = ½ × ρ × CdA × v²: it grows with the square of speed, and the power to beat it (drag × speed) with the cube. Sliding friction is simpler, F = μ × m × g on level ground, so on a very slippery surface (small μ) there is almost nothing to push against.

Worked example · a skydiver's top speed (90 kg, belly-down, from the skydiving page)

Terminal speed is where drag equals weight: ½ ρ CdA v² = m g, so v = √(2 m g ÷ (ρ CdA)). Air density ρ = 1.1 kg/m³, CdA = 0.56 m².

1. Weight90 × 9.81882.9 N
2. 2 × weight2 × 882.91,765.8 N

5. Square root√2,866.6≈ 53.5 m/s
6. In km/h53.5 × 3.6≈ 193 km/h

The skydiving page shows 53.6 m/s from the same inputs; the 0.1 difference is rounding. Head-down, CdA is 0.31 m² and the page gets about 257 km/h.

🚴
What your body does about itYou are the adjustable part. Changing your position changes CdA, and the sport pages show how much: the same 200 W gives about 27.9 km/h sitting upright and about 33.9 km/h on the hoods (cycling page). Holding a low, steady position is a trained skill, so build it in lessons rather than on the day.
In air, you double your speed. By what factor do drag and the power to beat it change?

Drag × 2² = × 4; power × 2³ = × 8.

How much friction acts on an 80 kg skater on level ice with μ ≈ 0.005?

0.005 × 80 × 9.81 = ≈ 3.9 N, about the weight of 400 g.

CH 5

Impacts & landing

It is not the fall that hurts; it is how quickly you stop.

A fall from height h reaches the ground at v = √(2 × g × h) whatever you land on. What you control is the stopping distance d: the average force is weight × (1 + h ÷ d), so doubling d halves the extra load.

Worked example · a 0.5 m drop, stiff legs against bent knees (from Get fit)
StepWorkingResult
1. Speed at the ground√(2 × 9.81 × 0.5) = √9.81≈ 3.13 m/s ≈ 11.3 km/h
2. Stop time, steady slowdown: t = 2d ÷ vstiff, 5 cm: 2 × 0.05 ÷ 3.13; bent, 25 cm: 2 × 0.25 ÷ 3.13≈ 0.032 s against ≈ 0.160 s
3. Average force ÷ weight: 1 + h ÷ d1 + 0.5 ÷ 0.05; 1 + 0.5 ÷ 0.2511 × against 3 ×
4. For a 70 kg person (weight 687 N)11 × 687; 3 × 687≈ 7,550 N against ≈ 2,060 N

Steps 1 and 3 are on Get fit; steps 2 and 4 are the same numbers carried one step further. The "1 +" is your own weight, which the ground must also hold up.

How each sport bends it

What your body does about itYour legs are the shock absorber. Bending the knees turns a 5 cm stop into a 25 cm one, and on Get fit that is the difference between about 11 × and about 3 × your weight (Your body in the physics). Strong, practised legs make that automatic.
You drop 3 m and stop over 0.6 m. What is the slowdown in g?

h ÷ d = 3 ÷ 0.6 = 5 g, as on the rock-climbing page. (Add 1 g for the force that also holds you up.)

Same 0.5 m drop, but you stop over 10 cm. What is the average force in multiples of your weight?

1 + 0.5 ÷ 0.10 = 6 ×, between the 11 × (5 cm) and 3 × (25 cm) cases.

CH 6

Balance & turning

To go round a bend, something has to push you towards the middle.

Turning needs a sideways push towards the centre of the curve, F = m × v² ÷ r, which grows with the square of speed and as the turn gets tighter. You lean so that this push passes through your centre of mass, tan(lean) = v² ÷ (r × g), and the ground can supply at most μ × m × g.

Worked example · a horse at a canter (5 m/s) on a 10 m circle, then the grip limit
StepWorkingResult
1. v²5 × 525
2. r × g10 × 9.8198.1
3. tan(lean)25 ÷ 98.1 = 0.255lean ≈ 14.3°

🧘
What your body does about itBalance is a skill your body trains. The Get fit self-check includes a tree-pose test, 10 seconds on each leg (from the Fit India protocol), and the routine there builds the leg and trunk control that keeps you under your own centre of mass; see Get fit.
3 m/s on a 10 m radius: what lean angle?

v² = 9 and r g = 98.1, so tan(lean) = 0.0917 and the lean is ≈ 5.2°, as on the horse-riding page.

A skater holds 15 km/h (4.17 m/s) on a 5 m radius. What lean is needed?

v² = 17.4, r g = 49.05, so tan(lean) = 0.354 and the lean is ≈ 19.5°, matching the skating page.
