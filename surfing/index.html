CH 1

Swell: how waves travel

The wave you ride may have started as a storm days ago and a thousand kilometres away.

Wind blowing over the sea makes waves. Far from the storm, they sort themselves into regular swell, described by its period T (seconds between crests) and height. Water itself barely travels: it moves round in circles as the wave's energy passes through. In deep water (deeper than about half a wavelength), linear wave theory gives L = g T² ÷ 2π ≈ 1.56 T² metres and crest speed c = g T ÷ 2π ≈ 1.56 T m/s. The energy moves at the group speed, half of c.

Worked example · deep-water swell by period
PeriodWavelengthCrest speedEnergy speedTime to travel 1,000 km
6 s (wind swell)≈ 56 m9.4 m/s16.9 km/h≈ 59 h
8 s≈ 100 m12.5 m/s22.5 km/h≈ 44 h
12 s (groundswell)≈ 225 m18.7 m/s33.7 km/h≈ 30 h
16 s≈ 400 m25.0 m/s45.0 km/h≈ 22 h

T = 8 s: L = 9.81 × 64 ÷ 6.283 ≈ 99.9 m; c = 9.81 × 8 ÷ 6.283 ≈ 12.5 m/s; group speed ≈ 6.25 m/s; 1,000,000 m ÷ 6.25 m/s ≈ 160,000 s ≈ 44.5 h.

Reading a forecast

  • Period matters as much as height: a 1 m, 12 s swell breaks with far more push than a 1 m, 6 s wind chop.
  • Long-period swell arrives first: because longer waves travel faster, the first sign of a distant storm is a long-period "forerunner".
  • Wind direction: light offshore wind (blowing from land to sea) grooms wave faces; onshore wind makes them crumbly and messy.
✋
Feel the numberCount the seconds between waves arriving at the beach: that's the period. Multiply it by itself and by 1.56, and you have roughly how far apart the crests were out at sea.
A 10-second swell: how long are the waves in deep water, and how fast does the energy travel?

L ≈ 1.56 × 100 = 156 m. Crest speed ≈ 15.6 m/s, so the energy travels at about 7.8 m/s (28 km/h).

CH 2

Shoaling & breaking

A wave breaks when the sea floor trips it up.

Once the depth is less than about a twentieth of the wavelength, the wave "feels" the bottom and its speed becomes c ≈ √(g d), where d is the depth. As it slows, the same energy is squeezed into a shorter, taller wave: shoaling. When the wave gets too tall for the depth, the crest outruns the base and spills or pitches forward. A widely used rule of thumb says this happens when the height is about H ≈ 0.78 d.

Worked example · where waves break (rule of thumb H ≈ 0.78 d)
Wave heightBreaking depth ≈ H ÷ 0.78Wave speed there ≈ √(g d)
0.5 m≈ 0.64 m≈ 2.5 m/s
1.0 m≈ 1.28 m≈ 3.5 m/s
1.5 m≈ 1.92 m≈ 4.3 m/s
2.0 m≈ 2.56 m≈ 5.0 m/s

1 m wave: d = 1 ÷ 0.78 ≈ 1.28 m; √(9.81 × 1.28) ≈ 3.5 m/s. Real breaking depth varies with beach slope and wave steepness; treat 0.78 as a classroom approximation.

Beaches, reefs and points

  • Beach breaks break over sandbars that shift with the seasons. Gentle slopes make soft, spilling waves: ideal for learning. Most Indian surf schools teach on beach breaks.
  • Reef and point breaks break over rock or coral in the same place each time, often more powerfully and over shallow, hard bottoms.
  • Steeper bottoms make plunging waves that pitch forward and can slam you down. That shallow impact zone is why you fall flat, never dive head-first, and protect your head as you surface.

Know the bottom before you paddle out

Ask your instructor what's under the waves: sand, rock or reef, and how the tide changes it. A spot that's friendly at high tide can be shallow and dangerous at low tide.

Waves are breaking in water about 2 m deep. Roughly how big are they?

H ≈ 0.78 × 2 ≈ 1.6 m, about head-high on many adults.

CH 3

Buoyancy & board volume

Every litre of board holds up about a kilogram. Beginners need lots of litres.

Archimedes' principle: anything in water is pushed up by a force equal to the weight of the water it displaces. Seawater has a density of about 1.025 kg per litre, so each litre of board volume can support about 1 kg when pushed fully under. A board that floats you high on the surface paddles faster and wobbles less.

Worked example · maximum buoyant support in seawater
BoardVolumeWeight of displaced seawater if fully submerged
Performance shortboard≈ 30 L≈ 31 kg
Fun board / mini-mal≈ 50 L≈ 51 kg
Big foam learner board≈ 80 L≈ 82 kg

80 L × 1.025 kg/L ≈ 82 kg. A 70 kg surfer's body also floats a little in seawater, so the board doesn't carry their whole weight, but the more volume to spare, the higher and steadier it sits. The board volumes here are typical examples, not a sizing guide; ask your school.

Why learner boards look the way they do

  • Length and width: a long, wide board is stable side-to-side and glides into small waves early.
  • Soft-top: a foam deck and flexible fins hurt far less when you fall on the board or it hits you. Kallialay Surf School, for example, provides soft-top boards for lessons.
  • Wax and rash vests: wax gives grip on hard decks; a rash vest protects skin from the deck and the sun.
✋
Feel the numberA 1-litre water bottle full of air takes a surprising push to hold under in a pool: about 1 kg-force. Your learner board is like 80 of them.
Roughly how much support could a 40 L board give if pushed fully under seawater?

40 × 1.025 ≈ 41 kg. That's why advanced surfers on small boards sit low in the water and duck-dive easily.

CH 4

Paddling, catching & riding

Surfing is falling down a hill that keeps moving towards the beach.

To catch a wave you need to be moving towards the beach as it lifts the back of your board. Once you're on the sloping face, part of gravity, g sin θ for a face at angle θ, pulls you down the slope while the wave keeps rebuilding the slope underneath you. The board starts to plane, skimming on top of the water instead of pushing through it.

Worked example · speed from dropping down the face (no friction)
Drop down the facev = √(2 g h)In km/h
0.5 m3.1 m/s≈ 11 km/h
1.0 m4.4 m/s≈ 16 km/h
1.5 m5.4 m/s≈ 20 km/h

1.5 m: √(2 × 9.81 × 1.5) ≈ 5.42 m/s × 3.6 ≈ 19.5 km/h. Friction and drag reduce this, but the wave keeps feeding you energy as long as you stay on the steep part of the face.

Where the physics shows up in a lesson

  • Whitewater first: broken waves (foam) push you towards the beach with no need to match a wave's speed. Most schools start you here.
  • Paddle position: too far back and the nose lifts and drags; too far forward and the nose digs in ("pearling"). Find the spot where the board lies flat.
  • Pop-up: hands under your chest, one smooth move to your feet, knees bent, weight centred. A low centre of mass is more stable.
  • Turning: leaning puts the board on its rail; the water's push on the tilted board and fins curves your path, like a bicycle leaning into a bend.
✋
Feel the numberA 1 m drop gives you about 16 km/h: a brisk cycling pace, on a board, on water.
You drop 0.8 m down a face. Ignoring friction, what speed do you gain?

√(2 × 9.81 × 0.8) ≈ 4.0 m/s (about 14 km/h).

CH 5

Rip currents

The calm-looking gap between breaking waves is often the most dangerous water on the beach.

Breaking waves carry water towards the shore. It has to return to sea, and it does so in narrow, fast channels: rip currents. They form in gaps between sandbars and along piers, groynes and rocks. The US National Weather Service says channel rips typically flow at about 1–2 feet per second (0.3–0.6 m/s) and can reach 8 feet per second (about 2.4 m/s), which NOAA notes is faster than an Olympic swimmer.

Worked example · why you swim sideways, not straight in
SituationYour speed through the waterRip speedResult
Swim straight at the beach1.0 m/s1.5 m/sYou go backwards at 0.5 m/s and tire fast
Swim parallel across a 30 m-wide rip1.0 m/s1.5 m/sOut of the rip in ≈ 30 s, carried ≈ 45 m seaward on the way
Typical rip, standing at waist depth—0.5 m/sHard to stand against, according to the NWS

Crossing time = 30 m ÷ 1.0 m/s = 30 s; seaward drift = 1.5 m/s × 30 s = 45 m. Once you're out of the channel, the breaking waves on either side help push you back in.

Spotting and escaping rips

  • Look for: a darker, calmer-looking gap in the line of breaking waves, foam or sediment drifting seaward, or water flowing out along rocks and piers.
  • If caught: don't fight it. Stay calm and float, keep hold of your board, signal for help, and paddle or swim parallel to the shore until you're free.
  • Swimming matters: Kovalam Surf Club asks for basic swimming skills; Kallialay Surf School asks group-lesson students to swim at least 50 m confidently without touching the ground.

Monsoon seas on the west coast

During the monsoon, much of India's west coast gets big, rough, unpredictable surf with strong currents. Learn in the calmer months, follow local warnings and lifeguards, and never surf alone in conditions you don't know.

A rip is 20 m wide and flows at 1 m/s. You swim across it at 0.8 m/s. How far out are you carried?

Crossing takes 20 ÷ 0.8 = 25 s; drift = 1 × 25 = 25 m seaward.

CH 6

Wave energy & impact

Twice the height, four times the energy. That's why "just a bit bigger" can be a lot more.

In linear wave theory, the energy in each square metre of sea is E = ⅛ ρ g H², and the power carried per metre of wave crest is P = ρ g² H² T ÷ 64π (ρ ≈ 1,025 kg/m³ for seawater). Energy grows with height squared, power also grows with period.

Worked example · wave power per metre of crest
HeightPeriodEnergy per m² of seaPower per m of crest
0.5 m6 s≈ 0.31 kJ≈ 0.7 kW
1 m8 s≈ 1.26 kJ≈ 3.9 kW
2 m12 s≈ 5.0 kJ≈ 23.5 kW

1 m, 8 s: P = 1025 × 9.81² × 1 × 8 ÷ (64π) ≈ 3,920 W/m. E = 1025 × 9.81 × 1 ÷ 8 ≈ 1,257 J per m².

Staying safe with all that energy

  • Leash on, board under control: a loose board pushed by whitewater can hit you or someone else hard. Keep it between you and the wave, never between you and other people.
  • Wipeouts: relax, protect your head with your arms as you surface, and wait for the turbulence to pass.
  • Etiquette is safety: one surfer per wave (the one closest to the breaking part has priority), don't drop in, and paddle around the line-up, not through it.
  • Sun and heat: India's water is warm, but the sun is strong: rash vest, reef-safe sunscreen and water.
✋
Feel the numberA modest 1 m, 8-second swell delivers about 4 kW per metre of beach, like a row of electric kettles every metre along the shore, all day long.
The swell grows from 1 m to 1.5 m at the same period. How much more power?

1.5² = 2.25, so about 2.25 times (≈ 8.8 kW/m at 8 s).

03 · The surf ladder

04 · Governing bodies

Who sets the rules

Surfing's rules come from a world federation recognised by the Olympic movement, a professional tour, and India's national federation and its state associations. We checked each on its own official website.

Global

World federation

International Surfing Association (ISA)

Surfing's world governing authority, as recognised by the International Olympic Committee. Runs the World Surfing Games and other world championships, and the Coaching and Instructing Program (Surf Level 1 Instructor, Level 2 Coach, judging), with an ISA/ILS water-safety course for instructors.

isasurf.org
Professional tour

World Surf League (WSL)

Runs the professional Championship Tour, Challenger Series, Qualifying Series and Junior Tour. Its Shore Temple Classic QS 2000 is held in India with SFI.

worldsurfleague.com

India

National federation

Surfing Federation of India (SFI)

The recognised national governing body for surfing and stand-up paddle (SUP) in India: recognised by the ISA, the Asia Surfing Federation and the Ministry of Youth Affairs and Sports. Accredits surf and SUP schools and runs national contests and ISA courses.

surfingfederationofindia.org
State associations

SFI state associations

SFI lists state associations including the Tamil Nadu Surfing Association (founded 2023), the Karnataka Surfing Association and Surfing Kerala (both founded 2025), with others under review.

surfingfederationofindia.org/accreditation
