CH 1

Running economy & energy cost

A kilometre costs about the same whether you jog it or race it. Racing just pays faster.

A long-standing rule of thumb from exercise physiology: running on the flat costs about 1 kcal per kilogram of body mass per kilometre (≈ 4.2 kJ/kg/km), and the figure changes surprisingly little with speed. In oxygen terms, that's about 200 mL of oxygen per kg per km; each litre of oxygen you use releases about 5 kcal from food, so the two rules agree. Individual runners vary by roughly ±10–20%: that spread is running economy.

Worked example · energy for our 70 kg reference runner
RaceDistanceEnergy (≈ 1 kcal/kg/km)Oxygen used
5K5 km≈ 350 kcal≈ 70 L
10K10 km≈ 700 kcal≈ 140 L
Half marathon21.1 km≈ 1,480 kcal≈ 295 L
Marathon42.2 km≈ 2,950 kcal≈ 590 L

Oxygen = 200 mL × 70 kg × distance. For the marathon: 0.2 × 70 × 42.2 ≈ 590 litres of oxygen, about 2,800 litres of air's worth of oxygen at sea level, all drawn in through your lungs.

What changes your economy

  • Training: years of running, plus strength and plyometric work, generally improve economy.
  • Technique and springiness: tendons such as the Achilles store and return elastic energy each stride (Ch 2).
  • Shoes: lighter shoes cost less to swing, and studies of some modern foam-and-plate racing shoes report a few per cent better economy for many runners. The size of the effect varies by person.
  • Hills, wind and surface: uphill costs extra; trails and sand cost more than smooth roads; a headwind adds air drag, which is small for most runners at everyday paces but grows with speed squared.

CH 2

Ground forces, cadence & stride

Running is a series of short collisions. The shorter the contact, the harder the hit.

Walking always keeps one foot on the ground. Running has a flight phase, when both feet are off it. Over a whole stride you don't accelerate up or down on average, so the ground's average push must equal your weight (Newton's second law). But the ground only gets to push while your foot is down, so during contact the force has to be much larger.

Worked example · cadence 170 steps/min, so each step takes 60 ÷ 170 ≈ 0.353 s
Ground contact timeAverage force during contactPeak force (half-sine shape)For 70 kg (687 N)
0.30 s (easy jog)≈ 1.18 × BW≈ 1.85 × BW≈ 1,270 N
0.25 s≈ 1.41 × BW≈ 2.22 × BW≈ 1,530 N
0.20 s (faster running)≈ 1.76 × BW≈ 2.77 × BW≈ 1,900 N

Average during contact = body weight × step time ÷ contact time. A force curve shaped like half a sine wave peaks at π/2 ≈ 1.57 times its average. This simple model lands right in the 2–3 × body weight range measured in running studies (walking is about 1.2 ×).

Springs in your legs

When your foot lands, tendons (especially the Achilles) and the arch of the foot stretch and store elastic energy, then release much of it as you push off, like a bouncing ball. That's a big part of why running is cheaper than its forces suggest. Fatigue, very stiff or very soft surfaces, and long downhills change how well this works.

Cadence and step length

Speed = cadence × step length. At 5:00/km (200 m per minute) and 170 steps per minute, each step is about 1.18 m: about 36,000 steps in a marathon. Many coaches suggest a slightly quicker cadence for runners who overstride (landing with the foot far ahead of the body), and studies find that modest cadence increases reduce loading at the knee and hip. There's no single "correct" footstrike: changing how you land mostly shifts load between the knee and hip and the calf and Achilles, so change gradually.

Downhill bites later

Running downhill makes the thigh muscles brake each step while lengthening (eccentric contractions). That's what causes the deep soreness after hilly races, including long mountain descents such as the drop from Khardung La. Train on descents before a hilly race.

You speed up to 4:30/km keeping 170 steps per minute. What's your new step length?

4:30/km = 1,000 m ÷ 4.5 min ≈ 222 m/min. Step length = 222 ÷ 170 ≈ 1.31 m.

CH 3

VO₂ max, thresholds & pacing

Your aerobic ceiling sets the limit. Economy and pacing decide how close you live to it.

VO₂ max is the most oxygen your body can take up and use per minute, usually in mL per kg per minute. It depends on how much blood your heart pumps and how well your muscles extract oxygen. Many untrained adults are in the 30s to 40s; elite endurance runners can exceed 70. Training raises it, especially in the first months; age gradually lowers it.

The oxygen a pace needs is simply economy × speed. With 200 mL/kg/km:

Worked example · oxygen needed vs a VO₂ max of 45 (sea level)
PaceSpeedOxygen neededShare of VO₂ max 45
7:00 /km8.6 km/h≈ 28.6 mL/kg/min≈ 63%
6:00 /km10 km/h≈ 33.3≈ 74%
5:00 /km12 km/h40.0≈ 89%
4:00 /km15 km/h50.0≈ 111%: not sustainable

Thresholds: how close can you run to the ceiling?

As effort rises, there's a point where lactate starts building up in the blood faster than your body can clear it. Above this lactate threshold, fatigue accumulates quickly. Trained runners can hold a higher share of their VO₂ max before they reach it, so they can race longer at a higher percentage. Very roughly, short races are run near the top of the range and marathons well below it.

Pacing

Because oxygen cost rises with speed and anything above threshold accumulates fatigue, an even pace (or a slightly faster second half) is usually the most economical way to race. Starting too fast borrows from later and spends carbohydrate quickly (Ch 4). Heat and altitude lower the pace you can hold at the same effort (Ch 5, Ch 6).

A runner with a VO₂ max of 52 races at 5:00/km. What share of their ceiling is that?

Need = 200 × (1 ÷ 5) = 40 mL/kg/min. 40 ÷ 52 ≈ 77%.

CH 4

Energy systems & the wall

Three engines, one tank that runs low, and a wall at around 30 km.

Muscles run on ATP, and they only hold a few seconds' worth, so three systems keep refilling it:

The three energy systems (approximate time scales)
SystemFuelDominatesRunning example
PhosphocreatineStored creatine phosphatefirst ~10 sA sprint to the finish line
Anaerobic glycolysisGlycogen/glucose, without oxygen~10 s to ~2 minA 400 m or 800 m race
Aerobic metabolismCarbohydrate and fat, with oxygenbeyond ~2 minEverything from 5K to ultras

The systems overlap; none switches off. But a 5K is already mostly aerobic, and a marathon is overwhelmingly so.

Why the wall arrives

A well-fed runner stores very roughly 400–500 g of glycogen in muscle and about 100 g in the liver: around 2,000 kcal. A 70 kg runner's marathon costs about 2,950 kcal (Ch 1), and at race pace a large share of it comes from carbohydrate. Fat stores are enormous, but fat releases energy more slowly and needs more oxygen per kcal. When glycogen runs low, often around 30–35 km, the pace you can hold drops sharply: the wall.

Two levers delay it: start with full stores (carbohydrate-rich meals in the days before), and take carbohydrate during the race. Sports-nutrition guidance commonly suggests about 30–60 g of carbohydrate per hour for long races, and up to about 90 g per hour for some athletes using mixed sugars. Practise race-day fuelling in training: guts adapt too.

You start with 2,000 kcal of glycogen and burn carbohydrate at 600 kcal/h at marathon pace. Without eating, roughly when do you run low?

2,000 ÷ 600 ≈ 3.3 hours. For a 4-hour marathoner, that's around the 33–35 km mark: right where the wall is reported. 45 g of carbohydrate an hour (≈ 180 kcal) pushes it well past the finish.

CH 5

Altitude: thin air, acclimatisation & AMS

The air is still 21% oxygen. There's just less air.

What drives oxygen into your blood is its partial pressure: about 21% of the total air pressure. Total pressure falls with height, so every breath carries fewer oxygen molecules, even though the percentage hasn't changed.

Worked example · International Standard Atmosphere
PlaceAltitudeAir pressurevs sea levelOxygen partial pressure
Mumbaisea level101.3 kPa100%21.2 kPa
(for comparison)2,000 m79.5 kPa≈ 78%16.7 kPa
Leh≈ 3,505 m (11,500 ft)65.7 kPa≈ 65%13.8 kPa
Khardung La top≈ 5,370 m (17,618 ft)51.4 kPa≈ 51%10.8 kPa

The Ladakh Marathon organisers describe it simply: oxygen drops to about 50% at Khardung La top. Real pressure also varies with weather and season by a few per cent.

What your body does about it

Within hours you breathe faster and deeper, and your heart rate rises at any given effort. Over days, your kidneys rebalance your blood chemistry so you can keep breathing harder. Over weeks, your body makes more red blood cells. That's acclimatisation, and it takes time whatever your fitness: the Ladakh organisers stress it is "not determined by individual fitness levels". Even acclimatised, your aerobic ceiling is lower up high: as a rough rule of thumb, VO₂ max falls by several per cent per 1,000 m above about 1,000 m (the Lab uses 7%), so the same pace feels much harder.

The organisers also advise complete rest on day 1, going slow, staying hydrated, avoiding alcohol for the first few days, monitoring your oxygen saturation, and visiting SNM Hospital in Leh if you feel any signs of altitude sickness. Their page mentions that many people take Diamox (acetazolamide); it's a prescription medicine, so discuss it with your doctor.

Altitude sickness

Acute mountain sickness (AMS) usually begins within hours to a day of arriving high: headache with nausea, fatigue, dizziness or poor sleep. Mild AMS often settles with rest and no further ascent. Two rarer conditions are emergencies: HAPE (fluid in the lungs: breathlessness at rest, cough, falling exercise tolerance) and HACE (swelling of the brain: confusion, unsteadiness, drowsiness). Both need immediate descent and medical care. Flying straight into Leh puts you at 3,500 m in an hour, with no gradual climb, which is exactly why the organisers ask for 7–15 days on the ground first.

Using the Lab's rule of thumb, what's the VO₂ max at Leh of a runner who has 50 at sea level? And what share of it does a 6:00/km pace need?

50 × (1 − 0.07 × 2.505) ≈ 41.2. 6:00/km needs ≈ 33.3, so ≈ 81%, against 67% at sea level. Same pace, much harder.

CH 6

Heat, sweat & hydration

Most of the energy you burn becomes heat. Sweat only helps when it evaporates.

Muscles turn only around a fifth to a quarter of their energy into movement; the rest becomes heat. Our 70 kg runner at 10 km/h burns about 700 kcal per hour ≈ 810 W, so roughly 650 W of heat: like a small room heater inside you. Your body sheds it mainly by evaporating sweat, which takes about 2.4 MJ per kilogram.

Worked example · sweat needed if all of it evaporates (80% of energy as heat)
RunnerSpeedHeat producedSweat to evaporate
55 kg10 km/h≈ 510 W≈ 0.76 kg/h
70 kg10 km/h≈ 650 W≈ 0.96 kg/h
70 kg12 km/h≈ 780 W≈ 1.16 kg/h
85 kg12 km/h≈ 950 W≈ 1.40 kg/h

Heavier and faster runners make more heat. Sweat that drips off cools you very little, so real sweat losses in humid heat are often higher than these numbers.

Humidity is the real enemy

Evaporation needs dry air to carry vapour away. On a humid coastal morning, you sweat just as hard but cool less, so your core temperature and heart rate climb at the same pace. Indian city races start early in the cooler months for this reason: the Vedanta Delhi Half Marathon is in October and the Tata Mumbai Marathon in January. Heat adaptation (training gradually in warm conditions over a week or two) increases sweat rate and lowers heart rate in the heat.

Hydration: neither too little nor too much

Losing a lot of body water raises heart rate and body temperature. But drinking far more than you sweat, especially plain water over many hours, can dilute blood sodium (hyponatraemia), which is dangerous too. Weigh yourself before and after long training runs to learn your own sweat rate, and drink to a plan. At high altitude the air is cold and very dry, so you lose extra water just by breathing hard; the Ladakh organisers make hydration packs mandatory for ultra runners.

Heat illness is an emergency

Confusion, stopping sweating, collapse or a very high body temperature during a run can mean heat stroke. Stop, get into shade, cool the person rapidly, and call the race medical team or emergency services.

You weigh 70.0 kg before an hour's run and 68.9 kg after, having drunk 0.4 L. What was your sweat loss?

Mass lost 1.1 kg + 0.4 kg drunk = about 1.5 L of sweat per hour, higher than the "all evaporates" estimate, as expected when some sweat drips off.

What makes a course (and a time) official

Course measurement

AIMS–World Athletics measurement

AIMS and World Athletics accept only the calibrated bicycle method for measuring road races, by accredited Grade A or B measurers. For world records, a course may drop no more than 1 m per km and the start and finish may be no more than 50% of the distance apart. The Ladakh Marathon says it is AIMS-certified.

aims-worldrunning.org › measurement
Race labels

World Athletics Label road races

World Athletics grants labels to road races meeting its standards. The TCS World 10K Bengaluru describes itself as a World Athletics Gold Label Road Race.

worldathletics.org › label road races
Qualifying times abroad

Boston Marathon qualifying standards

A real example of a qualifying ladder: for the 2027 race, men 18–34 need 2:55:00 and women 3:25:00, with slower standards by age group. Times must come from a course certified by USATF, AIMS or a national governing body, and meeting the standard doesn't guarantee entry (the 2027 cut-off was 5 min 17 s faster than standard).

04 · Governing bodies

Who sets the rules

World Athletics governs athletics worldwide, including road running, and works with AIMS on course measurement. Trail running has its own points system through ITRA. In India, the Athletics Federation of India (AFI) is the national body. Every body below was checked on its official website.
