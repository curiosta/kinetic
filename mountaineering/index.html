Go high, slowly. Thin air, cold and gravity set the rules.

Mountains are physics you breathe: air pressure halving on the way up, temperature falling with every kilometre, every kilo in your pack lifted by your legs, and snow slopes balanced between holding and sliding. CH 1

Thin air: pressure & oxygen

The air up high is still 21% oxygen. There's just less of it in every breath.

Air pressure is the weight of the air above you, so it falls as you climb. The share of oxygen stays the same, 20.95%, but its partial pressure, the push that moves oxygen into your blood, falls in step with the total: PO₂ = 0.2095 × P. The International Standard Atmosphere gives a good average for the pressure: P = 101.325 × (1 − 2.256 × 10⁻⁵ × h)5.256 kPa, with h in metres. The CDC's travel health guide says that at about 3,050 m the oxygen pressure you breathe in is only 69% of the sea-level value, and that arriving there quickly can lower the oxygen saturation of your blood to 88–91%.

CH 2

Acclimatisation & altitude illness

Fitness doesn't protect you. Time does.

Your body adjusts to thin air (acclimatises) mainly by breathing more. The CDC says the crucial first phase takes about 3–5 days after each ascent, and that training and fitness don't change your risk. Go too high too fast and you can get acute mountain sickness (AMS): a headache like a hangover, with poor appetite, dizziness, tiredness or nausea, usually 2–12 hours after arriving higher. Two rarer forms can kill: HACE (fluid on the brain: confusion, stumbling, drowsiness) and HAPE (fluid in the lungs: breathlessness at rest, cough, falling performance). For both, descent is the key treatment.

The ascent rules the CDC quotes from the Wilderness Medical Society: don't go from low altitude to sleep above 2,750 m in one day; once above 3,000 m, raise your sleeping altitude by no more than 500 m a day; and add an extra night for every 1,000 m of sleeping altitude you gain. Climbing higher in the day and sleeping lower ("climb high, sleep low") is how courses like HMI's use acclimatisation walks.

2,000 m ÷ 500 m = 4 days of climbing, plus 2 extra nights. The CDC notes these rules can still be too fast for some people and too slow for others.

The three rules that prevent deaths

From the CDC: know the early symptoms and admit to them; never go up to sleep higher with any symptoms of altitude illness, however mild; and go down if symptoms get worse despite rest or treatment. AMS usually improves quickly after descending 300 m or more. Discuss any altitude medicine with a doctor before you go.

You sleep at 3,000 m tonight. Using the 500 m rule, which is the earliest night you can sleep at 4,000 m?

Night 2 at 3,500 m, night 3 at 4,000 m, then an extra night there.

CH 3

Cold, wind chill & frostbite

Every 1,000 m up is about 6.5 °C colder. Then the wind arrives.

Rising air expands and cools, so the standard atmosphere drops 6.5 °C for every 1,000 m. Wind then strips heat from bare skin faster. The US National Weather Service (NWS) wind chill index turns air temperature and wind into how cold it feels on your face; in metric: WC = 13.12 + 0.6215T − 11.37V0.16 + 0.3965T·V0.16, with T in °C and V in km/h. The NWS says the index is defined only at or below 10 °C (50 °F) with wind above about 5 km/h (3 mph), that bright sun can make it feel 10–18 °F (about 6–10 °C) warmer, and that frostbite needs the actual air temperature to be below freezing.

Worked example · 25 °C at sea level, at 5,000 m (illustrative, from our Altitude Lab)
WindAir temperatureFeels like
Calm−7.5 °C−7.5 °C (no wind chill)
10 km/h−7.5 °C−12 °C
30 km/h−7.5 °C−16 °C
50 km/h−7.5 °C−18 °C

25 − 6.5 × 5 = −7.5 °C. At 30 km/h, V0.16 ≈ 1.72, so WC ≈ 13.12 − 4.66 − 19.59 − 5.12 ≈ −16 °C. The first few km/h of wind make the biggest difference.

Layers trap still air, which insulates; wind and wet clothing destroy that. That's why institute kit lists, such as NIM's, ask for heavy woollen socks, two kinds of gloves, a woollen cap, thermal innerwear and dark goggles, and why HMI's course includes classes on mountain clothing and its materials.

Air at 0 °C with a 30 km/h wind: what's the wind chill?

13.12 + 0 − 11.37 × 1.72 + 0 ≈ −6.5 °C.

CH 4

Load, height & energy

Every kilo in your pack is a kilo you lift up the mountain.

Lifting a mass m through a height h takes work W = m g h. Your muscles turn only part of the food energy they burn into that work; our examples use an illustrative 20%, so the energy you burn is about five times the work. Thin air also caps how hard you can work: the CDC notes that maximal exercise performance at high altitude is always lower than at sea level, even after acclimatising.

Worked example · a 70 kg climber gaining 1,000 m (from our Altitude Lab, illustrative)
PackTotal massLifting workEnergy burned (20%)
0 kg70 kg687 kJ821 kcal
10 kg80 kg785 kJ938 kcal
15 kg85 kg834 kJ996 kcal
25 kg95 kg932 kJ1,114 kcal

85 × 9.81 × 1000 ≈ 834 kJ; ÷ 0.20 ≈ 4,169 kJ; ÷ 4.184 ≈ 996 kcal. This is only the lifting: walking, keeping warm and the descent cost more.

That's why the institutes ask for fitness before you arrive. NIM recommends a month of walking at least 5 km with a 15 kg pack, 30 minutes of cardio a day, and being able to do 20 push-ups and 5 pull-ups, and it tests trainees on a 10 km walk with a 10 kg load before they go into the mountains. ABVIMAS suggests at least two months of cardio and strength training.

A 60 kg climber with a 10 kg pack gains 500 m. About how much energy at 20% efficiency?

70 × 9.81 × 500 ≈ 343 kJ; ÷ 0.20 ≈ 1,717 kJ ≈ 410 kcal.

CH 5

Slips on snow & self-arrest

On hard snow, a slip becomes a slide in seconds.

On a slope at angle θ, gravity pulls you down the slope with g sin θ and friction holds you back with μ g cos θ, where μ is the friction coefficient. On hard, icy snow μ is small, so once you slide you keep accelerating: a = g (sin θ − μ cos θ), and after sliding a distance d your speed is v = √(2 a d). That's why mountaineers learn self-arrest: rolling onto the ice axe and driving its pick into the snow immediately, before the speed builds. HMI's Basic Mountaineering Course includes self-arrest, glacier travel, fixed ropes and crevasse rescue at its base camp.

Worked example · sliding on a 30° slope of hard snow, μ = 0.1 (illustrative)
Distance slidSpeed (m/s)Speed (km/h)
5 m6.423
10 m9.032
20 m12.746
50 m20.172

a = 9.81 × (sin 30° − 0.1 × cos 30°) = 9.81 × (0.5 − 0.087) ≈ 4.06 m/s². After 20 m: √(2 × 4.06 × 20) ≈ 12.7 m/s. Soft snow (μ = 0.3) cuts that to about 9.7 m/s, which still hurts on rocks.

Ropes on a mountain work on the same physics as on a crag: stretch absorbs a fall's energy, and the angle between anchor points multiplies the load. Our Rock Climbing page works through fall factors and anchor angles.

On a 20° slope with μ = 0.1, what's the acceleration, and the speed after 20 m?

9.81 × (0.342 − 0.094) ≈ 2.43 m/s²; √(2 × 2.43 × 20) ≈ 9.9 m/s (about 36 km/h).

CH 6

Snow slopes & avalanches

The most dangerous slopes are steep enough to slide, but not so steep that snow can't build up.

Snow falls in layers. A slab avalanche starts when a weak layer under a stiffer slab can no longer hold it. The slab's weight pulls along the slope with a shear stress on the weak layer of about τ = ρ g d sin θ (ρ the snow density, d the slab thickness measured at right angles to the slope), so steeper slopes load the weak layer harder. Avalanche.org says most slab avalanches start where the steepest part of the slope is between 30° and 50°, citing a Swiss dataset of over 1,000 avalanches in which 96% released on 30–50° slopes and most on 34–45°. Avalanches can then run out onto much gentler ground below.

Worked example · a 0.5 m slab of snow at 200 kg/m³ (illustrative)
Slope anglesin θShear stress on the weak layer
20°0.34336 Pa
30°0.50490 Pa
38°0.62604 Pa
45°0.71694 Pa

200 × 9.81 × 0.5 ≈ 981 Pa; × sin 30° ≈ 490 Pa. A weak layer that holds 550 Pa would survive at 30° and fail at 38°. Real snow strength varies hugely, which is why avalanche forecasting is a skill, not a sum.

This page doesn't teach avalanche safety

Avalanche terrain needs training, a transceiver, probe and shovel you know how to use, a current local forecast and an experienced leader. HMI's Basic Course includes classes on mountain hazards and avalanches; our Skiing & Snowboarding page covers avalanches from a skier's side.

Same slab on a 35° slope (sin 35° ≈ 0.57): what's the shear stress?

981 × 0.57 ≈ 563 Pa.

04 · Governing bodies

Who sets the rules

Worldwide, the UIAA represents climbing and mountaineering. In India, the Indian Mountaineering Foundation governs expeditions, and a group of government institutes runs the courses. We checked each on an official source.

Global

World body

UIAA · International Climbing and Mountaineering Federation

Founded in 1932; 99 member associations in 77 countries (as of its 2025 General Assembly); recognised by the IOC since 1995; sets safety standards for equipment and training.

theuiaa.org
Mountain medicine

UIAA Medical Commission

Recommendation papers on altitude illness, nutrition and trek safety, written for climbers and doctors.

theuiaa.org

India

National body

Indian Mountaineering Foundation (IMF)

The apex national body for mountaineering and allied sports, formed on 5 January 1961, a UIAA member (according to the IMF), and the office that runs the expedition application process for Indian peaks.

indmount.org
Institute

Nehru Institute of Mountaineering (NIM), Uttarkashi

Set up after a 1964 proposal by the Ministry of Defence and the Uttar Pradesh government, and headed by a principal chosen by the Ministry of Defence; runs adventure, Basic, Advanced, instructor, rescue, skiing and sport climbing courses.

nimindia.net
Institute

Himalayan Mountaineering Institute (HMI), Darjeeling

Founded on 4 November 1954 to mark the first ascent of Everest by Tenzing Norgay and Edmund Hillary; field training in West Sikkim.

hmidarjeeling.com

ABVIMAS, Manali
