CH 1

Power & the three resistances

Every watt you make goes into rolling, climbing or pushing air.

Power is force × speed: P = F × v. On a bike, three forces resist you: rolling resistance (Crr × m g, from tyres squashing), gravity on a slope (m g sin θ), and air drag (½ ρ CdA v²). Rolling and gravity grow in step with speed in power terms; drag power grows with v³.

Worked example · 85 kg rider + bike, flat smooth road, Crr 0.005, CdA 0.32 m², ρ 1.2 kg/m³
SpeedRolling powerDrag powerTotal
20 km/h≈ 23 W≈ 33 W≈ 56 W
30 km/h≈ 35 W≈ 111 W≈ 146 W
40 km/h≈ 46 W≈ 263 W≈ 309 W

At 30 km/h (8.33 m/s): rolling = 0.005 × 85 × 9.81 × 8.33 ≈ 35 W; drag = ½ × 1.2 × 0.32 × 8.33³ ≈ 111 W. Doubling speed from 20 to 40 km/h needs more than five times the power. Drag areas and Crr are typical illustrative values.

✋
Feel the numberA bright household LED bulb uses about 10 W. Cruising at 30 km/h on the flat takes about 150 W: fifteen of them, from your legs, for as long as you ride.

Rolling resistance, more closely

Crr isn't a fixed property of "a bike". It comes mostly from the tyre casing flexing and springing back (hysteresis), so it depends on the tyre's construction, width, pressure and the surface. Because the force is Crr × m g, the power it costs grows only in step with speed, unlike drag.

Worked example · rolling power at 30 km/h, 85 kg rider + bike (illustrative Crr values)
Tyre and surface (typical range)CrrRolling forceRolling power
Fast road tyre, smooth tarmac0.003≈ 2.5 N≈ 21 W
Everyday road tyre (our lab's "smooth road")0.005≈ 4.2 N≈ 35 W
Heavy commuter tyre or worn surface0.008≈ 6.7 N≈ 56 W
Gravel (lab)0.012≈ 10 N≈ 83 W

Force = Crr × 85 × 9.81; power = force × 8.33 m/s. At 200 W on the hoods, swapping Crr 0.005 for 0.003 lifts steady speed from about 33.9 to 35.0 km/h; going to 0.008 drops it to about 32.4 km/h.

Why harder tyres aren't always faster: roughness resistance

On a perfectly smooth drum, more pressure means less casing flex and lower Crr. Real roads have bumps. A very stiff tyre passes those bumps into the bike and your body, where the vibration is soaked up as heat: that's lost power too. A 2024 model of this "roughness resistance" (Turner, Dublin City University, arXiv:2405.00019) finds it depends mainly on how rough the road is and how stiff the bike-plus-tyre is vertically. On moderately rough roads it can be larger than rolling resistance, and wider, softer tyres (or a more compliant frame) cut it. On very smooth roads, the stiff, narrow set-up still wins slightly because it has less drag. The practical lesson: there's a best pressure for each surface, and on Indian secondary roads it's usually lower than the number printed on the sidewall's maximum. Stay within the tyre and rim maker's limits.

The crossover: when does air beat the ground?

Drag force (½ρCdA v²) and rolling force (Crr m g) are equal at v = √(2 Crr m g ÷ (ρ CdA)). Above that speed, air is your biggest enemy; below it, the ground is.

Worked example · crossover speed, 85 kg, ρ 1.2 kg/m³
Set-upCrrCdADrag = rolling at
Road bike, hoods, smooth road0.0050.32 m²≈ 17 km/h
Upright MTB on gravel0.0120.60 m²≈ 19 km/h
Upright MTB on a dirt trail0.0250.60 m²≈ 27 km/h

Road bike: √(2 × 0.005 × 85 × 9.81 ÷ (1.2 × 0.32)) ≈ 4.7 m/s ≈ 17 km/h. On a trail, the ground dominates at almost any speed you'll actually ride, which is why tyre choice matters more than an aero tuck off-road.

The chain takes its cut

Our Power Lab assumes every pedal watt reaches the back wheel. In reality the chain and sprockets lose a little. Laboratory tests at Johns Hopkins University (Spicer and colleagues, Journal of Mechanical Design, 2001) measured derailleur chain-drive efficiencies from about 81% to 98.6%, with bigger sprockets and higher chain tension scoring better. Lubricant made no measurable difference on a new, clean chain in the lab; the researchers think its real job outdoors is keeping dirt out, and a dirty chain is a different story. A clean drivetrain in a sensible gear (not the smallest sprockets) should sit at the high end.

Worked example · 200 W at the pedals, 85 kg, hoods, flat smooth road
Drivetrain efficiencyWatts at the wheelSteady speed
100% (lab model)200 W≈ 33.9 km/h
98%196 W≈ 33.7 km/h
95%190 W≈ 33.3 km/h

Because drag power grows with v³, losing 5% of your power costs under 2% of your speed. Same model as the lab, with the wheel power reduced.

You hold 300 W against 30 N of total resistance. How fast are you going?

v = P ÷ F = 300 ÷ 30 = 10 m/s = 36 km/h.

CH 2

Air drag, position & drafting

Your body, not the bike, makes most of the drag. How you sit changes everything.

The drag area CdA combines how big you look from the front and how streamlined you are. Sitting upright on a mountain bike, you're a parachute; low on the drops of a road bike, you slice through. The same power then gives very different speeds.

Worked example · 200 W, 85 kg, flat smooth road (from our Power Lab)
PositionCdA (illustrative)Steady speed10 km takes
Upright (MTB)0.60 m²≈ 27.9 km/h≈ 21.5 min
Hands on the hoods0.32 m²≈ 33.9 km/h≈ 17.7 min
Low, on the drops0.27 m²≈ 35.7 km/h≈ 16.8 min

Solve 200 = v(0.005 × 85 × 9.81) + ½ × 1.2 × CdA × v³ for v. With CdA 0.32: v ≈ 9.42 m/s ≈ 33.9 km/h; about 80% of the 200 W goes into drag.

Drafting

A rider ahead pushes the air aside and leaves a wake of slower-moving air. Ride in it and your drag falls, which is why groups ride in lines and take turns at the front. The cost: you're centimetres from another wheel, with little time to react. Ride close only with riders you trust, never on a busy road, and always signal hazards.

How much does it help? Earlier studies of small single-file groups found the following riders' drag falling to roughly 70% to 50% of a rider alone. Inside a big, tightly packed race peloton the shelter is far greater: a 2018 study by Blocken and colleagues (CFD simulations of 121 riders, checked in four wind-tunnel campaigns; Journal of Wind Engineering and Industrial Aerodynamics) found riders in the middle and rear of the bunch facing only 5% to 10% of the drag of a rider alone.

Worked example · power to hold 35 km/h, 85 kg, CdA 0.32 m², smooth road
Where you rideDrag vs riding alonePower needed
Alone, or on the front100%≈ 217 W
Second wheel in a small line (upper estimate)≈ 70%≈ 164 W
Well sheltered in a small line (lower estimate)≈ 50%≈ 129 W
Mid-pack in a 121-rider race peloton (Blocken et al.)≈ 10%≈ 58 W

Rolling power (≈ 41 W) doesn't change; only the drag term is scaled. The peloton figure is for tightly packed professional racing on closed roads, not a Sunday group ride. Don't try to recreate it in traffic.

Thin air, hot air and wind

Drag is proportional to air density ρ, and ρ falls with heat and altitude. Same 200 W, same position:

Worked example · 200 W, 85 kg, hoods, flat smooth road (standard-atmosphere densities)
WhereAir densitySteady speed
Sea level, 15 °C1.23 kg/m³≈ 33.7 km/h
Sea level, 35 °C1.15 kg/m³≈ 34.4 km/h
About 920 m (Bengaluru's height)1.12 kg/m³≈ 34.7 km/h
About 2,200 m (Shimla's height)0.99 kg/m³≈ 36.0 km/h
About 3,500 m (Leh's height)0.86 kg/m³≈ 37.5 km/h

ρ = p ÷ (R T) from the International Standard Atmosphere. The catch: thinner air also carries less oxygen, so most riders can't hold the same 200 W high up, and the heat that thins the air also makes hard efforts harder. The lab's 1.2 kg/m³ is a round sea-level value.

Wind changes the airspeed, and drag follows the airspeed. Riding at 25 km/h over the ground into a 15 km/h headwind, the drag power is ½ρCdA × (airspeed)² × (ground speed): about 194 W in total instead of about 93 W in still air. A tailwind does the opposite, which is why out-and-back routes rarely average out the way you hope.

✋
Feel the numberSitting up into a headwind feels like hitting a wall because drag depends on your speed through the air, not over the ground. A 15 km/h headwind at 25 km/h feels like riding at 40 km/h in still air.
If you go 10% faster, by roughly how much does your drag power rise?

1.1³ ≈ 1.33: about a third more power, for a tenth more speed.

CH 3

Climbing & watts per kilo

Uphill, the air hardly matters. Your weight does.

On a gradient, gravity pulls back with m g sin θ, where tan θ = gradient ÷ 100. Climbing is slow, so drag is small and most of your power lifts you and the bike. That's why climbers talk about watts per kilo and height gained per hour.

Worked example · 200 W steady, smooth road, hoods position
GradientRider + bikeSpeedShare of power lifting youHeight gained per hour
5%85 kg≈ 14.7 km/h≈ 85%≈ 734 m
8%85 kg≈ 10.0 km/h≈ 92%≈ 796 m
8%75 kg≈ 11.2 km/h≈ 92%≈ 894 m

8%, 85 kg: gravity = 85 × 9.81 × sin(arctan 0.08) ≈ 66 N. At 2.77 m/s that's ≈ 184 W of the 200 W. Height per hour = v sin θ × 3,600 ≈ 796 m.

Pacing a climb

  • Steady beats surges: because drag barely matters uphill, extra watts go almost entirely into climbing, but a surge you can't sustain costs you later.
  • Gear down early to keep a comfortable cadence (Ch 4).
  • Descents: the height you gain becomes speed on the way down. Brake before bends, not in them (Ch 5).
A 70 kg rider holds 210 W. What's their power-to-weight ratio?

210 ÷ 70 = 3.0 W/kg (using rider mass; the lab uses rider + bike).

CH 4

Gears & cadence

Gears don't make power. They trade force for pedalling speed.

The gear ratio is chainring teeth ÷ cog teeth: how many times the rear wheel turns per pedal revolution. Multiply by the wheel's circumference and you get how far one pedal stroke moves you. Then speed = distance per stroke × cadence.

Worked example · distance per pedal turn and speed at 90 rpm
Bike and gearRatioPer pedal turnSpeed at 90 rpm
Road, 50 × 11 (top gear)4.55≈ 9.5 m≈ 51 km/h
Road, 34 × 28 (climbing gear)1.21≈ 2.6 m≈ 14 km/h
MTB, 32 × 103.20≈ 7.4 m≈ 40 km/h
MTB, 32 × 50 (lowest)0.64≈ 1.5 m≈ 8 km/h

Road wheel circumference taken as 2.1 m; 29-inch MTB wheel as 2.3 m. 50 ÷ 11 × 2.1 ≈ 9.55 m; × 90 per minute × 60 ÷ 1,000 ≈ 51.5 km/h. Real circumferences vary with tyre size.

Why cadence matters

For a given power, a higher cadence means less force on each pedal stroke (P = torque × pedalling speed). Spinning a lower gear is gentler on your knees and lets you respond to changes in gradient. Shift before the climb gets steep, while you can still ease the pressure on the pedals.

At 80 rpm in a gear that moves you 5 m per pedal turn, how fast are you going?

5 × 80 = 400 m per minute = 24 km/h.

CH 5

Cornering & braking

Two thin contact patches handle every turn and every stop.

Stopping distance = reaction distance + braking distance: d = v treact + v² ÷ (2a). Under braking, weight transfers to the front wheel, so the front brake does most of the work, until the rear lifts. A rough limit: maximum deceleration ≈ g × (horizontal distance from your centre of mass to the front contact patch ÷ its height).

Worked example · stopping at 0.5 g after a 1-second reaction
SpeedReaction distanceBraking distanceTotal
20 km/h≈ 5.6 m≈ 3.1 m≈ 8.7 m
30 km/h≈ 8.3 m≈ 7.1 m≈ 15.4 m
40 km/h≈ 11.1 m≈ 12.6 m≈ 23.7 m

30 km/h = 8.33 m/s; braking = 8.33² ÷ (2 × 0.5 × 9.81) ≈ 7.1 m. Tip-over limit example: centre of mass 1.0 m high and 0.6 m behind the front contact patch gives about 0.6 g. On gravel or a wet road, grip can be far lower.

Cornering

To follow a bend of radius r at speed v you lean at tan φ = v² ÷ (g r): at 10 m/s on a 20 m bend, about 27°; at 8 m/s on a 10 m bend, about 33°. Braking and cornering share the same tyre grip, so brake in a straight line before the bend and release as you lean.

On the road

Wear a helmet, use front and rear lights after dark, ride with the traffic and be predictable. Check brakes and tyres before every ride.

Doubling your speed multiplies braking distance by how much?

d ∝ v², so by 4.

CH 6

Trails, tyres & suspension

Off-road, the ground fights back. Tyres and suspension decide how much.

On dirt, tyres sink and knobs squirm, so rolling resistance rises steeply. Low pressures let the tyre wrap around rocks and roots for grip; suspension and bent legs spread out impacts so the wheels stay on the ground.

The roughness-resistance idea from Chapter 1 is at its strongest off-road: every root and rock you bounce over lifts you and the bike, and that energy is lost as vibration in your body unless the tyre or suspension absorbs it first. That's the physics behind wide, lower-pressure MTB tyres and suspension forks. A rigid, over-inflated bike can be slower on rough ground as well as harsher. (Suspension that bobs every time you pedal wastes some power too, which is why many forks and shocks have a firmer setting for climbing.)

Drops and landings

Worked example · average deceleration while landing a drop
Drop heightLanding speedStopped overAverage deceleration
0.5 m≈ 3.1 m/s0.25 m≈ 2 g
1.0 m≈ 4.4 m/s0.25 m≈ 4 g

Landing speed = √(2 g h); average deceleration = h ÷ stopping distance × g (vertical drop onto flat ground). Landing on a downslope (a proper landing ramp) turns much of that into forward speed instead.

Learn trail skills properly

Walk features before riding them, start small, wear a helmet (and gloves and pads for technical trails), and don't ride remote trails alone. A course such as NIM's mountain terrain biking course is a good place to start.

How much faster do you land from a 2 m drop than from a 0.5 m drop?

v ∝ √h, so √4 = 2 times faster (about 6.3 m/s against 3.1 m/s), with four times the energy.
