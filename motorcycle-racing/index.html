Kinetic · Adventure science · Motorcycle Racing & Off-road

Lean, brake, land. Two small patches of rubber hold you up.

Road racing, motocross, rally raid and off-road riding all come down to the grip two tyres can give and the energy you carry when it runs out. Take the challenge, learn the science with a Lean & Grip Lab, then see FMSCI licences, off-road schools and where to start, including the four-wheel crews of off-road rallies. On closed courses and trails only: never race on public roads.

CH 1

Lean angle, grip & the traction circle

A bike corners by leaning, and the lean is set by physics, not by courage.

To follow a curve of radius r at speed v, something must push you sideways towards the centre: F = m v² ÷ r. On a bike only the tyres can give that push, and they can give at most F = μ N, where N is the load and μ the grip coefficient. The bike leans so that gravity and the sideways force line up through the tyres: tan θ = v² ÷ (g r). So the lean angle tells you exactly how much grip you are using: you need μ ≥ tan θ.

Worked example · lean angle and the grip it needs (simple model, illustrative)
Lean angleSideways force ÷ weightGrip μ needed
30°0.58 g0.58
45°1.00 g1.00
64° (MotoGP's quoted maximum)2.05 g2.05

MotoGP says its riders lean up to 64° on slicks that work at about 80–120 °C (MotoGP.com, Sep 2026). Real bikes lean a few degrees more than this formula because a wide tyre moves the contact patch off the centre line.

Same corner, different road: a 50 m radius taken at a 45° lean means about 80 km/h with μ = 1.0 (illustrative dry grip). On a wet road with μ = 0.5 (illustrative), the most you can lean is atan 0.5 ≈ 26.6° and the same corner tops out at about 56 km/h. Ride the dry speed in the wet and the tyres simply cannot supply the force.

Grip is a budget shared between braking or accelerating and turning: the traction circle. Braking at 80% of the limit leaves only √(1 − 0.8²) = 0.6, or 60%, for turning. That's why trail-braking deep into a corner, or grabbing the brake mid-corner, is where so many crashes start.

You lean at 40° in a dry corner. Roughly what grip are you using?

tan 40° ≈ 0.84: in this simple model you need μ of at least 0.84. In the wet (μ ≈ 0.5) that lean is beyond the limit.

CH 2

Countersteering & the gyroscope

To turn left at speed, you first push the left bar forward: the front wheel briefly points right.

A bike can't lean itself. Above walking pace, a rider starts a turn by countersteering: a brief steer the "wrong" way moves the tyre contact patches out from under the centre of mass, gravity then tips the bike into the lean, and the bike turns the way you wanted. Gyroscopic effects from the spinning wheels help, but they are not the main mechanism (Wikipedia, Countersteering; see also Fajans, American Journal of Physics, 2000).

The wheels do carry angular momentum L = I ω, and ω grows with speed: ω = v ÷ r. The faster you go, the stiffer the bike feels to tip, and the more deliberate the bar push you need.

Worked example · how fast a 0.3 m-radius wheel spins (illustrative wheel size)
Road speedω (rad/s)Revolutions per minute
30 km/h27.8265
100 km/h92.6884
300 km/h277.82,653

Angular momentum rises in step with ω, so at 300 km/h each wheel holds 10× the spin it had at 30 km/h. That stability is useful in a straight line and is one reason quick changes of direction take force and planning.

Why it matters for risk: in an emergency, many riders instinctively steer towards the obstacle they are staring at. Countersteering is a trained reflex. Practise it with an instructor on a closed course, never by experimenting on a public road.

You want the bike to lean right at 80 km/h. Which way do you push the bars?

Push the right bar forward: the front wheel briefly points left, the contact patch moves left and the bike falls into a right lean.

CH 3

Braking, load transfer & the stoppie limit

Brake hard and the bike's weight piles onto the front tyre, so the front does most of the stopping.

When you brake at deceleration a, the centre of mass (height h) wants to keep going, which moves load from the rear tyre to the front: ΔN = m a h ÷ L, with L the wheelbase. On a bike, h is large compared with L, so most braking comes from the front tyre. There's a second limit too: brake hard enough and the rear wheel lifts (a "stoppie"). In a simple model that happens at amax = g b ÷ h, where b is the distance from the front contact patch back to the centre of mass. With b = h = 0.7 m (illustrative) the bike tips at about 1 g, whatever the grip.

Worked example · stopping from 100 km/h, d = v² ÷ (2 a)
DecelerationBraking distancePlus 1 s of reaction
1.0 g (strong dry braking, illustrative)39.3 m67.1 m
0.5 g (wet or a cautious rider, illustrative)78.7 m106.5 m

In one second of reaction at 100 km/h you cover 27.8 m before the brakes do anything. Halve the deceleration and the braking distance doubles. MotoGP describes its bikes braking from 360 km/h to 60 km/h in about three seconds on carbon brakes and slicks; that is a racing machine on a prepared track, not a road bike.

Locking the front wheel removes its ability to steer and to hold the bike up, which is why a locked front almost always means a fall. Anti-lock brakes (ABS) release and reapply the brake many times a second to stay just short of a lock.

You double your speed. What happens to your braking distance at the same deceleration?

It goes up four times, because d = v² ÷ (2a).

CH 4

High-side, low-side & what the gear absorbs

A crash is a problem of energy: where it goes, and over what distance you stop.

A low-side is when a tyre slides out and the bike falls on its inside; the rider usually slides. A high-side is when a rear tyre slides, then suddenly grips again: the bike snaps upright and throws the rider over the top, often from a height and into the bike's path. High-sides are typically the more violent of the two.

Your kinetic energy is E = ½ m v². Sliding on tarmac with friction μ, you stop in d = v² ÷ (2 μ g). Hitting something solid stops you in centimetres instead of metres, and the average force goes up in proportion.

Worked example · an 80 kg rider at 100 km/h (illustrative numbers)
SituationStopping distanceAverage deceleration
Sliding on tarmac, μ ≈ 0.578.7 m (about 5.7 s)0.5 g
Into a gravel trapabout 20 mabout 2 g
Into a solid barrier0.5 mabout 79 g

Energy to get rid of: ½ × 80 × 27.8² ≈ 30.9 kJ. Run-off areas, gravel traps and air fences exist to turn the last row into the first two.

The helmet. A head hitting the ground at 25 km/h and stopping in 5 mm averages about 490 g; a crushable liner that stretches the stop to 30 mm brings it down to about 82 g (illustrative). That's why a helmet that has taken a hit must be replaced: the liner has used up its crush. The FIM, motorcycling's world body, requires helmets homologated to its FRHPhe-02 standard at its championships from 2026 (FIM), and airbag suits are standard in Grand Prix racing.

Safety The numbers behind rally raid and racing

Since 1979, 79 people have died in connection with the Dakar Rally; 33 were competitors, and 24 of those were on motorcycles (Wikipedia list). India's two Dakar finishers both know the cost: C. S. Santosh suffered a diffuse axonal brain injury and an induced coma after a 2021 crash (Autocar India), and Harith Noah has raced back from a T5 vertebra fracture and a 2025 wrist injury (Dakar.com). Gear and run-off reduce the force; they don't make a high-speed fall safe.

Why is a high-side usually worse than a low-side?

Because the rear tyre grips again suddenly, the bike snaps upright and throws the rider up and over, adding height and an impact with the ground or the bike.

CH 5

Motocross & supercross: jumps and landing loads

Every jump ends with a landing, and the suspension has a few hundred millimetres to absorb it.

Leave a ramp at speed v and angle θ and, ignoring air resistance, you fly a parabola: range R = v² sin 2θ ÷ g, peak height h = (v sin θ)² ÷ (2g). Land on a matching downslope and most of your speed carries on along the slope. Land flat (a "case" or an overshoot) and all of the vertical speed √(2 g h) has to be stopped by the suspension, the tyres and your legs.

Worked example · a jump at 15 m/s (54 km/h) off a 30° ramp (illustrative)
QuantityValue
Range19.9 m
Peak height2.87 m
Vertical speed on a flat landing7.5 m/s
Average deceleration over 0.3 m of suspension travelabout 9.6 g

Average deceleration ≈ drop height ÷ stopping distance, here 2.87 ÷ 0.3. The peak is higher than the average. A matched landing ramp removes most of this load, which is why track builders shape landings and why a short jump is so punishing.

In India, supercross and motocross are run under FMSCI. The MRF MoGrip FMSCI National Supercross Championship 2026 lists six rounds, from Shillong in April to Kolhapur on 29 November.

You come up short and land flat from a 2 m drop. Roughly what average g does 0.3 m of suspension see?

About 2 ÷ 0.3 ≈ 6.7 g on average, and more at the peak.

CH 6

Off-road: bikes on loose ground, and four-wheel crews

On dirt, sand and rock the grip is lower and changes every metre, so technique is about managing grip, not speed.

Motorcycle off-road: trail, enduro and adventure riding

Standing on the pegs. Standing puts your weight through the footpegs, low down at the bike's centre, instead of high up on the seat. The bike can move and pitch under you while your mass stays steady, and bent knees and elbows become extra suspension.

Throttle and traction. Loose surfaces give far less grip, roughly μ ≈ 0.3–0.5 (illustrative), and it varies. Smooth throttle keeps the rear tyre just short of spinning; a sudden handful breaks traction. On a climb of angle α the tyre must supply grip of at least μ ≥ tan α: about 0.36 for 20°, 0.58 for 30°. Smooth clutch control ("feathering") lets you meter tiny amounts of drive when grip is marginal.

Momentum on climbs. Kinetic energy can carry you up a short rise: v = √(2 g h). Climbing 3 m on momentum alone needs about 7.7 m/s, around 28 km/h at the bottom (ignoring losses): more than most beginners expect, and too much if the top is blind.

Worked example · tyre pressure and contact patch (simple model: patch area ≈ load ÷ pressure)
Load on the tyrePressureContact patch
1,000 N1.5 barabout 67 cm²
1,000 N1.0 barabout 100 cm²
1,000 N0.8 barabout 125 cm²

Lower pressure lets the tyre spread and wrap over stones, which is why off-road riders drop pressure. Real tyres are stiffer than this model and too little pressure risks pinch flats and rim damage: follow the tyre maker's range.

Falls at low speed and high speed. Most beginner off-road falls happen at walking pace. Energy rises with the square of speed: an 80 kg rider carries about 309 J at 10 km/h and about 11.1 kJ at 60 km/h, 36 times as much. Learning slowly on a training ground, in boots, knee braces, body armour, gloves and a helmet, keeps the inevitable falls in the low-energy row.

Four-wheel off-road: a driver and co-driver crew

Off-road events for four-wheelers are usually run by a crew of two: a driver and a co-driver who navigates, spots obstacles and rigs the winch. At Rainforest Challenge India in Goa each team is a driver plus co-driver.

Angles. A vehicle's approach and departure angles say how steep a slope it can meet front or back without hitting the bumper; the breakover angle says how sharp a crest it can cross without grounding the middle. Roughly, breakover ≈ 2 atan(2c ÷ WB), with c the ground clearance and WB the wheelbase: a 2.5 m wheelbase with 0.22 m clearance gives about 20° (illustrative).

Rollover. A vehicle tips sideways on a slope when its centre of mass moves outside the downhill wheels. The US road-safety agency NHTSA uses the static stability factor SSF = T ÷ 2H (track width over twice the centre-of-mass height) to rate rollover risk (NHTSA). In the simplest static model, a vehicle tips at a side slope of atan(SSF).

Worked example · static side-slope limit (illustrative vehicle)
Track TCoM height HSSFStatic tip angle
1.5 m0.7 m1.07about 47°
1.5 m (roof load raises the CoM)0.8 m0.94about 43°

Real vehicles tip well before the static figure: suspension leans, wheels hit rocks and ruts and the slope is never even. A roof load lowers the limit, which is why heavy kit goes low and inside.

Low range. A low-range gearbox multiplies wheel torque: a 2.5:1 low range gives about 2.5× the torque at the wheels at the same engine torque, at 1/2.5 of the speed. Slow, controlled crawling keeps the tyres from spinning and digging in.

Winching. Pulling a stuck 2,000 kg vehicle out of deep mud can take a force of about half its weight (illustrative), about 9.8 kN. Routing the line through a snatch block on an anchor and back to the vehicle halves the winch tension to about 4.9 kN, but the anchor and the block then carry about twice that. A loaded line stores energy like a spring; if it or a fitting fails, it whips. That's why crews keep people out of the line's path and lay a damper over it.

Water crossings. Every cubic metre of vehicle under water gives about 9.8 kN of buoyancy: for a 2,000 kg vehicle with 1 m³ submerged that's half its weight, so the tyres press down and grip with only half the force. Flowing water pushes sideways too: drag F = ½ ρ Cd A v² on 1.5 m² at 2 m/s is about 3 kN (Cd ≈ 1, illustrative). And water drawn into the air intake can wreck the engine instantly (hydrolock).

A climb is 30°. In the simple model, what grip does the tyre need?

tan 30° ≈ 0.58: often more than loose dirt gives (≈ 0.3–0.5), so the climb needs momentum, a better line or a different surface.
