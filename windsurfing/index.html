CH 1

Apparent wind & the wind window

Your sail never feels the true wind once you move.

When you sail, your own motion makes a headwind. The sail feels the vector sum of that and the true wind: the apparent wind. Sailing upwind it's stronger than the true wind; sailing downwind it's weaker. In every direction, the faster you go, the further forward it swings, so you sheet the sail in as you speed up.

Worked example · true wind 15 kn, board speed 10 kn (from our Sail Lab)
Heading off the windApparent windAngle from your noseSail force (6 m²)
45° (upwind)≈ 23.2 kn≈ 27°≈ 522 N
90° (beam reach)≈ 18.0 kn≈ 56°≈ 316 N
135° (broad reach)≈ 10.6 kn≈ 93°≈ 110 N

Beam reach: √(15² + 10²) ≈ 18.0 kn, at arctan(15 ÷ 10) ≈ 56° from the nose. 1 kn = 1.852 km/h.

🏄
Feel the numberSame wind, same speed: heading upwind your sail feels more than twice the wind it feels on a broad reach, and almost five times the force.

Speed pulls the wind forward. Stay on a broad reach (135° off the true wind) and let the board accelerate. The apparent wind swings round towards the nose, which is why a planing windsurfer sheets in hard even when heading downwind.

Worked example · broad reach, true wind 15 kn, board speeding up (Sail Lab model)
Board speedApparent windAngle from your nose
0 kn (standing still)15.0 kn135°
5 kn≈ 12.0 kn≈ 118°
10 kn≈ 10.6 kn≈ 93°
20 kn (planing)≈ 14.2 kn≈ 48°

At 20 kn: x = 15 cos 135° + 20 ≈ 9.4, y = 15 sin 135° ≈ 10.6, so √(9.4² + 10.6²) ≈ 14.2 kn at arctan(10.6 ÷ 9.4) ≈ 48°.

The wind window: where you can sail. Picture a clock face with the true wind blowing from 12 o'clock. You can't sail straight at it: there's a no-go zone of very roughly 45° on either side. Every other heading is a point of sail: close-hauled just outside the no-go zone, then beam reach (wind on your side), broad reach and finally a run (wind from behind). Close-hauled and beam reaches are driven mainly by lift from the sail as a wing (Ch 2). On a run, the wind mostly just pushes on the sail, and you can never go faster downwind than the wind itself. That's why fast windsurfers reach across the wind rather than run straight before it.

True wind 12 kn on the beam, board speed 9 kn. What apparent wind?

√(12² + 9²) = 15 kn.

CH 2

The sail as a wing & the harness

A sail is a vertical wing. You are the mast's anchor.

A windsurf sail is an aerofoil. Battens and the curve of the mast give it a built-in camber, like an aircraft wing standing on its end. Air flowing round the curved sail speeds up on the curved downwind (leeward) side and slows on the windward side, so pressure is lower to leeward. That pressure difference makes a force, F = ½ ρ v² A CL, roughly at right angles to the apparent wind. Part of it drives you forward; part pushes you sideways, which the fin (and a daggerboard, on some boards) resists (Ch 3).

Worked example · sail size and wind (CL 1.0, illustrative)
Sail12 kn apparent18 kn apparent
4.5 m²≈ 105 N≈ 236 N
6.0 m²≈ 140 N≈ 315 N
7.5 m²≈ 175 N≈ 394 N
9.0 m²≈ 210 N≈ 473 N

18 kn = 9.26 m/s: 0.5 × 1.225 × 9.26² × 6 ≈ 315 N. Going from 12 to 18 kn multiplies the force by (18 ÷ 12)² = 2.25. Spread over 6 m², 315 N is a pressure difference of only about 53 Pa, around 1/2000 of atmospheric pressure, yet it can pull a person across the water.

That v² is why sailors carry several sails: a big sail for light wind, a small one when it's strong.

Angle of attack, sheeting and stall. Pulling in with your back hand (sheeting in) increases the sail's angle to the apparent wind, its angle of attack. Up to a point, more angle means more lift. Too much, and the airflow can no longer follow the leeward curve: it breaks away, lift collapses and drag soars. That's a stall. Too little angle and the front of the sail flaps (luffs) and the force fades. Good sailors keep the sail just short of stalling, and re-trim as the apparent wind shifts with every change of speed (Ch 1).

The harness and harness lines. You hold the rig up and lean against its pull. A harness (worn round your waist or seat) has a hook that clips into a harness line, a loop of rope on each side of the boom, so your body weight takes the load instead of your arms. The line needs to straddle the sail's centre of effort. If your front hand is doing most of the work, the force is centred ahead of the lines: move the line forward. If your back hand is, move it back. When it's set right you can hook in and briefly let go with both hands.

Worked example · how far you lean, 75 kg rider (simplified, illustrative)
Sideways pull from the sailLean from vertical to balance it
219 N (15 kn, standing still)≈ 17°
316 N (18 kn apparent)≈ 23°
522 N (23 kn apparent)≈ 35°

Treat yourself as a straight body pivoting at your feet, with the pull at your centre of mass: tan(lean) = pull ÷ weight. 75 × 9.81 ≈ 736 N, so 316 ÷ 736 ≈ 0.43 and arctan 0.43 ≈ 23°. Real riders also bend their legs and move their hips, but the idea holds: more pull, lean further out.

The apparent wind rises from 10 to 15 kn. How much more sail force?

(15 ÷ 10)² = 2.25 times.

CH 3

Steering, fin & daggerboard

No rudder. You steer by moving the sail.

The sail's force acts through its centre of effort (CE). The water's sideways resistance acts through the board's centre of lateral resistance (CLR), near the fin or daggerboard. If the CE is directly above the CLR the board goes straight. Tilt the rig forward and the CE moves ahead of the CLR: the force turns the nose away from the wind (bearing away). Tilt it back and the tail is pushed away, turning the nose into the wind (heading up).

Worked example · a turning moment (illustrative values)
Rig tiltCE ahead (+) or behind (−) the CLRTurning moment at 300 NBoard turns
Upright0 m0 N·mStraight
Forward+0.3 m90 N·mAway from the wind
Back−0.3 m90 N·m the other wayInto the wind

Moment = force × lever arm: 300 N × 0.3 m = 90 N·m. On a planing board, riders also steer by tilting the board onto its rail.

Fins and daggerboards are underwater wings. Without them, the sideways part of the sail force would just push the board sideways across the water. A fin meets the water at a small angle as the board slips very slightly sideways (leeway), and makes sideways lift with the same formula as the sail, ½ ρ v² A CL, but in water about 840 times denser than air. So a fin a fraction of the sail's size can balance the sail, if the board is moving fast.

Worked example · sideways grip from a 0.04 m² fin, CL 0.3 (illustrative)
Board speedFin side forceWhat it means
5 kn≈ 41 NToo little: a learner's board would slide sideways
10 kn≈ 163 NGetting there
20 kn≈ 651 NPlenty: why fast boards need only a fin

At 20 kn (10.3 m/s): 0.5 × 1,025 × 10.3² × 0.04 × 0.3 ≈ 651 N. Side force grows with speed squared, so halving your speed leaves a quarter of the grip.

That's why beginner and racing longboards (such as the Raceboard and Techno 293 classes) carry a daggerboard: a much larger blade through the middle of the board that gives grip at low speed and lets you point upwind. When the board is going fast on a reach, riders pull the daggerboard up. Left down at speed, its own lift tends to tip the board onto its rail.

You want to turn towards the wind. Which way do you tilt the rig?

Back, towards the tail.

CH 4

Hull speed, planing & foiling

Push water aside, skim over it, or fly above it.

A board pushing through the water makes a bow wave. A wave's speed depends on its length, v = √(g L ÷ 2π), so once the bow wave is as long as the board, going faster means climbing your own wave. That's the hull speed. With enough wind, a light board climbs onto the surface and planes, skimming instead of pushing water aside. A hydrofoil (as on the Olympic iQFOiL) goes further: an underwater wing lifts the whole board clear of the water.

Floating first: volume. Standing still, the board floats by buoyancy: it must displace its own weight plus yours in water. Each litre of seawater it pushes under supports about 1 kg, so a 100 kg load of rider plus rig sinks a board to its deck at about 98 litres. Learner boards carry far more volume than that, so they float high and stable while you uphaul (Ch 6). Fast boards carry less, and only work once they're planing.

Planing: from floating to skimming. Naval architects compare speed with length using the Froude number, Fr = v ÷ √(g L). Hull speed is Fr ≈ 0.4. Somewhere above Fr ≈ 1, the flat underside acts like a water-ski: it deflects water downwards, the reaction lifts the board, and most of it rises out of the water. The wetted area shrinks, drag falls, and the board accelerates, which raises the apparent wind (Ch 1) and the sail force (Ch 2). That's why "getting on the plane" feels like a sudden surge. Riders help it happen by bearing away onto a reach, moving back into the footstraps, hooking into the harness, and pumping the sail in marginal wind.

Worked example · a 2.5 m board (rules of thumb, illustrative)
RegimeFroude numberBoard speed
Displacement (hull speed)≈ 0.4≈ 3.8 kn
Planing begins (roughly)≈ 1≈ 9.6 kn

Hull speed of a 2.5 m board: √(9.81 × 2.5 ÷ 6.28) ≈ 1.98 m/s ≈ 3.8 kn, which is Fr = 1 ÷ √(2π) ≈ 0.4. Fr = 1: √(9.81 × 2.5) ≈ 4.95 m/s ≈ 9.6 kn. Real boards vary with shape, weight and rider.

Worked example · foil lift, 0.1 m² wing, CL 0.4, 85 kg rider and board (from our Sail Lab, illustrative)

Foil: 0.5 × 1,025 × (10 × 0.514)² × 0.1 × 0.4 ≈ 543 N. Water is about 800 times denser than air (1,025 ÷ 1.225 ≈ 840), which is why such a small wing can lift a person.

Lift grows with speed squared. If the foil just flies at 13 kn, about what share of the lift does it give at 6.5 kn?

(6.5 ÷ 13)² = 25%.

CH 5

Tacking, gybing & VMG

To go upwind, you zig-zag. Angle and speed trade off.

No sail can drive you straight into the wind: there's a "no-go zone" on either side of it (Ch 1). To reach a point upwind you sail at an angle, then turn through the wind (a tack) and sail the other way. Turning with the wind behind you is a gybe. Your upwind progress is the velocity made good, VMG = speed × cos(angle off the wind).

Worked example · pointing high vs sailing fast (illustrative)
Angle off the windBoard speedVMG
40°6 kn≈ 4.6 kn
45°8 kn≈ 5.7 kn
55°10 kn≈ 5.7 kn
90°10 kn0 kn

8 × cos 45° ≈ 5.7 kn. Pointing higher isn't always better: sailing a little lower and faster can make the same progress.

Beginner courses teach turning the board slowly (Quest's Level 2 introduces tacks and gybes in non-planing conditions). Then comes turning at speed: Quest's Level 3 and its "turning with speed" course cover tacks and gybes in planing conditions, and the RYA scheme runs carve gybe clinics.

The carve gybe: a banked turn on water. A planing board turns by tipping onto its inside rail, so the rail and fin carve an arc. Turning in a circle of radius r at speed v takes a sideways (centripetal) force m v² ÷ r towards the centre of the turn. You lean into the turn, like a cyclist, until your weight and that force balance: tan(lean) = v² ÷ (g r). As you head downwind through the turn, the apparent wind drops and swings behind you (Ch 1), so the rig goes light. That's the moment to flip the sail onto the new side and keep planing out of the turn.

Worked example · lean angle in a carve gybe (illustrative)
SpeedTurn radiusLean from vertical
15 kn15 m≈ 22°
15 kn8 m≈ 37°
20 kn10 m≈ 47°

20 kn = 10.3 m/s: 10.3² ÷ (9.81 × 10) ≈ 1.08, and arctan 1.08 ≈ 47°. Faster or tighter turns need more lean. If you lean too little, the board can't hold the arc and the rail lets go.

At 12 kn and 60° off the wind, what's your VMG?

12 × cos 60° = 6 kn.

CH 6

Uphaul, waterstart & self-rescue

Know how you'll get up, and how you'd get home without the wind.

Uphauling: a lift with your legs. Beginners start standing on a floaty board with the rig lying in the water downwind. You pull the uphaul rope hand over hand, keeping your back straight and leaning back so your body weight and legs do the lifting, not your arms. It's hardest at the start: water lying on the sail adds weight until the top of the mast clears and the water drains. Once the clew (back corner) is out of the water, you're lifting only the rig's own weight and can bring it up to the sailing position.

Waterstarting: let the wind lift you. Better sailors skip the uphaul. Floating beside the board, you raise the rig into the wind, put your feet on the board, and let the sail's pull lift you up and on. That only works when the sail can pull hard enough, and sail force grows with the wind speed squared (Ch 2). Quest's Level 3 introduces stronger winds and the steps for beach and water starts, and its waterstart course is about getting up "in windy conditions".

Worked example · pull of a 6 m² sail while you're not moving (CL 1.0, illustrative)
True windSail forceShare of a 75 kg rider's weight (736 N)
8 kn≈ 62 N≈ 8%
12 kn≈ 140 N≈ 19%
15 kn≈ 219 N≈ 30%

The water still supports most of you, and the board starts moving as you rise, which raises the apparent wind. But nearly four times the pull at 15 kn as at 8 kn is why waterstarts are taught in stronger wind and uphauling in light wind.

Wind and weather. Sail force grows with the square of the wind (Ch 2), so a gust that's 40% stronger nearly doubles it. And wind direction decides where you drift if something goes wrong: offshore wind (land to sea) carries you away from the beach. Learn in steady, moderate wind, ideally cross-shore, with rescue cover.

Worked example · the wind at Quest's Palk Bay school
Season (Quest)WindForce on a 6 m² sail (at the wind speed, illustrative)
Dec–Mar, afternoons10–15 kn≈ 97–219 N

0.5 × 1.225 × (25 × 0.514)² × 6 ≈ 608 N. Stronger seasons suit planing and smaller sails; lighter winds suit first lessons.

  • Self-rescue: if you can't sail back, you can drop or de-rig the sail onto the board and paddle in. Quest's Level 1 course includes self-rescue drills.
  • Swimming: Quest asks Level 1 students to swim 100 m unaided, or 200 m with a personal flotation device.
  • Stay with the board. It floats, and it's easier to spot than a swimmer.
  • Monsoon: on the west coast, Goa's water-sports policy bars water sports in the monsoon, and in 2026 Uttara Kannada (Karwar) banned sea water sports from 1 June to 31 August.

Learn with a school

Take lessons at a recognised centre with a qualified instructor and a rescue boat, wear a buoyancy aid while learning, and tell someone ashore when you go out.

A gust takes the wind from 15 to 21 kn. About how much more sail force?

(21 ÷ 15)² ≈ 1.96 times: nearly double.

04 · Governing bodies

Who sets the rules

Olympic windsurfing comes under World Sailing, and the IWA represents the windsurfing classes; training schemes come from bodies such as the RYA and VDWS; in India, competition runs through the Yachting Association of India, with state associations and Karnataka's state academy running training.
