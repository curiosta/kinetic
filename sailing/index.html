CH 1

How a sail makes lift

Upwind, a sail isn't pushed. It's a wing standing on its end.

A curved sail set at a small angle to the wind turns the airflow. To bend the air around it, the sail needs lower pressure on its curved, downwind (leeward) side and higher pressure on its windward side; that pressure difference is the sail's force, called lift even though it acts sideways and forward. The air on the two sides doesn't have to "meet up" at the back: the sail simply turns the flow, like a wing. The force grows with the air's density ρ, the sail area A, the square of the wind speed V and a lift coefficient CL that depends on the sail's shape and angle: F = ½ ρ V² A CL. India's Yachting Association teaches "how sails work" in its Racing Skills course.

Worked example · a 7 m² sail, CL = 1.0, air at 1.225 kg/m³ (from our Wind & Sail Lab, illustrative)
WindIn m/s½ρV² (pressure)Force on 7 m²
6 kn3.095.8 Pa41 N
12 kn6.1723.3 Pa163 N
18 kn9.2652.5 Pa368 N

1 knot = 0.514 m/s. At 12 kn: ½ × 1.225 × 6.17² ≈ 23.3 Pa; × 7 m² ≈ 163 N. Double the wind and the force goes up four times; triple it and the force is nine times bigger.

Same 7 m² sail: what's the force at 12 kn on a 10 m² sail instead?

23.3 Pa × 10 m² ≈ 233 N. Force grows in step with area.

CH 2

Apparent wind

The wind you feel on a moving boat isn't the wind on the shore.

When you move, you feel a headwind from your own speed. On board, it adds to the true wind (the wind a moored boat feels) to make the apparent wind, and that's the wind your sails actually use. It's a vector sum: AWS = √(TWS² + BS² + 2·TWS·BS·cos TWA), where TWS is the true wind speed, BS the boat speed and TWA the angle between your course and the true wind. Speeding up always swings the apparent wind forward, so you trim your sails in as you accelerate. Sailing upwind, the apparent wind is stronger than the true wind; sailing downwind, it's weaker.

Worked example · 12 kn of true wind from the side (a beam reach, 90°), from our Wind & Sail Lab
Boat speedApparent wind speedApparent wind angle
0 kn12.0 kn90°
3 kn12.4 kn76°
5 kn13.0 kn67°
8 kn14.4 kn56°

At 90°, cos TWA = 0, so AWS = √(12² + 5²) = 13.0 kn, and the angle is cos⁻¹(5 ÷ 13) ≈ 67°. The faster the boat, the further forward the wind.

True wind 8 kn at 90°, boat speed 6 kn: apparent wind speed?

√(8² + 6²) = √100 = 10 kn, at cos⁻¹(6 ÷ 10) ≈ 53°.

CH 3

Points of sail, tacking & gybing

You can't sail straight into the wind, so you zigzag.

The sail force acts at right angles to the apparent wind, so only part of it drives you forward: drive = F × sin(apparent angle). Point too close to the wind and the drive vanishes; the sails flap and the boat stops in the no-go zone (stuck there, you're "in irons"). Our examples use 45° either side of the wind, a typical figure for a dinghy. Bearing away, you pass through close-hauled, close reach, beam reach (wind at 90°), broad reach and the run (wind from behind). To get upwind you beat, zigzagging and turning the bow through the wind (a tack). Downwind, turning the stern through the wind is a gybe: the boom swings across fast, so the crew must duck and the helm must control it. YAI's Basic Skills 2 course covers tacking and gybing on a triangle course.

Worked example · reaching a mark 1 km straight upwind (illustrative boat speeds)
Angle to the true windDistance sailedBoat speedSpeed made good upwind
40°1.31 km3.6 kn2.76 kn
45°1.41 km4.0 kn2.83 kn
50°1.56 km4.3 kn2.76 kn
60°2.00 km4.8 kn2.40 kn

Distance = 1 km ÷ cos(angle); speed made good (VMG) = boat speed × cos(angle). Pointing higher shortens the path but slows the boat; sailing lower is faster but longer. Here, 45° wins.

When two boats meet on opposite tacks while racing, the Racing Rules of Sailing (Rule 10) say the boat on port tack (wind over its left side) keeps clear of the boat on starboard tack. YAI teaches Rule 10 and its neighbours in its Racing Skills course.

How far do you sail to reach a mark 2 km upwind, tacking at 45°?

2 ÷ cos 45° = 2 × 1.41 ≈ 2.83 km.

CH 4

Centreboard, keel & sideways slip

The sail pulls sideways. A fin underwater says no.

Close-hauled, more of the sail's force pushes sideways than forward. Without something to resist it, the boat would just slide downwind. A dinghy's centreboard (or daggerboard) and a yacht's keel are underwater wings: the boat slips sideways a little (leeway), so water meets the board at a small angle, and the board makes lift that opposes the sail's sideways pull. Water is about 840 times denser than air (1025 vs 1.225 kg/m³), so a board much smaller than the sail can balance it, but only once the boat is moving: like the sail's, the board's force grows with the square of the speed. Downwind there's little sideways force, so dinghy sailors raise the board part of the way to cut drag.

Worked example · a 0.25 m² board with a lift coefficient of 0.3 (illustrative)
Boat speedIn m/s½ρV² in sea waterSideways force the board can make
1 kn0.51136 Pa10 N
3 kn1.541,220 Pa92 N
5 kn2.573,391 Pa254 N

Force = ½ × 1025 × V² × 0.25 m² × 0.3. Compare the air: 13 kn of apparent wind gives only 27 Pa, about 124 times less than water at 5 kn. At 1 kn the board can't hold the sail's pull (74 N on our beam reach, more close-hauled), so a boat that has stalled slides sideways until it builds speed again.

What sideways force can the same board make at 2 kn (1.03 m/s)?

½ × 1025 × 1.03² ≈ 543 Pa; × 0.25 × 0.3 ≈ 41 N.

CH 5

Heeling, hiking & capsize

The sail pushes high up, the board pushes low down: the boat wants to tip.

The sail's sideways force acts high on the mast, and the board's opposing force acts low in the water. Together they make a heeling moment: force × the height between them. In a dinghy, the crew's weight is the main thing that pushes back (the righting moment), so you sit on the windward side and lean out (hike), or stand out on a wire (trapeze), which YAI teaches in Basic Skills 3. When a gust hits, you can also ease the sheet to spill wind. If the heeling moment wins, you capsize.

Worked example · a 7 m² sail, heeling force 2.5 m above the board, crew 1 m out (from our Wind & Sail Lab, illustrative)
SituationHeeling forceHeeling momentCrew weight to balance
12 kn wind, beam reach, 5 kn74 N185 N·m19 kg
12 kn wind, close-hauled (45°), 5 kn244 N610 N·m62 kg
18 kn wind, close-hauled (45°), 5 kn439 N1,098 N·m112 kg

Weight = moment ÷ (9.81 × lever). 244 N × 2.5 m = 610 N·m; ÷ (9.81 × 1 m) ≈ 62 kg. In 18 kn, one sailor sitting 1 m out isn't enough: hike further out, trapeze, ease the sheet, or stay ashore.

Why a dinghy rights (or doesn't) without a keel

A yacht's lead keel puts the centre of mass deep under the water, so heel lifts that weight and creates a strong righting moment even with nobody on board. A dinghy is different: almost all the righting comes from crew weight on the windward side (and a little from the hull's form as it heels and immerses more beam). That is why hiking and trapezing matter so much on a Laser, 420 or Optimist, and why an empty dinghy blown over stays over until someone climbs on the centreboard.

Worked example · same heeling moment, keel vs dinghy (illustrative)
BoatHow it rightsRough righting available at 20° heel
Keelboat, 1,000 kg bulb 1.5 m below waterlineBallast pendulum≈ 1,000 × 9.81 × 1.5 × sin 20° ≈ 5.0 kN·m
Dinghy, 70 kg crew hiking 1.2 m outCrew on the rail≈ 70 × 9.81 × 1.2 × cos 20° ≈ 0.77 kN·m

The keelboat's ballast alone dwarfs one hiker; a dinghy needs the crew outboard, or a trapeze, or an eased sheet. Numbers are teaching models, not a particular class.

Learn capsize recovery before you need it

Capsizing is a normal part of dinghy sailing, and courses teach it early: RYA Level 2 includes capsize recovery, and YAI's syllabus moves from actions on capsize to man-overboard drills, a fully inverted ("turtle") boat and what to do if a crew member is trapped underneath. Under a turtled hull an air pocket can linger, but you cannot count on it — get out, stay with the boat, and follow the drill you practised. YAI's syllabus starts with clothing and safety equipment, including a buoyancy aid, and teaches you to find and read weather forecasts. Wear a buoyancy aid every time.

With a heeling moment of 610 N·m, how much crew weight balances it sitting 1.5 m out?

610 ÷ (9.81 × 1.5) ≈ 41 kg. A longer lever needs less weight, which is why a trapeze works.

CH 6

Hull speed, planing & the boats

A heavy hull gets trapped in its own wave. A light one climbs out.

A boat pushing through the water makes a bow wave. Deep-water waves travel at v = √(gλ ÷ 2π), so a wave as long as the boat's waterline moves at about 1.25 × √L m/s, or 2.43 × √L knots (the old rule: 1.34 × √(length in feet)). At this hull speed the boat sits in a trough between its own bow and stern waves, and going faster means climbing the bow wave. A heavy displacement hull rarely can. A light dinghy with enough power can lift onto the water's surface and skim on it: planing. Racing dinghies and skiffs are built light for exactly this.

Worked example · hull speed for different waterline lengths
Waterline lengthWave speed (m/s)Hull speed
3 m2.164.2 kn
4 m2.504.9 kn
5 m2.795.4 kn
6 m3.066.0 kn

√(9.81 × 4 ÷ 6.283) ≈ 2.50 m/s ≈ 4.9 kn. Doubling the length raises hull speed by only √2 ≈ 1.41 times.

Froude number: when a dinghy starts to climb its wave

Naval architects track Fr = v ÷ √(g L). Around Fr ≈ 0.4 the bow wave is about as long as the waterline — hull speed. A light dinghy that can reach Fr ≈ 0.5–0.7 begins to climb onto that wave and plane; a heavy keelboat usually cannot. (The same Fr idea shows up for swimmers on our swimming page.)

Worked example · 4 m waterline dinghy (hull speed ≈ 4.9 kn from the table above)
Boat speedFr = v ÷ √(gL)What it feels like
3.0 kn (1.54 m/s)≈ 0.25Displacement: pushing a bow wave ahead
4.9 kn (2.50 m/s)≈ 0.40Hull speed: sitting between bow and stern wave
7.0 kn (3.60 m/s)≈ 0.57Climbing out: planing if the boat is light enough

√(9.81 × 4) ≈ 6.26 m/s. Fr = 2.50 ÷ 6.26 ≈ 0.40 at hull speed; Fr = 3.60 ÷ 6.26 ≈ 0.57 at 7 kn. Flat sections, light weight and enough sail power (or a kite / foil) decide whether you get there.

The Olympic boats

At the LA28 Olympics, World Sailing's programme has ten sailing events. Six are boats at the Port of Los Angeles: the ILCA 7 (men) and ILCA 6 (women) one-person dinghies, the mixed 470 dinghy, the mixed Nacra 17 catamaran, and the 49er and 49erFX skiffs. The other four are boards at Long Beach (iQFOiL windsurfing and Formula Kite); see our Windsurfing and Kitesurfing pages.

What's the hull speed of a 9 m waterline?

2.43 × √9 = 2.43 × 3 ≈ 7.3 kn.

04 · Governing bodies

Who sets the rules

World Sailing governs the sport worldwide and writes the racing rules; national bodies run training and championships. In India that's the Yachting Association of India, with state associations and academies below it. We checked each on an official source.

World Sailing

The world governing body: it publishes the Racing Rules of Sailing every four years (the current edition is RRS 2025–2028) and runs Olympic sailing.
