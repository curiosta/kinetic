CH 1

The overhead: no straight way up

In open water, a problem can end with a slow swim to the surface. In a cave, the only way out is the way you came in.

Cave divers call any place with rock or ice above them an overhead environment. The cavern is the entrance zone you can still see daylight from; beyond it is the cave, with no natural light at all. Training agencies set firm limits for each zone. TDI's Cavern Diver course, for example, limits graduates to 61 linear metres (200 ft) from the surface, a maximum depth of 40 m, a continuous guideline, and no restrictions or areas too small for two divers to swim side by side.

Worked example · how far "out" really is (example swim speed 10 m per minute)
Distance insideSwim out at 10 m/minAt 5 m/min (zero visibility, on the line)
30 m3 min6 min
61 m (TDI cavern limit)≈ 6 min≈ 12 min
200 m20 min40 min

Time = distance ÷ speed: 61 ÷ 10 ≈ 6 min. Halving your speed doubles the time, and every one of those minutes uses gas at depth (Ch 2). The swim speeds are examples, not standards.

Why cave divers train in steps

  • Accident analysis: the NSS Cave Diving Section says analysing accidents produced safety rules that greatly improved survival, published in Sheck Exley's Basic Cave Diving: Blueprint for Survival (1979).
  • The results: the same NSS-CDS history records cave-diving deaths falling from an all-time high of 26 in 1974 to fewer than five a year after its training programme began.
  • Step by step: TDI's path runs Cavern → Intro to Cave → Full Cave, each with its own limits (see the ladder below).

Safety Open-water training isn't cave training

An open-water card doesn't cover overhead diving. Don't swim into a cavern or cave, even "just to look", without cave training and a guideline.

You're 120 m inside a cave and visibility drops to zero, halving your speed from 8 to 4 m per minute. How long is the swim out?

120 ÷ 4 = 30 minutes, instead of 15 at normal speed.

CH 2

Pressure & gas use

At 30 m every breath takes about four times the gas it does at the surface.

Water pressure adds about 1 bar for every 10 m of sea water, or about 10.2 m of fresh water (many caves are fresh). Your regulator delivers gas at the surrounding pressure, so by Boyle's law each lungful holds more gas the deeper you are: gas use at depth = surface rate × absolute pressure, with P ≈ 1 + depth ÷ 10.2 bar in fresh water.

Worked example · gas use in fresh water (example surface rate 20 L/min)
DepthAbsolute pressureGas useMinutes from a 12 L cylinder at 200 bar (2,400 L)
Surface1.0 bar20 L/min120 min
10 m≈ 2.0 bar≈ 40 L/min≈ 60 min
20 m≈ 3.0 bar≈ 59 L/min≈ 41 min
30 m≈ 3.9 bar≈ 79 L/min≈ 30 min
40 m≈ 4.9 bar≈ 98 L/min≈ 24 min

30 m: P = 1 + 30 ÷ 10.2 ≈ 3.94 bar; 20 × 3.94 ≈ 79 L/min; 2,400 ÷ 79 ≈ 30 min. That's the whole cylinder: with the rule of thirds (Ch 3) you'd turn after about 10. Treating gas as ideal is a small simplification at 200 bar.

What changes your gas use

  • Depth: the biggest factor, straight from the pressure.
  • Effort and cold: swimming against flow, task loading and shivering all raise your breathing rate.
  • Stress: fast, shallow breathing burns gas when you can least afford it. TDI's courses include stress management and breathing techniques.

CH 3

The rule of thirds

One third in, one third out, one third for the problem you didn't plan for.

By 1968 Sheck Exley had proposed the rule of thirds, which the NSS Cave Diving Section says is still used today: you turn the dive when you've used a third of your starting gas. TDI's cavern and intro-to-cave limits write it as penetration limited to "the rule of thirds," or 1/6th if using doubles; its Full Cave level keeps the rule of thirds. The third you keep in reserve is for emergencies, most of all a buddy who loses their gas.

Worked example · turn pressures and the shared exit
StartA third (rounded down)Turn atLeft at the turn
200 bar66 bar134 bar134 bar (two-thirds)
230 bar76 bar154 bar154 bar

Shared exit, same depth and same time out: you used one third getting in. If your buddy loses all their gas at the turn and breathes exactly as you do, two of you need 2 × ⅓ = ⅔ for the exit: everything you have left, with nothing spare. If your buddy breathes 25% faster, you'd need ⅓ × (1 + 1.25) = 0.75 of your gas: more than you have. That's why thirds is a minimum, and why dissimilar cylinder sizes need their own sums (TDI teaches gas management "to include dissimilar volumes").

Using the rule

  • Turn on the first signal: whoever reaches their turn pressure first turns the whole team.
  • Round in the safe direction: round the third down, so the turn pressure is higher.
  • Watch the flow: thirds assumes the swim out takes as long as the swim in. If you swim in with the flow, the exit is against it and takes longer, so the same gas buys less distance home.

Safety Thirds assumes things go to plan on the way out

Lost line, silt-out and stress all slow the exit and raise breathing. The reserve is there for those, not for extending the dive.

You start at 210 bar. What's your turn pressure on thirds?

210 ÷ 3 = 70, so turn at 140 bar.

Sources: NSS-CDS history · TDI Intro to Cave Diver · TDI Full Cave Diver

CH 4

Guidelines, silt & zero visibility

One careless fin kick can turn crystal-clear water into brown fog that takes hours to settle.

Cave floors are often covered in fine silt and clay. Stirred up, the particles sink very slowly. Stokes' law gives the settling speed of a small grain in still water: v = 2 (ρgrain − ρwater) g r² ÷ 9μ. Because v depends on the radius squared, a grain ten times smaller sinks a hundred times slower.

Worked example · settling speed in still water at about 20 °C (mineral grains, 2,650 kg/m³)
Grain diameterSettling speedTime to fall 1 m
200 µm (fine sand)≈ 3.6 cm/s*≈ 30 s
20 µm (silt)≈ 0.36 mm/s≈ 46 min
2 µm (clay)≈ 3.6 µm/s≈ 3 days

20 µm: r = 10 µm; v = 2 × 1,650 × 9.81 × (10⁻⁵)² ÷ (9 × 0.001) ≈ 3.6 × 10⁻⁴ m/s; 1 m ÷ 0.00036 m/s ≈ 2,800 s ≈ 46 min. *Stokes' law only holds for slow, tiny grains; it overestimates the fine-sand speed. Cold water is more viscous, so everything settles even more slowly.

How cave divers deal with it

  • A continuous guideline to open water: TDI requires it at every cave level; divers practise following the line with their eyes closed and sharing air by touch in the dark.
  • Line markers: the NSS-CDS history describes how arrow-shaped markers on the line came to point the way to the nearest exit.
  • Anti-silting finning: TDI's courses train finning and trim that keep water moving up and back, not down into the floor.
  • Lights: TDI's skills include simulating a primary light failure and exiting on a backup light.

Feel the numberClay-sized silt falls about a third of a metre a day. Stir up a passage and the next team may find it still cloudy.
Grain A is 40 µm across, grain B is 4 µm. How much faster does A settle?

Speed ∝ r², so (40 ÷ 4)² = 100 times faster.

CH 5

Depth: dense gas & the 40 m limit

Deeper gas is thicker gas: harder to breathe, and with more nitrogen pressing on your brain.

Air at the surface has a density of about 1.2 kg/m³ (the NASA standard atmosphere gives 1.225 at 15 °C). Compress it and density rises with pressure: at 40 m in fresh water (≈ 4.9 bar) your air is nearly five times as dense, so every breath takes more work. The partial pressure of each gas rises too: pgas = fraction × absolute pressure. TDI's cavern, intro and full cave levels all set a maximum depth of 40 m, and Tech Dive Thailand's deep cave course uses trimix because, in its words, narcosis is an issue to avoid in this kind of overhead environment.

Worked example · breathing air in fresh water
DepthAbsolute pressureAir densityNitrogen partial pressure (79%)Oxygen partial pressure (21%)
Surface1.0 bar≈ 1.2 kg/m³0.79 bar0.21 bar
20 m≈ 3.0 bar≈ 3.6 kg/m³≈ 2.3 bar≈ 0.62 bar
40 m≈ 4.9 bar≈ 6.0 kg/m³≈ 3.9 bar≈ 1.03 bar

40 m: P = 1 + 40 ÷ 10.2 ≈ 4.92 bar; density ≈ 1.225 × 4.92 ≈ 6.0 kg/m³; pN₂ ≈ 0.79 × 4.92 ≈ 3.9 bar; pO₂ ≈ 0.21 × 4.92 ≈ 1.03 bar.

Why depth limits matter more in caves

  • Clear thinking: following a line, counting gas and solving problems all need a clear head; nitrogen narcosis dulls it.
  • Harder breathing: denser gas means more effort per breath, which raises breathing rate and gas use.
  • Decompression: deeper and longer dives need decompression stops; TDI's Full Cave course needs separate decompression training if decompression dives are part of it.
What's the oxygen partial pressure of air at 30 m in fresh water?

P ≈ 3.94 bar; 0.21 × 3.94 ≈ 0.83 bar.

Sources: NASA Glenn: Earth atmosphere model · TDI Full Cave Diver · Tech Dive Thailand

CH 6

Buoyancy & trim under a ceiling

Rise a few metres too fast in open water and you surface. In a cave you hit the roof.

Archimedes: you float when the water you displace weighs as much as you and your kit. A buoyancy compensator (BC) adds lift with a bubble of gas, and by Boyle's law that bubble shrinks as you go down and grows as you go up. Near the surface the change is largest: the bubble doubles in size between 10 m and the surface.

Worked example · 2 litres of gas in the BC, fresh water
Start depthRise toVolume becomesExtra lift
30 m (3.94 bar)20 m (2.96 bar)≈ 2.7 L≈ 0.7 kg
10 m (1.98 bar)Surface (1 bar)≈ 4.0 L≈ 2 kg

P₁V₁ = P₂V₂: 2 × 3.94 ÷ 2.96 ≈ 2.66 L. Each extra litre displaces about 1 kg of fresh water. Unless you vent as you rise, a small ascent speeds itself up.

Trim and drag

  • Horizontal trim: a flat, streamlined body presents less frontal area, so water drag (∝ area × speed²) is lower and your fins stay off the floor.
  • Stay neutral: small, early adjustments beat big corrections. TDI's skills include buoyancy control, proper trim and propulsion techniques.
  • No dangling kit: anything hanging can snag the line or the rock.
✋
Feel the numberHalf a litre of extra gas is about half a kilogram of lift: enough to drift you up into the ceiling in a minute if you don't notice.
1 litre of gas in your BC at 20 m (2.96 bar). What's its volume at 5 m (1.49 bar)?

1 × 2.96 ÷ 1.49 ≈ 2 litres: about 1 kg more lift.

04 · Governing bodies

Who sets the rules

Cave diving has no Olympic-style federation. Its rules come from the world underwater federation's standards, training agencies' course standards and cave-diving organisations' safety work. We checked each on its own official website.

Global

World federation

CMAS, the World Underwater Federation

Publishes international diver-training standards, including Cave Diver Level II (Apprentice Cave Diver) and three cave-diving instructor levels.

cmas.org
Training agency

Technical Diving International (TDI)

Runs the Cavern Diver, Intro to Cave Diver and Full Cave Diver courses, each with published prerequisites and limits.

tdisdi.com
Cave-diving organisation

NSS Cave Diving Section (NSS-CDS)

Founded in 1973 by cave-diving members of the US National Speleological Society. Its accident analysis produced the safety rules published in Basic Cave Diving: Blueprint for Survival (1979).
