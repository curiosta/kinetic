CH 1

Energy systems & fuelling

Your legs can go all day. Your carbohydrate stores can't.

Long triathlons are aerobic: muscles burn a mix of fat and carbohydrate with oxygen. Fat stores are huge, but the harder you go, the more of your energy comes from carbohydrate, and the body only stores a few hundred grams of it as glycogen in muscle and liver (an illustrative ballpark: about 500 g, roughly 2,000 kcal, for a trained athlete). Run low and your pace drops sharply: what endurance athletes call "hitting the wall".

Your muscles are only about a quarter efficient (we use 23%, illustrative): to put 180 W into the pedals your body burns about 180 ÷ 0.23 ≈ 780 W of food energy.

Worked example · 180 W on the bike (illustrative values)
ItemNumberHow
Food energy burned≈ 673 kcal/h180 ÷ 0.23 × 3,600 s ÷ 4,184 J/kcal
From carbohydrate (60%)≈ 101 g/h673 × 0.6 ÷ 4 kcal/g
Eating 60 g/h≈ 41 g/h shortcovered by glycogen
500 g of glycogen at 41 g/h≈ 12 hbut the run burns more per hour than the bike

The ratio of fat to carbohydrate changes with intensity and training, so the 60% is a teaching number, not your number.

What the guidelines say

The joint position statement on nutrition and athletic performance from the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, Dietitians of Canada and the American College of Sports Medicine (2016) suggests 30–60 g of carbohydrate per hour during endurance exercise of 1–2.5 h, and up to 90 g/h for ultra-endurance exercise over 2.5–3 h, using glucose-fructose mixtures ("multiple transportable carbohydrates") that the gut can absorb faster. For events longer than 90 minutes it describes carbohydrate loading of 10–12 g per kg of body mass per day for the 36–48 hours before.

🍌
Feel the number90 g of carbohydrate an hour is roughly what three energy gels or a few bananas give you (illustrative), every hour, for many hours, while racing. Guts can be trained: practise race fuelling in long training sessions, never for the first time on race day.
At 200 W with the same assumptions, roughly how many grams of carbohydrate do you burn per hour?

200 ÷ 0.23 × 3,600 ÷ 4,184 ≈ 748 kcal/h; × 0.6 ÷ 4 ≈ 112 g/h.

CH 2

Pacing & power

Going a little harder costs a lot more than it gives.

On a flat road most of your power goes into pushing air, and air drag power grows with the cube of speed: Pair = ½ ρ CdA v³. Ride 10% faster and you need about 33% more power for the air part. That's why steady, even pacing beats surges: every surge burns extra carbohydrate (Ch 1) for a small time gain, and you still have to run afterwards.

Worked example · 90 km in the hoods, CdA 0.32 m² (from our Race-Day Lab)
PowerSpeed90 km splitCarbohydrate burned
150 W≈ 30.7 km/h≈ 2:56≈ 84 g/h
180 W≈ 32.9 km/h≈ 2:44≈ 101 g/h
200 W≈ 34.2 km/h≈ 2:38≈ 112 g/h

From 150 W to 200 W is 33% more power for about 11% more speed: you save about 18 minutes but burn about a third more carbohydrate every hour.

Pacing a whole race

  • Swim: start controlled; a wild first 200 m spikes your heart rate for little gain.
  • Bike: ride steady power, ease off on descents and don't spike up climbs; a power meter or heart rate helps.
  • Run: the race often starts here. Many coaches suggest running the first kilometres slightly slower than feels right.
If air drag were all of the power, how much more power would 20% more speed need?

1.2³ ≈ 1.73, so about 73% more.

CH 3

Swim drag & wetsuit buoyancy

Water is 800 times denser than air. Position beats power.

Drag in water follows the same law as in air, F = ½ ρ CdA v², but with ρ ≈ 1,000 kg/m³ instead of 1.2. Legs that sink add frontal area and drag, so a high, flat body line matters more than raw strength. Our Swimming page covers drag, buoyancy and open-water skills in detail.

A wetsuit is closed-cell foam, full of tiny gas bubbles. It lifts the hips and legs, which cuts drag, and keeps you warm. That's why rules limit when you may wear one.

Worked example · wetsuit lift (illustrative values)
ItemNumberHow
Neoprene volume≈ 5.4 L1.8 m² of suit × 3 mm average thickness
Water it displaces≈ 5.4 kg1 L of water ≈ 1 kg
Suit's own mass≈ 1.1 kgfoam about 0.2 kg per litre
Net extra lift≈ 4.3 kg5.4 − 1.1, mostly at hips and legs

How much does a wetsuit really help? A measurement

Dutch researchers measured the drag on 12 swimmers (eight men, four women) doing front crawl over a row of instrumented push-off pads, with and without a triathlon wetsuit (Toussaint et al., Med Sci Sports Exerc, 1989). At 1.25 m/s, a typical triathlon swim speed, the wetsuit cut drag by 14%, from 48.7 N to 41.8 N; at 1.50 m/s by 12%. Most of that came from extra buoyancy (the lift in the table above), but the suit helped the lighter women as much as the heavier men, so a smoother surface probably helps too.

Worked example · same effort, 14% less drag (simple model: drag ∝ v², power = drag × speed)
SwimNo wetsuit, 1.25 m/sWetsuit, same powerTime saved
Sprint, 750 m10:00≈ 9:30≈ 30 s
Olympic, 1.5 km20:00≈ 19:00≈ 1 min
70.3, 1.9 km25:20≈ 24:04≈ 1 min 15 s
Full, 3.8 km50:40≈ 48:09≈ 2 min 30 s

Power to beat drag = F × v = 48.7 × 1.25 ≈ 61 W without the suit, 41.8 × 1.25 ≈ 52 W with it. If drag grows with v², power grows with v³, so at the same 61 W the wetsuit swimmer goes (48.7 ÷ 41.8)1/3 ≈ 1.052 times faster: about 1.32 m/s. Real swimmers also change their stroke in a suit, so treat this as a rough guide; it shows why rules decide who may wear one.

Wetsuit rules (IRONMAN 2026 Competition Rules)

  • Age-group athletes may wear wetsuits in water up to and including 24.5 °C.
  • From 24.5 °C to 28.8 °C, IRONMAN may at some events allow a separate non-competitive wetsuit wave, whose athletes aren't eligible for awards or world championship slots.
  • Wetsuits are prohibited above 28.8 °C; for professionals the limit is 21.9 °C. Thickness may not exceed 5 mm.

World Triathlon events set their own temperature limits in the World Triathlon Competition Rules; check the athlete guide for your race.

Feel the numberDoubling your swim speed needs four times the drag force and eight times the power. A small gain in body position is worth a lot of fitness.
Water at 26 °C, an age-group athlete, IRONMAN rules: can you race for an age-group award in a wetsuit?

No. 26 °C is above 24.5 °C; at most, the race may offer a non-competitive wetsuit wave without awards or slots.

CH 4

Drafting rules & bike aerodynamics

Your body is most of the drag. CdA is the number that matters.

CdA (drag coefficient × frontal area, in m²) sums up how "slippery" you and your bike are. Folding down onto aero bars shrinks your frontal area and smooths the airflow. Our Cycling & MTB page works through rolling resistance, climbing and power in more detail.

Worked example · 180 W, 80 kg, flat road (from our Race-Day Lab)
Position (illustrative CdA)Speed90 km180 km
Upright, 0.40 m²≈ 30.6 km/h≈ 2:56≈ 5:53
Hoods, 0.32 m²≈ 32.9 km/h≈ 2:44≈ 5:29
Aero bars, 0.25 m²≈ 35.5 km/h≈ 2:32≈ 5:04

Same power, same legs: the aero position saves about 49 minutes over 180 km compared with sitting up. Hold it only as long as you can stay comfortable and in control.

Why drafting is banned in most age-group racing

Riding close behind another rider puts you in their slipstream and can cut your drag a lot, so non-drafting races keep riders apart. World Triathlon's updated competition rules unified the draft zone to 12 m, with 25 s to pass; IRONMAN's 2026 rules use 12 m (about six bike lengths of clear space) and 25 s for age-group athletes, and 20 m for professionals. Once you enter the zone you must keep moving through it; if you're passed, you must drop back out of the passer's zone.

How big is the slipstream? A computer-simulation answer

Blocken and colleagues (TU Eindhoven, KU Leuven and ETH Zürich) simulated the airflow around two riders, checked against wind-tunnel tests, with the riders 1 cm to 1 m apart (Computers & Fluids, 2013). In the time-trial position (the one triathletes use), the rider behind had 13.8% less drag at 1 cm, still 11.7% less at 1 m. Upright, the saving was up to 27%. Surprisingly, the rider in front gained too, up to 2.6%, because the follower fills the low-pressure pocket behind them.

Worked example · air-drag power at 40 km/h in the time-trial position (study's drag areas, air 1.2 kg/m³)
RiderDrag area CdAPower against airSaving
Riding alone0.135 m²≈ 111 Wn/a
Following at 1 m0.119 m²≈ 98 W≈ 13 W
Following at 1 cm0.116 m²≈ 95 W≈ 16 W
Leading, follower at 1 cm0.131 m²≈ 108 W≈ 3 W

P = ½ ρ CdA v³ = 0.5 × 1.2 × 0.135 × 11.11³ ≈ 111 W. Rolling resistance (not shown) is unchanged by drafting. The study's rider was a single person in a tight position, so your own numbers will differ; the study didn't test gaps beyond 1 m. The 12 m draft zone keeps age-groupers far outside the gaps it measured.

🚴
Feel the numberTo clear 12 m in 25 s you need to ride at least 12 ÷ 25 = 0.48 m/s (about 1.7 km/h) faster than the rider ahead. At 33 km/h in the hoods that takes roughly 27 W more for those 25 seconds.
A pass takes you 30 s through someone's 12 m draft zone. What's wrong?

The limit is 25 s: that would be a drafting violation and a time penalty.

CH 5

Running economy on tired legs

The run is a different sport when you start it after the bike.

Running costs energy roughly in proportion to your body mass and distance. A common rule of thumb (illustrative) is about 1 kcal per kg per km, nearly whatever your pace. Off the bike, legs feel heavy and stiff for the first kilometres, and tired muscles can make each stride slightly less economical. Our Running page covers pace, cadence and heat in more detail.

Worked example · a 70 kg athlete (illustrative values)
RunEnergy (1 kcal/kg/km)If 5% less economical
Sprint, 5 km≈ 350 kcal≈ 368 kcal
Standard, 10 km≈ 700 kcal≈ 735 kcal
70.3, 21.1 km≈ 1,477 kcal≈ 1,551 kcal
Full, 42.2 km≈ 2,954 kcal≈ 3,102 kcal

A full-distance marathon alone can burn more energy than your glycogen holds (Ch 1), which is why fuelling on the bike matters for the run.

  • Brick sessions (a run straight after a ride) train the switch from cycling to running.
  • Short, quick steps off the bike, then settle into your normal stride.
  • Walk the aid stations if that's what lets you drink and eat properly.
A 60 kg athlete runs the 10 km of a standard triathlon. About how much energy?

60 × 10 × 1 ≈ 600 kcal.

CH 6

Heat, hydration & transitions

Only about a quarter of your energy moves you. The rest is heat.

If 23% of the energy you burn becomes motion (Ch 1), about 77% becomes heat. Your body sheds it mainly by sweating: evaporating sweat carries heat away, and in humid air it evaporates less. The 2016 position statement gives sweat rates of 0.3–2.4 L per hour, depending on intensity, fitness, heat acclimatisation and weather, and suggests drinking enough to keep the total body-fluid deficit under 2% of body mass, because bigger deficits can impair thinking and performance, especially in heat.

Worked example · heat and sweat (illustrative values)
ItemNumberHow
Heat made at 180 W≈ 600 W780 W burned × 0.77

Drinking far more than you sweat is risky too. Find your own sweat rate by weighing yourself before and after training sessions.

Transitions: the fourth discipline

  • T1 (swim to bike) and T2 (bike to run) count in your race time.
  • IRONMAN's 2026 rules: mount and dismount only at the marked lines; no stopping in the flow zones; keep your kit in your bag or spot; don't touch other athletes' kit.
  • Your helmet must be fastened from the moment you take your bike off the rack until you rack it again.

Race safely

Get a medical check before you train for a long-distance race, build up gradually, practise open-water swimming with others and with water-safety cover, and know the race's cut-off times and medical support. In heat, slow down early rather than late.

T1 takes 4 min and T2 takes 3 min. You cut both to 2 min. How much time do you save?

(4 + 3) − (2 + 2) = 3 minutes, with no extra fitness.

04 · Governing bodies

Who sets the rules

Triathlon's rules come from World Triathlon; in India the national federation runs the sport. IRONMAN is different: a commercial race organiser with its own rules, not a governing body. We checked each on an official website.

World Triathlon

The international federation for triathlon and its multisport family (duathlon, aquathlon, cross and long-distance triathlon). Sets the Competition Rules, including the unified 12 m / 25 s draft zone, and runs age-group world championships through national federations.
