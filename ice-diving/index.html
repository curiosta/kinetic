CH 1

Ice above, ice underfoot

The ice is your roof, your dive platform and the only way back in, so it gets measured before anyone kits up.

Ice floats because water expands as it freezes: the USGS says its density drops by about 9 percent, so roughly 90 percent of a floating block sits below the waterline. For divers that means a solid lid. CMAS's ice-diving standard says diving under ice must be treated as an overhead environment, very close to the cavern zone of a cave, and it requires trained, secured staff to measure the ice's thickness before any course activity.

Worked example · the Minnesota DNR's guideline for new, clear ice (white ice: double it)
Clear iceAboutDNR guidelineWhite ice needed for the same
Under 4 inunder 10 cmStay off—
4 in10 cmIce fishing or other activities on foot≈ 20 cm
5–7 in13–18 cmSnowmobile or small ATV≈ 25–36 cm

1 inch = 2.54 cm, so 4 in ≈ 10.2 cm. The DNR says white ice (snow that melted and refroze) is only about half as strong as new clear ice, and that ice can be 60 cm thick in one place and 2.5 cm a few metres away.

What the overhead changes

  • One way out: the entry hole. Divers stay tied to it with a line held by a tender on the surface (Ch 5).
  • Rule of thirds: CMAS says the rule of thirds, or a stricter one, must be applied in all overhead environments, including under ice.
  • No current: CMAS says its ice course must never be given in water with current, such as rivers or tidal flows.

Safety Never walk out on ice you haven't checked

Thickness varies across a single lake, and snow cover hides weak spots. Follow local authorities and the dive team leader.

You measure 12 cm of white ice. Does it meet the DNR's on-foot guideline?

No. White ice needs about double the 10 cm clear-ice figure: about 20 cm.

Sources: USGS: water density · Minnesota DNR: ice thickness guidelines · CMAS Ice Diving Standards (2009)

CH 2

Why the coldest water is right under the ice

Fresh water is densest near 4 °C, so the coldest water floats on top, just under the ice.

Most liquids get denser as they cool. Fresh water does too, until about 4 °C; below that it gets less dense again. The USGS explains that this is why lakes freeze from the top: water at about 4 °C sinks below water at 0 °C. So in a frozen lake the water touching the ice is at about 0 °C, and the slightly warmer, denser water lies deeper.

Worked example · density of pure water (USGS table)
TemperatureDensityCompared with 4 °C water
0 °C0.99987 g/cm³lighter: floats on top
4 °C1.00000 g/cm³densest: sinks
10 °C0.99975 g/cm³lighter
Iceabout 0.9 g/cm³floats, about 90% submerged

The differences are tiny, 0.013% between 0 °C and 4 °C, but in still water they're enough to layer a lake. Salt water behaves differently: dissolved salt changes both its density and its freezing point.

Bubbles under the lid

  • Boyle's law: the gas you breathe out at 20 m (about 3 bar in fresh water) roughly triples in volume by the time it reaches the ice.
  • It collects: with no surface to escape through, exhaled gas spreads in pockets under the ice.
  • Light: snow on the ice blocks much of the daylight; CMAS requires a chemical light stick on each diver when visibility is under 10 m or daylight is weak.

Feel the numberA litre of 4 °C water weighs about 0.13 g more than a litre of 0 °C water: less than a grain of rice, yet enough to sort a whole lake.
You exhale 1 litre of gas at 15 m in fresh water (about 2.5 bar). Roughly how big is it under the ice?

About 2.5 litres: volume × pressure stays the same (Boyle's law).

CH 3

Cold: heat loss in water and wind

Water pulls heat out of you far faster than air at the same temperature.

Heat flows from your skin into the water by conduction and convection. Water conducts heat about 23 times better than air (thermal conductivity ≈ 0.56 vs 0.024 W/m·K near 0 °C) and stores far more heat per litre, so it chills you quickly. The US CDC's NIOSH says cold-water immersion causes immersion hypothermia, which develops much more quickly than standard hypothermia, and that hypothermia can occur in any water below 70 °F (21 °C). The CMAS standard strongly advises students to use dry suits and requires instructors to.

Worked example · wind chill on the ice for the tender and waiting divers (US National Weather Service formula)
Air temperatureWind 10 km/hWind 20 km/hWind 40 km/h
−5 °C≈ −9 °C≈ −12 °C≈ −14 °C
−10 °C≈ −15 °C≈ −18 °C≈ −21 °C
−20 °C≈ −27 °C≈ −30 °C≈ −34 °C

NWS formula in °F and mph: WC = 35.74 + 0.6215T − 35.75V0.16 + 0.4275TV0.16. −10 °C (14 °F) at 20 km/h (12.4 mph) gives −0.1 °F ≈ −18 °C. The NWS notes wind chill is how cold it feels on skin; objects don't cool below the air temperature.

Staying warm enough to think

  • Dry suit: a layer of gas and insulation instead of a film of water against the skin. CMAS requires a Dry Suit Diver certificate plus 10 dry-suit dives if you use one on its course.
  • Surface team: CMAS lists a windbreak shelter, warm dry clothes and warm drinks for everyone on the ice.
  • Physiology: the CMAS course covers hypothermia, frostbite, dehydration and low blood sugar.
Water conducts heat about 23 times better than air. Why does a dry suit help so much?

It keeps a layer of gas and insulation against your skin instead of water, so heat has to cross a poor conductor to escape.

Sources: CDC NIOSH: cold-related illnesses · NWS: understanding wind chill · CMAS Ice Diving Standards (2009). Thermal conductivities are standard physical data.

CH 4

Regulators that freeze open

Expanding gas cools, and in near-freezing water that can ice up a regulator until it free-flows.

Your regulator drops gas from cylinder pressure (up to about 200 bar) to a pressure you can breathe. Gas cools as it expands, and the colder the water, the less heat is available to warm the valve parts back up. Any moisture can then freeze, jamming a valve open: a free-flow that pours out gas. CMAS lists freezing and free-flowing regulators as a core topic, says very dry gas in the tanks is of paramount importance, and requires regulators approved to the cold-water part of the EN 250 standard.

Worked example · minimum gas under the CMAS standard, and the third you can use going in (example surface rate 20 L/min at 10 m)
Set-up (CMAS minimum)Gas at 200 barOne thirdTime to the turn at 10 m (≈ 40 L/min)
Student single tank, 10 L, two outlets2,000 L≈ 667 L≈ 17 min
Student doubles, 2 × 7 L2,800 L≈ 933 L≈ 23 min
Instructor doubles, 2 × 8 L3,200 L≈ 1,067 L≈ 27 min

10 m in fresh water: P ≈ 1.98 bar; 20 × 1.98 ≈ 40 L/min; 933 ÷ 40 ≈ 23 min. A free-flow can empty a cylinder far faster than breathing does, which is why the next point matters.

How the standard handles it

  • Two complete, independent regulators per diver; CMAS forbids octopus rigs and allows only DIN fittings.
  • Separate first stages: dry-suit and BC inflators must be connected to different first stages.
  • Keep kit warm and dry before and between dives, and practise free-flow management and a shutdown drill in training.

Safety A free-flow is an out-of-gas emergency in slow motion

Trained divers switch to the other regulator, shut down the frozen one, signal and turn the dive. Learn and drill it with an ice-diving instructor.

Why does CMAS insist on two completely independent regulators rather than one first stage with two mouthpieces?

If a first stage freezes or free-flows, a second mouthpiece on it fails too. An independent system can be shut off without losing the other.

Sources: CMAS Ice Diving Standards (2009) · Shiretoko Diving Project (explains free-flow prevention for its drift-ice dives)

CH 5

Lines, holes & finding your way back

Under the ice, the hole is a tiny target. The line is how you hit it.

CMAS ice divers are tied to a tender line fixed to the ice and handled by a line tender on the surface, with at most 50 m of line between diver and tender. Communication is by line pulls and touch signals. A small heading error grows with distance: sideways miss = distance × tan(error).

Worked example · how far a compass error puts you from the hole
Distance from the hole2° error5° error10° error
10 m0.35 m0.87 m1.8 m
30 m1.0 m2.6 m5.3 m
50 m (CMAS line limit)1.7 m4.4 m8.8 m

50 × tan 5° = 50 × 0.0875 ≈ 4.4 m. Under ice you can't pop up to look, so you follow the line home.

What the standard requires

  • Tender line and back-up: per entry, one tender line for instructor and student and another for the stand-by diver, fixed with hollow ice screws.
  • Personal safety reel with at least 50 m of line, and two cutting tools per diver.
  • Lights: a main and a backup lamp, each lasting at least twice the planned dive time.
  • Lost-line drills: finding a broken or lost line, search patterns under ice, and a dive-site sketch on a wrist slate.
You're 40 m from the hole with a 5° compass error. Roughly how far off would you surface, if you could?

40 × tan 5° ≈ 3.5 m to the side: easily enough to miss the hole and hit solid ice.

Sources: CMAS Ice Diving Standards (2009). Geometry: tangent of the heading error.

CH 6

Cold, depth limits & decompression

Cold changes how your body handles dissolved gas, so ice-diving courses stay shallow and within no-stop limits.

By Henry's law, the gas your body dissolves rises with the gas's partial pressure, so deeper dives load more nitrogen. Getting rid of it depends on blood flow, and cold narrows blood vessels in your skin and limbs. CMAS lists an increased risk of decompression illness in its ice-diving physiology topics, limits its course to 30 m, allows no more than two dives a day and, citing the adverse effects of cold on circulation, strongly recommends that every course dive stays within the no-decompression limits for air.

Worked example · nitrogen partial pressure breathing air in a fresh-water lake
DepthLake at sea levelLake at 2,000 m altitude
Surface0.79 bar (1.0 bar total)0.63 bar (0.80 bar total)
10 m≈ 1.56 bar≈ 1.40 bar
30 m (CMAS course limit)≈ 3.11 bar (3.9 × the surface)≈ 2.95 bar (4.7 × the surface)

At 2,000 m the NASA standard atmosphere gives about 0.80 bar at the surface. At 30 m: 0.80 + 30 ÷ 10.2 ≈ 3.74 bar total; 0.79 × 3.74 ≈ 2.95 bar of nitrogen, 4.7 times the surface value. Bigger ratios mean sea-level tables don't apply; CMAS has a separate Altitude Diver standard.

Why the limits are conservative

  • Depth: 30 m maximum on the CMAS course.
  • No stops: all course dives within no-decompression limits, even when using nitrox.
  • One-to-one: one student per instructor during instructional dives under ice; diving alone is not permitted.
At a sea-level lake, what's the nitrogen partial pressure at 20 m breathing air?

P ≈ 1 + 20 ÷ 10.2 ≈ 2.96 bar; 0.79 × 2.96 ≈ 2.3 bar.

Sources: CMAS Ice Diving Standards (2009) · CMAS training standards (Altitude Diver) · NASA Glenn: Earth atmosphere model
