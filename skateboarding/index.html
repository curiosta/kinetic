CH 1

Pushing, rolling & coasting

Every push is a short burst of force. Then rolling resistance slowly takes it back.

When you push, your foot shoves the ground backwards and the ground shoves you forwards. Once you're coasting, rolling resistance (Crr × m g) slows you at a = Crr × g, so you roll a distance d = v² ÷ (2 Crr g) before stopping (ignoring air drag).

Worked example · coasting distance after a push (illustrative Crr)
Speed after pushingSurfaceCrrRolls about
4 m/s (14 km/h)Smooth skatepark concrete0.01≈ 82 m
4 m/sRougher concrete0.02≈ 41 m
3 m/s (11 km/h)Rougher concrete0.02≈ 23 m

4² ÷ (2 × 0.02 × 9.81) ≈ 41 m. Speed squared means a slightly harder push carries you much further. Air drag and small stones shorten real distances.

Pushing well

  • Weight on the front foot, over the front truck, so the board stays under you while the back foot pushes.
  • Short, smooth pushes beat big lunges: you stay balanced and can stop.
  • Learn to stop first: foot-brake (drag the back foot), or step off safely at low speed.
To coast twice as far, how much faster do you need to be going?

d ∝ v², so √2 ≈ 1.4 times faster.

CH 2

Balance, stance & turning

Keep your centre of mass over the board, and the board does what you ask.

You stay up as long as your centre of mass is above the area between the wheels. Drift outside it and gravity creates a turning moment that tips you off. Bent knees lower your centre of mass and give you room to correct. Whether you ride left foot forward (regular) or right foot forward (goofy) is simply what feels natural.

How trucks steer

Skateboard trucks are built so that tilting the deck swings the axles: lean toe-side or heel-side and the board carves that way. To hold a curve of radius r at speed v you must lean into it at tan φ = v² ÷ (g r).

Worked example · lean angle for a carve
SpeedCarve radiusLean angle
4 m/s (14 km/h)3 m≈ 29°
5 m/s (18 km/h)4 m≈ 33°

tan φ = 4² ÷ (9.81 × 3) ≈ 0.54 → φ ≈ 29°. Looser trucks turn more for the same lean, but wobble more at speed.

Inside the truck: tilt becomes steering

Each truck's hanger swings around a pivot axis (the kingpin direction) that is tilted at an angle λ from the deck. When you tilt the deck by β, the axle yaws by a steering angle δ, with tan δ = tan λ × sin β. This is the steering relation used in skateboard-dynamics research going back to Hubbard (1979); Varszegi and colleagues (2016) write it the same way using the complementary "rake" angle. The front and rear trucks steer in opposite directions, so with a wheelbase L between the axles the board follows a curve of radius about R ≈ L ÷ (2 tan δ).

Worked example · wheelbase 0.36 m (illustrative), no bushing squash or wheel slip
Deck tilt β50° pivot axis: steer δ50°: turn radius44° pivot axis: steer δ44°: turn radius
2°≈ 2.4°≈ 4.3 m≈ 1.9°≈ 5.3 m
5°≈ 5.9°≈ 1.7 m≈ 4.8°≈ 2.1 m
10°≈ 11.7°≈ 0.87 m≈ 9.5°≈ 1.1 m

5° tilt, 50° axis: tan δ = tan 50° × sin 5° ≈ 1.19 × 0.087 ≈ 0.104; R ≈ 0.36 ÷ (2 × 0.104) ≈ 1.7 m. A steeper pivot axis turns more for the same tilt. The 3 m carve from the table above needs only about 3° of deck tilt by this geometry, while your body leans about 29°. Deck tilt and body lean aren't the same angle. Real boards need a bit more tilt because bushings squash unevenly and wheels slip slightly.

Why the same tilt feels twitchy at speed

The truck fixes the radius for a given tilt, but the lean you need for that radius grows with v². Hold the deck at 5° (a 1.7 m curve on the 50° trucks above) and see what balance demands:

Worked example · lean needed on a 1.7 m curve, tan φ = v² ÷ (g R)
SpeedBody lean neededWhat it means
2 m/s (7 km/h)≈ 13°Easy, relaxed carve
3 m/s (11 km/h)≈ 28°A committed carve
4 m/s (14 km/h)≈ 43°Very hard to hold
5 m/s (18 km/h)≈ 56°Not realistic: you'd be thrown outward

So at speed, a few degrees of accidental tilt is a big steering input. That's why fast riders keep the deck almost flat, and why tighter bushings or a lower pivot angle feel more stable downhill.

Speed wobble is the same story with a delay in it. A 2016 study in the Journal of the Royal Society Interface (Varszegi, Takacs, Stepan and Hogan) modelled the skater's balance corrections with a human reflex delay. The range of correction strengths that keeps the board stable shrinks as speed rises, and standing ahead of the board's centre tolerates longer reflex delays than standing behind it. That matches the classic advice: weight over the front truck, smooth small corrections, and slow down before it starts.

✋
Feel the numberSpeed wobbles happen when small side-to-side oscillations grow faster than you correct them. Weight forward, knees bent and a lower speed calm them down. Over-correcting hard and late, or leaning back, usually makes them worse.
At 3 m/s, what lean angle holds a 2 m carve?

tan φ = 9 ÷ (9.81 × 2) ≈ 0.46 → φ ≈ 25°.

CH 3

The ollie

Snap, jump, slide, level. Physics does the rest.

Snapping the tail down pivots the board over the back wheels; the ground pushes back on the tail and the board pops up. You jump at the same moment and slide your front foot forward, which drags the nose up and levels the board under you. Once you're in the air you're a projectile: to clear a height H you need an upward speed of v = √(2 g H), and you'll be airborne for about t = 2v ÷ g.

Worked example · what an ollie needs (rolling at 4 m/s)
Obstacle heightTake-off speed (up)AirtimeForward distance in the air
10 cm≈ 1.40 m/s≈ 0.29 s≈ 1.1 m
20 cm≈ 1.98 m/s≈ 0.40 s≈ 1.6 m
30 cm≈ 2.43 m/s≈ 0.49 s≈ 2.0 m
50 cm≈ 3.13 m/s≈ 0.64 s≈ 2.6 m

30 cm: √(2 × 9.81 × 0.3) ≈ 2.43 m/s; airtime 2 × 2.43 ÷ 9.81 ≈ 0.49 s; 4 × 0.49 ≈ 1.98 m. Idealised: it assumes you land at take-off height and the board rises with you.

Why practice matters more than strength

Height needs √h more speed, so twice the height needs only about 1.4 times the pop. Timing (snapping the tail exactly as you jump) matters more than force. Practise standing still on grass or carpet first, then rolling slowly.

You pop with 2 m/s upward speed. How high can you clear?

H = v² ÷ (2g) = 4 ÷ 19.62 ≈ 0.20 m (20 cm).

CH 4

Ramps, bowls & pumping

A ramp swaps height for speed and back again. The curve pushes back hard.

Dropping in from height h, you reach the bottom at v = √(2 g h) (ignoring friction). Following a curve of radius r needs a centripetal force m v² ÷ r, so at the bottom of the transition the ramp pushes on you with m g (1 + 2h ÷ r): more than your weight.

Worked example · dropping into a 1.8 m-radius transition, 60 kg skater
Drop heightSpeed at the bottomG-force at the bottomPush through your legs
0.5 m≈ 11.3 km/h≈ 1.56 g≈ 916 N
1.0 m≈ 15.9 km/h≈ 2.11 g≈ 1,243 N
1.5 m≈ 19.5 km/h≈ 2.67 g≈ 1,570 N
2.0 m≈ 22.6 km/h≈ 3.22 g≈ 1,897 N

1.5 m: v = √(2 × 9.81 × 1.5) ≈ 5.42 m/s; g-load = 1 + 2 × 1.5 ÷ 1.8 ≈ 2.67; push = 60 × 9.81 × 2.67 ≈ 1,570 N. Friction makes real speeds a little lower.

Pumping

Crouch as you go into the curve, then stand up through it while the ramp is pushing hardest. You do work against that extra force and gain energy, like pumping a swing. That's how bowl skaters keep going without pushing.

Drop in small first

Commit with your weight over the front truck; leaning back is the classic fall. Start on low ramps with a helmet and pads, and give other skaters their turn.

A bigger transition radius (say 3 m) for the same 1.5 m drop: what g-force?

1 + 2 × 1.5 ÷ 3 = 2.0 g: bigger curves are gentler.

CH 5

Falling safely

Everyone falls. The trick is to stop slowly.

Average force during a stop depends on how far you take to stop: average deceleration = v² ÷ (2 × stopping distance). Falling from standing, your body (centre of mass about 1 m up) reaches the ground at about √(2 × 9.81 × 1) ≈ 4.4 m/s.

Worked example · stopping from 4.4 m/s
How you landStopping distanceAverage deceleration
Stiff arm on concrete≈ 5 cm≈ 20 g
Knee pads, slide≈ 20 cm≈ 5 g
Roll out of it≈ 50 cm≈ 2 g

For a fall from 1 m, deceleration in g = 1 m ÷ stopping distance: 1 ÷ 0.05 = 20 g. Illustrative distances.

How to fall

  • Get low when you feel it going: less height, less speed.
  • Roll onto your shoulder or side instead of reaching out with a straight arm.
  • On a ramp, slide down on your knee pads.
  • Helmet always. World Skate's competition rules (used in the RSFI rulebook) require all park skaters, and street skaters under 18, to wear a helmet in practice and competition.
Halving your stopping distance does what to the average force?

It doubles it.

CH 6

Wheels, bearings & contests

Small wheels spin fast. And in contests, five judges decide.

A wheel turns once per circumference: revs per second = v ÷ (π d). Smaller wheels spin faster for the same speed, and the bearings take it.

Worked example · wheel spin at 15 km/h (4.17 m/s)
Wheel diameterTurns per kmSpin at 15 km/h
52 mm≈ 6,121≈ 1,530 rpm
54 mm≈ 5,895≈ 1,474 rpm
60 mm≈ 5,305≈ 1,326 rpm

54 mm: circumference π × 0.054 ≈ 0.170 m; 4.17 ÷ 0.170 ≈ 24.6 turns per second ≈ 1,474 rpm.

Hard or soft wheels?

Bigger, softer wheels deform over stones and cracks and meet them at a gentler angle, so they suit rough streets and cruising. Smaller, harder wheels are lighter and slide more predictably on smooth park surfaces.

Cracks and pebbles: the geometry of getting stopped

When a wheel of radius R meets a step or pebble of height h, the push from the edge acts along the line from the edge to the axle. The higher h is compared with R, the more of that push points backwards, against your motion. That's the moment the board stops and you don't.

Worked example · share of the edge's push pointing backwards (rigid wheel, geometry only)
Obstacle52 mm park wheel60 mm wheel70 mm cruiser wheel
3 mm crack lip≈ 47%≈ 44%≈ 41%
5 mm pebble≈ 59%≈ 55%≈ 52%
10 mm stone≈ 79%≈ 75%≈ 70%

The edge-to-axle line makes an angle θ with the vertical where cos θ = (R − h) ÷ R; the backward share is sin θ. 5 mm on a 52 mm wheel: cos θ = 21 ÷ 26 → θ ≈ 36°, sin θ ≈ 0.59. A softer wheel also squashes around the obstacle, which lowers the effective h further. These are geometry comparisons, not stopping forces.

Soft wheels: grip versus roll

Wheel hardness is measured on a durometer scale (the "A" number on the wheel); a lower number is softer. On smooth concrete, a harder wheel flexes less and wastes less energy rolling. On rough tarmac, a softer wheel absorbs bumps that would otherwise shake your body, the same "roughness resistance" idea explained on our cycling page. A softer wheel also grips more before it slides, which helps on carves and hurts when you want to slide on purpose.

Bearings and what "ABEC" really means

Almost every skateboard wheel holds two "608"-size bearings: an 8 mm bore, 22 mm outside diameter and 7 mm width, according to board maker Sector 9. The ABEC number on the box is a precision class from the Annular Bearing Engineers' Committee: it sets dimensions and tolerances, not speed. Bones Bearings, a bearing maker, points out that ABEC ignores side loads, impact resistance, lubrication and shields, which matter more for skating. Its estimate is that 90% of skating happens below about 2,000 rpm, consistent with the roughly 1,500 rpm in the table above. Clean, undamaged bearings matter more than the number.

How contests are scored

The RSFI skateboarding rulebook follows World Skate rules for the two Olympic disciplines, street and park, with no age limits. In park, skaters get at least two runs of 30–50 seconds; five judges score each run from 0 to 100, the highest and lowest scores are dropped and the remaining three averaged. The best run counts. In the Olympic street format, skaters do 2 runs of 45 seconds and 5 single tricks; the best run plus the 2 best tricks make the score.

Judges score 70, 74, 76, 80 and 91. What's the run score?

Drop 91 and 70: (74 + 76 + 80) ÷ 3 = 76.67.

04 · Governing bodies

Who sets the rules

Skateboarding is governed with the roller sports, from the world federation down to state units. We checked each on an official website. We could not verify a separate "Skateboarding Federation of India". A "Skateboarding India Federation" website promotes skateparks, but it isn't the body World Skate lists for India, so it isn't shown as a governing body.

Global

World Skate

The governing body for skateboarding and roller sports, recognised by the International Olympic Committee. Sets the competition rules for street and park.
