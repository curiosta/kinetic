CH 1

Gravity on a slope

The steeper the slope, the bigger the share of your weight that pulls you down it.

On a slope at angle θ, your weight mg splits in two: mg sin θ along the slope, pulling you downhill, and mg cos θ into the snow, pressing you onto it. Subtract snow friction (Ch 2) and you get the acceleration: a = g (sin θ − μ cos θ). Slopes are often quoted as a percentage: gradient = tan θ × 100.

Worked example · 75 kg skier, cold groomed snow (μ ≈ 0.05), no air drag
SlopeGradientPull down the slopeAccelerationSpeed after 50 m straight
5°9%64 N0.37 m/s²≈ 22 km/h
15°27%190 N2.07 m/s²≈ 52 km/h
25°47%311 N3.70 m/s²≈ 69 km/h
35°70%422 N5.22 m/s²≈ 82 km/h

15°: 75 × 9.81 × sin 15° ≈ 190 N; a = 9.81 × (0.259 − 0.05 × 0.966) ≈ 2.07 m/s²; v = √(2 × 2.07 × 50) ≈ 14.4 m/s ≈ 52 km/h. Without drag, mass cancels: heavy and light skiers accelerate alike.

Why beginners start on gentle slopes

On a 5° nursery slope, speed builds slowly enough to practise the snow plough. Below about 3° on groomed snow, gravity barely beats friction and you'll hardly move at all: try it in the Slope Lab.

What's the gradient of a 20° slope, and the pull on a 60 kg skier?

tan 20° ≈ 0.36, so 36%; pull = 60 × 9.81 × sin 20° ≈ 201 N.

CH 2

Friction & the snow

You're not sliding on ice. You're sliding on a film of water you just made.

The friction between a waxed base and snow is low, typically quoted around μ ≈ 0.03–0.1. A big reason is frictional melting: rubbing heats the contact points and makes a microscopic water layer that lubricates the base. Very cold, dry snow melts less and feels slow; wet spring snow can feel sticky because water films create suction.

Worked example · friction on a 15° slope, 75 kg skier (illustrative μ)
SnowμFriction forceAcceleration
Hard and icy0.03≈ 21 N2.25 m/s²
Cold groomed0.05≈ 36 N2.07 m/s²
Wet spring0.10≈ 71 N1.59 m/s²

Friction = μ × mg cos θ = 0.05 × 75 × 9.81 × 0.966 ≈ 36 N. These μ values are typical classroom figures; real values vary with temperature, snow crystals, wax and speed.

Snow plough to parallel

Beginners brake with the snow plough: tails pushed apart, skis tipped onto their inside edges so they scrape snow sideways. IISM Gulmarg's 14-day basic course syllabus builds from walking and sliding to snow plough, snow plough turns, traversing, uphill swings and side stepping, then basic swings; the intermediate and advanced courses move on to side-slip garlands, downhill swings and parallel turns with open and then closed skis, and slalom.

✋
Feel the numberA friction coefficient of 0.05 means a 75 kg skier on the flat needs a push of only about 37 N, about the weight of a 4 kg bag, to keep sliding.
On wet snow (μ = 0.1), what's the gentlest slope on which you'll start to slide?

You slide when tan θ > μ: θ > arctan 0.1 ≈ 5.7°.

CH 3

Air drag & top speed

Gravity keeps pulling. Eventually the air pushes back just as hard.

Air drag is ½ ρ Cd A v²: air density × drag area × speed squared. As you speed up, drag grows until gravity along the slope equals friction plus drag. That's your top (terminal) speed: vmax = √(2 (mg sin θ − μ mg cos θ) ÷ (ρ CdA)). Unlike acceleration, top speed does depend on mass: heavier skiers go faster when drag matters.

Worked example · 75 kg skier, μ = 0.05, air density 0.91 kg/m³ (≈ 3,000 m)
SlopeUpright (CdA ≈ 0.6 m²)Racing tuck (CdA ≈ 0.25 m²)
10°≈ 66 km/h≈ 102 km/h
15°≈ 86 km/h≈ 133 km/h
25°≈ 115 km/h≈ 178 km/h

15°, upright: (190.4 − 35.5) N × 2 ÷ (0.909 × 0.6) ≈ 568 → √568 ≈ 23.8 m/s ≈ 86 km/h. Drag areas are typical illustrative values. Air density from the International Standard Atmosphere.

Altitude and drag

Gulmarg's gondola climbs from about 2,650 m to 3,980 m, according to IISM. At 3,980 m the standard atmosphere gives about 61% of sea-level pressure, so the air is thinner and drag a little lower. Racers in a tuck exploit drag; recreational skiers control speed with turns instead (Ch 4).

If you halve your drag area, by what factor does your top speed rise?

vmax ∝ 1 ÷ √(CdA), so by √2 ≈ 1.41.

CH 4

Edges, turns & speed control

Turns aren't just for changing direction. They're your brakes.

To follow a curve of radius r at speed v, something must push you sideways with force m v² ÷ r. On skis or a snowboard, that's the snow pushing back on your tilted edge. To balance, you lean into the turn at angle φ with tan φ = v² ÷ (g r).

Worked example · lean angle for a turn
SpeedTurn radiusLean angleSideways force on a 75 kg skier
10 m/s (36 km/h)15 m≈ 34°≈ 500 N
10 m/s10 m≈ 46°≈ 750 N
15 m/s (54 km/h)20 m≈ 49°≈ 844 N

tan φ = 10² ÷ (9.81 × 15) ≈ 0.68 → φ ≈ 34°; force = 75 × 100 ÷ 15 = 500 N.

How turns control speed

  • Across the fall line: the steepest line down the hill is the fall line. Ski across it and the slope along your path is far gentler.
  • Skidding vs carving: a skidded turn scrapes snow and converts kinetic energy into heat; a carved turn rides the edge cleanly and keeps speed. Beginners skid; experts choose.
  • Sidecut: a ski is narrower at the waist than at the tip and tail. Tilt it on edge and it bends into an arc, which is what makes carving possible.
  • Snowboarding: the same physics on one board, with heel-side and toe-side edges. ABVIMAS (Solang) and IISM (Gulmarg) both run snowboarding courses.
You turn at 8 m/s on a 12 m radius. What lean angle do you need?

tan φ = 64 ÷ (9.81 × 12) ≈ 0.54 → φ ≈ 29°.

CH 5

Falls, collisions & avalanches

Speed is energy, and every stop has to put it somewhere.

Kinetic energy is ½ m v²: double your speed and you carry four times the energy into any fall or collision. A helmet, a controlled speed and space between you and others are the main defences.

Worked example · energy of a 75 kg skier
SpeedKinetic energySame as falling from
5 m/s (18 km/h)≈ 0.9 kJ≈ 1.3 m
10 m/s (36 km/h)≈ 3.8 kJ≈ 5.1 m
15 m/s (54 km/h)≈ 8.4 kJ≈ 11.5 m

½ × 75 × 15² ≈ 8,440 J; equivalent height = v² ÷ (2g) = 225 ÷ 19.62 ≈ 11.5 m.

Slope etiquette is physics too

  • The skier below has priority: they can't see you. Give them room.
  • Stop where you're visible from above, and look uphill before setting off.
  • Ski in control, at a speed where you could stop or avoid someone.

Avalanches

Snow builds up in layers. When a weak layer fails under a stiffer slab, the slab can release and slide. Most slab avalanches start on slopes of roughly 30°–45°, often triggered by the victim or their group. Gulmarg's high terrain above the gondola is serious avalanche country.

Off-piste needs training, kit and a guide

Never leave marked, controlled slopes without avalanche training, a transceiver, probe and shovel (and the skills to use them), a current avalanche forecast and a qualified local guide. This page doesn't teach avalanche safety.

How much more energy does a skier carry at 54 km/h than at 18 km/h?

Speed is 3 times higher, so energy is 3² = 9 times higher.

CH 6

Cold, altitude & sun

India's ski slopes are high. The air is thin, the sun is fierce and the cold is real.

Indian ski areas sit high: Auli at around 2,800 m (Uttarakhand Tourism), and Gulmarg from about 2,650 m to 3,980 m at the gondola top (IISM). The International Standard Atmosphere gives the drop in air pressure, and with it the oxygen in every breath.

Worked example · air pressure and oxygen vs sea level (standard atmosphere)
PlaceAltitudePressure vs sea levelOxygen per breath
Gulmarg resort≈ 2,650 m≈ 72%≈ 28% less
Auli≈ 2,800 m≈ 71%≈ 29% less
Gulmarg gondola top≈ 3,980 m≈ 61%≈ 39% less

ISA pressure ratio = (1 − 2.256 × 10⁻⁵ × h)^5.256. At 3,980 m: ≈ 0.61. Oxygen stays 20.95% of the air, so its partial pressure falls in proportion.

Staying well

  • Altitude: take it easy on day one, drink water, and descend if headache, nausea or breathlessness get worse.
  • Cold: IISM notes Gulmarg can drop to −8 °C in winter. Layers, gloves, a hat under the helmet; watch fingers, toes and faces for frostbite.
  • Sun: fresh snow reflects most sunlight and thin air filters less UV. Goggles or dark glasses prevent snow blindness; sunscreen on face and lips.
✋
Feel the numberAt Gulmarg's top station, every breath holds about the oxygen of 0.6 of a sea-level breath. That's why climbing back up in ski boots leaves you gasping.
Roughly what share of sea-level pressure is there at 3,000 m?

(1 − 2.256 × 10⁻⁵ × 3000)^5.256 ≈ 0.69: about 69%.

04 · Governing bodies

Who sets the rules

Skiing in India is shaped by the world federation, a national federation that changed in 2026, and the government institutes that run nearly all the training. We checked each on an official website.

Global

World federation

International Ski and Snowboard Federation (FIS)

The global authority for competitive skiing and snowboarding: Alpine, Cross-Country, Ski Jumping, Freestyle and Snowboard. Its directory lists each country's national association.

fis-ski.com

India

National federation

Bhartiya Ski and Snowboard Association (BSSA)

Describes itself as India's national governing body for skiing and snowboarding, recognised by the Indian Olympic Association and affiliated to FIS. PTI reported in June 2026 that FIS's Congress granted BSSA full membership and disaffiliated Ski and Snowboard India; FIS's online directory still listed the older body when we checked.

bssa.in
Government institute

Indian Institute of Skiing & Mountaineering (IISM), Gulmarg

A permanent office of the Ministry of Tourism, Government of India, founded in 1968 as the Gulmarg Winter Sports Project. Runs basic, intermediate and advanced skiing courses (December to March), snowboarding and an MOI course.

iismgulmarg.in

Atal Bihari Vajpayee Institute of Mountaineering & Allied Sports (ABVIMAS), Manali

Runs elementary, basic, intermediate, advanced, Nordic and snowboarding courses at Solang and Narkanda, plus a 21-day Method of Instruction (Skiing) course.
