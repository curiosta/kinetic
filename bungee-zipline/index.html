Fall, stretch, bounce, glide. Gravity does the work; the cord and the cable do the catching.

CH 1

Free fall: the first few seconds

Until the cord goes tight, a bungee jump is pure free fall.

When you leave the platform, the cord is slack. For the first L metres (the cord's unstretched length) the only big force on you is gravity, so you speed up at about g = 9.81 m/s². With no air drag, the time to fall that far is t = √(2L / g) and your speed when the cord starts to pull is v = √(2 g L). Air drag trims a little off those speeds; our Skydiving page shows what happens when you fall long enough for drag to win.

Worked example · free fall for different cord lengths (no drag, from our Bungee & Zip Lab)
Cord length LFree-fall timeSpeed when it goes tight
10 m1.43 s14.0 m/s (50 km/h)
20 m2.02 s19.8 m/s (71 km/h)
30 m2.47 s24.3 m/s (87 km/h)
40 m2.86 s28.0 m/s (101 km/h)

For 20 m: √(2 × 20 ÷ 9.81) ≈ 2.02 s and √(2 × 9.81 × 20) ≈ 19.8 m/s; × 3.6 ≈ 71 km/h. Doubling the cord length doesn't double the speed: it multiplies it by √2 ≈ 1.41.

How fast are you falling, with no drag, when a 15 m cord goes tight?

√(2 × 9.81 × 15) ≈ 17.2 m/s, about 62 km/h.

CH 2

Hooke's law & elastic energy

The cord catches you by storing your falling energy, then gives most of it back.

An ideal elastic cord obeys Hooke's law: stretched by x, it pulls back with F = k x, where k (in N/m) is its stiffness. The energy it stores is E = ½ k x², so twice the stretch stores four times the energy. At the lowest point you've stopped, so all the height you lost, L + x, has turned into stretch energy: m g (L + x) = ½ k x². Solving that quadratic gives the maximum stretch: x = [m g + √((m g)² + 2 k m g L)] ÷ k.

Worked example · one cord (20 m, k = 100 N/m), different jumpers, a 60 m platform (illustrative, from our Lab)
JumperMaximum stretchLowest point below the platformRoom left
40 kg17.1 m37.1 m22.9 m
70 kg24.8 m44.8 m15.2 m
90 kg29.6 m49.6 m10.4 m
110 kg34.2 m54.2 m5.8 m

For 70 kg: m g = 686.7 N; x = [686.7 + √(686.7² + 2 × 100 × 686.7 × 20)] ÷ 100 = (686.7 + 1,794) ÷ 100 ≈ 24.8 m. Check: 686.7 × 44.8 ≈ 30,770 J and ½ × 100 × 24.8² ≈ 30,780 J.

Real bungee cords are bundles of rubber strands, and they are not ideal springs. Their stiffness changes as they stretch, so the force doesn't rise in a straight line, and rubber turns some of the energy into heat on every stretch (it unloads along a different curve than it loads). India's adventure guidelines ask operators to buy rubber from recognised sources, record its shelf life, store it cool, dark and away from UV light, and inspect it regularly, with cords made and certified by the jump masters.

A cord stretched 10 m stores 5,000 J. How much does it store stretched 20 m?

E grows with x², so twice the stretch stores four times the energy: 20,000 J.

CH 3

Peak g, rebound & damping

The hardest pull comes at the very bottom, when you've stopped for an instant.

At the lowest point the cord pulls up with k x. What you feel is that pull compared with your weight, k x ÷ m g: "3.6 g" means the harness is pulling on you with 3.6 times your body weight. Your fastest moment isn't when the cord goes tight: you keep speeding up until the cord's pull equals your weight, at a stretch of m g ÷ k, and only then start to slow down.

Worked example · peak pull on a 20 m cord (illustrative, from our Lab)
Jumper & cordMaximum stretchPeak pullTop speed
40 kg, k = 100 N/m17.1 m4.3 × body weight75 km/h
70 kg, k = 100 N/m24.8 m3.6 × body weight77 km/h
110 kg, k = 100 N/m34.2 m3.2 × body weight80 km/h
70 kg, k = 200 N/m (stiffer)15.6 m4.6 × body weight74 km/h

For 70 kg: 100 × 24.8 ≈ 2,480 N ÷ 686.7 N ≈ 3.6. Top speed comes 686.7 ÷ 100 ≈ 6.9 m into the stretch: v² = 2 × 9.81 × 26.9 − (100 ÷ 70) × 6.9² ≈ 460, so v ≈ 21.4 m/s ≈ 77 km/h. A stiffer cord stops you in a shorter distance, so it must pull harder; a light jumper on a cord meant for a heavier one gets a harder stop.

Then you rebound. With no losses, energy conservation would fling you straight back up to the platform. Real jumps lose energy to air drag and to heat in the rubber (damping). If, say, 20% of the stored energy were lost on the first stretch (an illustrative figure), the 70 kg jumper above would rise to about 9 m below the platform instead of all the way, and each bounce would be smaller until they hang still where the pull equals their weight, about 20 + 6.9 ≈ 26.9 m down.

Who shouldn't jump

India's adventure guidelines list medical restrictions for bungee jumping that include heart problems, high blood pressure, neurological disorders, spondylitis and back problems, a recent fracture, surgery or hospital stay, epilepsy, osteoporosis and pregnancy. Jumpin Heights lists similar conditions. Tell the crew about anything relevant, and follow the jump master's decision.

A 70 kg jumper's cord stretches 30 m with k = 100 N/m. What's the peak pull in body weights?

100 × 30 = 3,000 N; 3,000 ÷ 686.7 ≈ 4.4 × body weight.

CH 4

Cord length vs jump height

The cord has to be long enough to thrill you and short enough to miss the ground.

The lowest point is L + x below the platform, and it must stay well above the ground or water. Chapter 2 showed that a heavier jumper on the same cord goes lower, which is why operators weigh every jumper and have weight limits: India's adventure guidelines give 40–110 kg and an age range of 12–45 years for bungee jumping, and Jumpin Heights and AJ Hackett publish their own limits for each site.

There's a neat result for cords made of the same rubber. A longer piece is softer in proportion to its length, k = E A ÷ L (E A is a property of the rubber and its cross-section). Put that into the energy balance and L cancels: the stretch is always the same fraction of the cord's length, and the peak pull is the same whatever the length. Longer cords give a longer fall and a lower bottom, not a harder stop.

Same rubber, a 15 m cord, 70 kg jumper: how far below the platform is the lowest point?

Stretch = 1.24 × 15 ≈ 18.6 m, so 15 + 18.6 ≈ 33.6 m.

CH 5

Zip lines: slope, sag, friction & drag

On a zip line, gravity pulls you down the slope and the air pushes back.

EN 15567-1, the European standard for ropes courses, defines a zip line as an activity in which the participant glides under gravity in a sloping direction. On a line sloping at θ, gravity pulls you along it with m g sin θ; the trolley's wheels resist with about μ m g cos θ, and the air with ½ ρ CdA v². You speed up until they balance, at a top speed of v = √(2 m g (sin θ − μ cos θ) ÷ (ρ CdA)). Gravity's pull grows with your mass but drag doesn't, so heavier riders go faster.

Worked example · top speed on a straight line (μ = 0.01, CdA = 0.5 m², illustrative, from our Lab)
Slope40 kg rider70 kg rider110 kg rider
3°27 km/h35 km/h44 km/h
6°40 km/h53 km/h66 km/h
10°53 km/h70 km/h87 km/h

70 kg at 6°: sin 6° − 0.01 × cos 6° ≈ 0.0946; 2 × 70 × 9.81 × 0.0946 ÷ (1.2 × 0.5) ≈ 216.5, so v ≈ 14.7 m/s ≈ 53 km/h. Real lines sag, so the slope is steep at the start and flattens (or rises) towards the end, and many are too short to reach top speed. Operators say Jumpin Heights' Flying Fox reaches up to 140 km/h and Jais Flight in the UAE up to 160 km/h.

A cable can never be perfectly straight. Under its own weight it hangs in a curve called a catenary, and a rider pulls a V into it. At mid-span, the two halves of the cable hold your weight W between them, so the tension is about T ≈ W L ÷ (4 s) for a span L and a sag s: the tighter the line, the bigger the tension. That's why anchors are built so strong; India's guidelines ask that rock anchors hold at least four times the load put on them.

Worked example · a 300 m span with a 100 kg rider at mid-span (ignoring the cable's own weight, illustrative)
SagSag as % of spanCable tension
18 m6%4.1 kN
9 m3%8.2 kN
4.5 m1.5%16.4 kN

981 N × 300 m ÷ (4 × 9 m) ≈ 8.2 kN, more than eight times the rider's weight. Halving the sag doubles the tension. 300 m is the length Flying Fox gives for its longest zip at Mehrangarh Fort.

Same line at 6°: a 110 kg rider's top speed is 66 km/h. Roughly why is it higher than a 70 kg rider's 53 km/h?

Top speed grows with √m: √(110 ÷ 70) ≈ 1.25, and 53 × 1.25 ≈ 66 km/h.

CH 6

Brakes, trolleys & harness load paths

Every gram of you hangs from one chain of parts. Each link has to hold.

At the end of the line, your kinetic energy ½ m v² has to go somewhere. Stopping from speed v over a distance d needs an average deceleration of a = v² ÷ (2 d), so a short, sharp stop means big forces. EN 15567-1 requires a passive braking system to always be in place on a zip line (one that works without anyone acting, such as gravity, a bungee, a net or impact absorbers), a primary brake to control deceleration in the arrival area, and an emergency brake where a failure of the primary brake could cause serious injury. Riders who must brake actively need training and equipment, and if the landing can't be seen from the start, departures must be controlled.

Worked example · stopping a rider arriving at 53 km/h (14.7 m/s)
Braking distanceAverage decelerationIn g
20 m5.4 m/s²0.55 g
10 m10.8 m/s²1.1 g
5 m21.7 m/s²2.2 g
3 m36.1 m/s²3.7 g

14.7² ÷ (2 × 5) ≈ 21.7 m/s² ÷ 9.81 ≈ 2.2 g. Spreading the stop over a longer distance is the whole job of a good brake.

The load path runs from your body through the harness, the lanyards and karabiners, the trolley's pulley wheels, the cable and finally the anchors or towers. India's guidelines for zip wires ask for a sit harness (with a chest or full-body harness where appropriate), two points of attachment such as lanyards with screwgate karabiners, equipment that meets UIAA or EN/CE standards, a guide's check of the fit before use, and a full inspection by an inspector at least every 12 months. At least one guide per course must be able to bring a stuck rider back to the ground from mid-span within 30 minutes. Our Rock Climbing page works through rope forces and anchors in more detail.

A rider arrives at 10 m/s and the brake stops them in 4 m. What's the average deceleration?

10² ÷ (2 × 4) = 12.5 m/s², about 1.3 g.

04 · Governing bodies

Who sets the rules

No body certifies bungee jumpers or zip-line riders. Instead, standards bodies write the rules operators build and run to, and in India the Ministry of Tourism and the ATOAI set minimum safety guidelines. We checked each on an official source.

Global

Zip-line standards

ACCT International

Founded in 1993 as the Association for Challenge Course Technology; an ANSI-accredited standards developer for challenge courses, aerial adventure parks, canopy tours and zip lines. Its current standard is ANSI/ACCT 03-2019.

Trainers, builders and inspectors of ropes courses; maintains ERCA standards for construction, inspection and operation, and certifies inspection and training bodies.
