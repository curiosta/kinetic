One breath. Pressure, oxygen and calm do the rest.

Freediving is physics you can feel: water pressure squeezing the air in your body, oxygen and carbon dioxide racing each other, and buoyancy that flips as you go deeper. Take the challenge, learn the science with a Depth & Breath Lab, then find courses in India and abroad. And never freedive alone.

CH 1

The dive reflex

Hold your breath with your face in water and your body quietly switches to saving oxygen.

All mammals, humans included, share a diving response. When you hold your breath, and more strongly with a cold, wet face, the vagus nerve slows the heart (bradycardia) and the sympathetic nerves narrow blood vessels in the limbs, skin and gut (peripheral vasoconstriction). Oxygen-rich blood is kept for the heart and brain. A 2021 physiology review reports heart rates as low as 20–30 beats a minute in deep dives, and describes the spleen contracting to push extra red blood cells into circulation.

CH 2

Oxygen, CO₂ & the urge to breathe

The urge to breathe is a CO₂ alarm, not an oxygen gauge.

During a breath-hold, oxygen falls and carbon dioxide rises. CO₂ is a powerful stimulus to breathe; low oxygen is a much weaker one. Divers Alert Network (DAN) defines hyperventilation as any breathing that drops your CO₂, such as fast or deep breathing or long exhalations. It doesn't add useful oxygen, but it delays the alarm, so you can run low on oxygen before you feel the need to breathe. DAN's advice: breathe normally until the final breath, and never hyperventilate.

Worked example · why a delayed alarm is dangerous (illustrative numbers)
StartCO₂ at startUrge to breathe (at 6.7 kPa, rising 0.4 kPa/min)Oxygen runs low (from Ch 1)
Normal breathing5.3 kPa≈ 3.5 min≈ 3.3 min
After hyperventilating3.5 kPa≈ 8 min≈ 3.3 min

(6.7 − 5.3) ÷ 0.4 = 3.5 min; (6.7 − 3.5) ÷ 0.4 = 8 min. The numbers are made up to show the idea: after hyperventilating, the alarm can come long after the oxygen has run low, with no warning in between.

If CO₂ starts at 4.5 kPa and rises 0.4 kPa a minute, when does it reach 6.7 kPa?

(6.7 − 4.5) ÷ 0.4 = 5.5 min, later than the oxygen budget in Ch 1.

CH 3

Hypoxia & blackout

The danger isn't at the bottom. It's in the last few metres, and at the surface.

What pushes oxygen into your blood is its partial pressure: the oxygen fraction × the total pressure. At depth, the squeezed lungs raise it; on the way up it falls with the pressure, while you keep using oxygen. Too little reaching the brain (hypoxia) can cause a sudden loss of consciousness: a blackout. A DAN article says 99 percent of blackouts happen at the surface or within 16 feet (about 5 m) of it, and that most problems happen in the last third of the ascent.

Worked example · 10% oxygen left in the lungs on a dive to 30 m (from our Depth & Breath Lab)
DepthTotal pressureLung oxygen partial pressure
30 m4 bar0.40 bar (40.5 kPa)
20 m3 bar0.30 bar (30.4 kPa)
10 m2 bar0.20 bar (20.3 kPa)
Surface1 bar0.10 bar (10.1 kPa)

Same gas, same oxygen: only the pressure changes. From 10 m to the surface the partial pressure halves, which is why the end of the ascent is the riskiest part.

Never freedive alone

DAN's two most repeated rules: never breath-hold dive alone, and don't hyperventilate. A trained buddy watches every dive, meets you on the way up (for deeper dives, DAN describes buddies meeting the diver at a third of their depth), stays within reach for your recovery breaths at the surface, and knows how to bring you back. One DAN author advises staying out of the water for at least 24 hours after a blackout or loss of motor control.

With 8% oxygen in the lungs at 20 m (3 bar), what's the partial pressure there and at the surface?

0.08 × 3 = 0.24 bar at 20 m; 0.08 × 1 = 0.08 bar at the surface.

CH 4

Boyle's law & lung squeeze

Every 10 m of sea adds another atmosphere. Your lungs feel every one of them.

Sea water adds about 1 bar of pressure for every 10 m, on top of 1 bar of air. Boyle's law says a gas's pressure × volume stays constant at a fixed temperature, so the air in your lungs, sinuses, ears and mask shrinks as you go down: V = V0 ÷ P. When the lungs reach the volume left after a full exhale (residual volume), simple physics would predict trouble. The 2021 review puts that depth around 40–50 m after a full breath, and describes a blood shift into the chest that limits the effect; going too deep too fast can still injure the lungs (lung squeeze).

Worked example · 6 L of air in the lungs at the surface (from our Depth & Breath Lab)
DepthPressureLung volumeShare of surface volume
0 m1 bar6.0 L100%
10 m2 bar3.0 L50%
20 m3 bar2.0 L33%
30 m4 bar1.5 L25%
40 m5 bar1.2 L20%

6 L × 1 bar = V × 4 bar → V = 1.5 L at 30 m. With an illustrative residual volume of 1.5 L, this diver's lungs reach it at 30 m; a bigger breath (8 L) moves that to about 43 m.

Your mask is an air space too: breathe a little air into it through your nose on the way down, or it squeezes your face. Unlike scuba divers, freedivers don't breathe compressed air at depth; our Scuba page covers what changes when you do.

A 7 L breath at 25 m (3.5 bar): what volume?

7 ÷ 3.5 = 2 L.

CH 5

Equalisation

The first 10 m are the hardest on your ears.

Your middle ears and sinuses are air spaces with stiff walls. As you descend, you have to push air into them to match the water pressure, or the eardrum bends painfully. In the Valsalva method you pinch your nose and push with your chest muscles; it gets harder head-down and as the lungs compress. In the Frenzel method you close the airway to the lungs and use the tongue and throat as a piston on the air in your mouth and nose, so a small amount of air does the job. AIDA teaches Frenzel in its AIDA3 course, and deeper techniques such as mouthfill (storing air in the mouth for the deep part of the dive) in AIDA4.

Worked example · how much an air space shrinks over each 10 m
Depth changePressure changeVolume change
0 → 10 m1 → 2 bar−50%
10 → 20 m2 → 3 bar−33%
20 → 30 m3 → 4 bar−25%
30 → 40 m4 → 5 bar−20%

Volume ratio = Pbefore ÷ Pafter: 2 ÷ 3 = 0.67, a 33% drop. Equalise early and often near the surface; never force it.

By how much does an air space shrink from 40 m to 50 m?

5 ÷ 6 ≈ 0.83, a 17% drop.

CH 6

Buoyancy, freefall & the disciplines

Swim hard for the first few metres, then let the sea pull you down.

Near the surface, air in your lungs and wetsuit makes you float. As both compress, you displace less water; at the neutral depth buoyancy equals weight, and below it you sink without kicking: freefall. The 2021 review says this usually happens within the first 20 m or so, depending on body, suit, weights, lung volume and water. A DAN article warns against overweighting and advises weighting so that a blacked-out diver would end up at the surface.

Worked example · 6 L lungs, 3 mm suit, 2 kg of lead (from our Depth & Breath Lab, illustrative)
DepthLungs + suit gasNet buoyancy
Surface9.0 L+5.0 kg (floats)
10 m4.5 L+0.4 kg
12 m4.1 L≈ 0 (neutral)
20 m3.0 L−1.1 kg (sinks)
30 m2.3 L−1.9 kg (sinks)

Net = 1.025 × gas volume ÷ P − 2 kg lead − 2.2 kg body. At 20 m: 1.025 × 9 ÷ 3 − 4.2 ≈ −1.1 kg.

The disciplines

AIDA's competition disciplines: STA (static apnea: a breath-hold at the surface, the only one timed); DYN, DYNB and DNF (dynamic: horizontal distance with a monofin or fins, with bifins, or with no fins); and depth: CWT (constant weight with fins or monofin), CWTB (with bifins), CNF (no fins) and FIM (free immersion: pulling down and up the rope). AIDA also lists variable weight (VWT) and no limits (NLT), and says it stopped sanctioning NLT attempts in 2015 because of the extreme risks.

Same diver, 4 kg of lead instead of 2: about where's the neutral depth?

P = 1.025 × 9 ÷ (4 + 2.2) ≈ 1.49 bar → about 5 m. More lead means sinking sooner, and a harder swim up.

04 · Governing bodies

Who sets the rules

Competitive freediving has two world bodies, AIDA and CMAS, with different rules and records; training comes from agencies such as AIDA, PADI, SSI and Molchanovs. In India, a new AIDA national body was accepted as an observer in 2026. We checked each on an official source.
