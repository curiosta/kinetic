CH 1

Apparent wind

The wind you feel isn't the wind that's blowing.

As soon as you move, you add your own "headwind" to the true wind. The wind you and your kite actually feel, the apparent wind, is the vector sum of the two: stronger, and swung towards the front, the faster you go. Kites go further: a kite swooping across the sky moves faster than you, so it feels even more wind (Ch 3).

Worked example · true wind 15 kn from the side (beam reach)
Your speedApparent windAngle from your nose
0 kn (standing)15.0 kn90°
10 kn≈ 18.0 kn≈ 56°
15 kn≈ 21.2 kn45°
20 kn25.0 kn≈ 37°

At right angles the two winds add like the sides of a right triangle: √(15² + 20²) = 25 kn. Angle = arctan(15 ÷ 20) ≈ 37°. 1 knot = 1.852 km/h.

Feel the numberRide across a 15 kn wind at 20 kn and you feel 25 kn on your face: two-thirds more wind than is actually blowing.
True wind 12 kn from the side, you ride at 16 kn. What apparent wind?

√(12² + 16²) = 20 kn.

CH 2

Kite lift & power

Double the wind and the kite pulls four times as hard.

A kite is a wing. Its pull is F = ½ ρ v² S CL: air density ρ, the apparent wind speed v at the kite, the kite area S and a lift coefficient CL that depends on its shape and angle. Because of the v², small changes in wind make big changes in pull. The energy the wind carries grows even faster, with v³: ½ ρ v³ watts through each square metre.

Worked example · 12 m² kite, CL 0.6 (from our Kite Lab, illustrative)
True windEdge of window (1.0×)Mid-window (1.3×)Power zone (1.6×)
12 kn≈ 168 N (17 kg)≈ 284 N (29 kg)≈ 430 N (44 kg)
15 kn≈ 263 N (27 kg)≈ 444 N (45 kg)≈ 672 N (69 kg)
20 kn≈ 467 N (48 kg)≈ 789 N (80 kg)≈ 1,195 N (122 kg)
25 kn≈ 729 N (74 kg)≈ 1,233 N (126 kg)≈ 1,867 N (190 kg)

15 kn = 7.72 m/s; × 1.3 = 10.0 m/s. F = 0.5 × 1.225 × 10.0² × 12 × 0.6 ≈ 444 N. The kg figures are the pull divided by 9.81.

Why kites come in sizes

Pull scales with area, so riders choose a smaller kite in stronger wind. In our model a 7 m² kite at 20 kn pulls about as hard as a 12 m² kite at about 15 kn mid-window. Bars also depower the kite: pushing the bar away lowers its angle of attack and its CL.

The wind rises from 12 kn to 24 kn. How much more does the kite pull?

(24 ÷ 12)² = 4 times.

CH 3

The wind window

Where the kite sits in the sky decides how hard it pulls.

Stand with your back to the wind. The wind window is the quarter-sphere of sky downwind of you where the kite can fly. At its edge (out to the sides and overhead, called the neutral zone) the kite sits almost still and feels only about the true wind, so it pulls gently. Deep in the middle, the power zone, the kite flies across the wind, moves fast and feels much more apparent wind (Ch 1), so it pulls hard.

Worked example · same wind, different spots (15 kn, 12 m², illustrative)
Kite positionWind at kitePullCompared with edge
Edge / overhead (neutral)≈ 15 kn≈ 263 N1×
Mid-window≈ 19.5 kn≈ 444 N≈ 1.7×
Power zone≈ 24 kn≈ 672 N≈ 2.6×

1.3² ≈ 1.69 and 1.6² = 2.56: pull follows the square of the wind the kite feels.

That's why the IKO's Level 1 course starts on the beach: flying the kite at the edge of the window, launching and landing it there, and learning the safety systems, including the quick release, before you ever stand on a board.

Your kite is pulling too hard. Which way do you steer it?

Towards the edge of the window (overhead or out to the side), and push the bar out to depower.

CH 4

Edging, leaning & planing

You're a lever between a kite and a fin-shaped edge.

The kite pulls you sideways and up through the harness. You resist by leaning back and digging the board's edge into the water, which acts like a keel. To balance, the angle you lean from vertical follows tan θ ≈ pull ÷ weight (ignoring the kite's lift upward). Once the board moves fast enough, water pressure under it lifts it up to plane on the surface, and drag falls.

Worked example · a 75 kg rider (736 N of weight, illustrative)
Horizontal pullPull ÷ weightLean from vertical
284 N39%≈ 21°
444 N60%≈ 31°
672 N91%≈ 42°

θ = arctan(444 ÷ 736) ≈ 31°. A lighter rider on the same kite has to lean further or depower more.

Planing

The lift the water gives the board also grows with speed squared. If a board just planes at 8 kn with you on it (illustrative), at 6 kn it gets only (6 ÷ 8)² ≈ 56% of that lift and sinks back into the water. A waterstart (an IKO Level 2 skill) uses a dive of the kite through the power zone to get you up to planing speed fast.

A 60 kg rider (589 N) feels 444 N of horizontal pull. About what lean angle?

arctan(444 ÷ 589) ≈ 37°.

CH 5

Riding upwind

Every beginner drifts downwind. Independent riders don't.

You can't ride straight into the wind, but you can ride at an angle to it, zig-zagging upwind. What counts is your velocity made good (VMG): the part of your speed that takes you upwind, VMG = speed × cos(angle off the wind). Edging harder and pointing higher swaps a little speed for a lot of upwind progress.

Worked example · riding at 15 kn (illustrative angles)
Angle off the windUpwind progress (VMG)What happens
50°≈ 9.6 knClimbing upwind
70°≈ 5.1 knSlowly gaining
90°0 knHolding ground
110°≈ −5.1 knDrifting downwind

15 × cos 50° ≈ 9.6 kn. Keep the kite lower and further forward in the window, and the edge steady.

Riding upwind and controlling speed by edging are both IKO Level 3 (Independent) skills. That level is what the IKO says lets you ride on your own and rent equipment, because you can get back to where you started.

At 12 kn and 60° off the wind, what's your VMG?

12 × cos 60° = 6 kn upwind.

CH 6

Wind, weather & safety

A gust is a force multiplier. Respect the v².

Because pull grows with the square of the wind, gusts hit hard. A gust from 15 to 21 kn (40% stronger) nearly doubles the pull: (21 ÷ 15)² ≈ 1.96. In the power zone, on a kite sized for the lull, that can be enough to drag or lift a rider (often called being "lofted").

Worked example · gusts on a 12 m² kite, mid-window (illustrative)
Steady wind → gustPull multiplierPull
15 → 18 kn1.44×≈ 444 → 639 N
15 → 21 kn1.96×≈ 444 → 870 N
15 → 25 kn2.78×≈ 444 → 1,233 N
  • Wind direction: offshore wind (blowing from land to sea) carries you away from the beach; onshore wind pushes you towards obstacles. Learn in steady, cross-shore wind with clear space downwind.
  • Safety systems: know your quick release and leash, and practise self-landing and self-rescue (IKO Levels 1–3).
  • Spot conditions: the IKO's Level 1 starts with an assessment of the spot, the environment and the activity's risks. Shallow, flat water helps beginners.
  • Right of way: IKO Level 2 introduces the rules for passing other riders.

Learn with a school

Take lessons from an IKO-certified (or equivalent) instructor with a rescue boat on the water, be able to swim, and never fly a kite near power lines, roads or crowds.

The wind rises from 14 to 21 kn. About how much harder does your kite pull?

(21 ÷ 14)² = 1.5² = 2.25 times.

04 · Governing bodies

Who sets the rules

Kitesurfing's training levels come from the IKO; Olympic racing comes under World Sailing; in India, competition runs through the Yachting Association of India. We checked each on an official website.
